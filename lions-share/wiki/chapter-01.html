The 120 Carat Sapphire

Lesson: A Plan That Changed the World

Chapter 1 · book pages 18-25

Summary

Chapter 1 opens with a personal anecdote: the author's three-and-a-half-year-old son spreads out a street map of Los Angeles on his bedroom floor and announces he is making a "treasure map." The author marvels that his son has independently grasped the first essential step toward acquiring anything desired: having a roadmap. This sets the chapter's controlling metaphor — that the book itself is a map offering "a place to start, directions to follow, and, ultimately, a destination at which to arrive."

The narrative then rewinds to the summer of 1975 to tell the founding story of Apple, framed as a study in planning. The author recounts how two young Californians named Steve — Steve Jobs (tall, brash, artistic) and Steve Wozniak ("Woz," quiet, practical, an engineering mind) — were introduced by their mutual friend Bill Fernandez in the San Francisco Bay Area. Their first venture was Wozniak's "blue box" device for free long-distance calls, of which Jobs sold 200. In 1975, computer hobbyists first gathered in a garage in Menlo Park, California, forming The Homebrew Computer Club. Inspired by it, Wozniak built a ready-made circuit-board computer that his employer Hewlett Packard rejected five times. Jobs convinced him to go into business; after returning from a fruit orchard in Oregon, Jobs named the company "Apple." A Homebrew member with a retail chain ordered 50 computers, then 150 more, launching the Apple I.

The chapter's central figure is the company's first CEO, Mike Scott ("Scotty"), who the author argues was the under-credited architect of Apple's success. First investor Mike Markkula stipulated that Apple needed a leader who could create and execute a business blueprint, and recommended Scott, who managed hundreds of people at National Semiconductor (then the world's largest semiconductor maker). Scott took a pay cut to become Apple's first CEO, making "two Mikes and two Steves" the four principal owners. Scott emphasized positive company culture and scaling; for four years Apple doubled in size every three months. The chapter includes the famous badge anecdote: Woz got employee #1, Jobs threw a fit and got #0, and Scotty took badge #007. Despite friction with Jobs (haggling with suppliers, the "Apple beige" color dispute, the six-week debate over the rounded case edge), Scott kept Apple on course. He captained Apple from 1977–1981, overseeing the Apple II and III and the first 5½" floppy disk (as printed in the book; the real-world format was 5¼"); the Apple II sold over six million units. Apple went public in 1980 (first-day value over $1.7 billion, creating 40 millionaires, then 260 more) and is described as valued today around $2.5 trillion.

After leaving Apple, Scott designed and built rocket ships and worked on a Star Trek-like tricorder, but his most beloved project was assembling one of the world's most valuable rare gem collections — an estimated 2,600 different mineral specimens, including a cobalt blue mineral named "Scottybite" after him. His collection was displayed publicly at the Bowers Museum near Los Angeles. The featured treasure item is a 120-carat raw sapphire crystal from Ratnapura, Sabaragamuwa Province, Sri Lanka, once owned by Mike Scott. The chapter notes most sapphires are found in streams with edges weathered smooth by water, so a sapphire in its original six-sided crystalline form is rare; fewer than a dozen 120-carat raw crystals are thought to exist. Images show the stone in normal light (blue) and under ultraviolet light (red).

The chapter closes with the author reflecting on parenting — his wife Kimberly and their shared values of kindness, generosity, and integrity — and crystallizing the lesson: the first step in treasure hunting is to formulate a map, and the second is readying oneself to put the plan into action. He instructs readers to treat the book's pages as their map and, when they encounter "stories or ideas you feel you can divine from," to "write them down, draw them out, and organize them." It then transitions to Chapter 2 and Tuyet Nguyet, who spent over fifty years cultivating knowledge of rare art and whose collection contributed over a dozen objects to the treasure.

The Treasure(s)

The featured item is a 120-carat raw sapphire crystal in its original six-sided crystalline form, sourced from Ratnapura, Sabaragamuwa Province, Sri Lanka, and formerly owned by Mike Scott, Apple's first CEO. The chapter stresses that most sapphires are recovered from streams, where water and time weather their edges smooth — so an intact raw crystal is rare, with fewer than a dozen 120-carat raw crystals thought to exist. The book shows the stone twice: blue in normal light, and red under ultraviolet light.

Provenance note from research: the sapphire is described as coming from Scott's gem collection, which serves as the chapter's main clue-by-association thread. The Arkenstone (Robert Lavinsky's dealership) has confirmed it supplied "some of the fine minerals included in the treasure stash" for the book — including an emerald formerly owned by Andrew Carnegie — and the same dealership handled Scott's estate minerals after his 2025 death — though there is no public confirmation that a specimen in the boxes came specifically from Scott's personal collection. Caution: published sources describe Scott's headline sapphires as a ~400-carat golden Sri Lankan sapphire and a 64-carat blue Sri Lankan sapphire, not specifically a "120-carat" stone, so the book's figure does not cleanly match documented sources.

The subtitle "A Plan That Changed the World" frames Apple's founding as a story about the power of a deliberate roadmap. The author uses both his toddler son (who instinctively makes a "treasure map") and Mike Scott (who provided the missing business blueprint that vision-rich but directionless Jobs and Wozniak needed) to teach the treasure-hunter's first two steps: (1) formulate a map, and (2) ready yourself to put the plan into action. He explicitly tells the reader to treat the book's pages as their map — "a place to start, directions to follow, and, ultimately, a destination at which to arrive" — and to write down, draw out, and organize the stories and ideas they "divine from."

This is meta-guidance on the hunt's methodology rather than a coded location clue. The chapter sets up how to read the rest of the book: as a map to be annotated, not a cipher to be cracked.

Research Findings

Mike Scott (Apple's first CEO)

Michael "Scotty" Scott (Feb 11, 1943 – April 12, 2025; some sources list 1945) was Apple Computer's first CEO (Feb 1977–March 1981) and Apple employee #7. Before Apple he was director of manufacturing at National Semiconductor; Mike Markkula recruited him as CEO because Jobs and Wozniak were considered too inexperienced. He is famous internally for instituting Apple's badge numbers and for the 1980 "Black Wednesday" layoffs. Post-Apple, Scott became a serious collector and recognized expert on colored gemstones and minerals. By 2008 his collection encompassed 2,600+ mineral specimens and 200+ exceptional gemstones, including a ~400-carat golden sapphire from Sri Lanka and a 1,730-carat ruby crystal from Myanmar. He co-authored/commissioned the book "Light & Stone: Highlights From The Scott Gem Collection" (2002, via the Bowers Museum) and funded the RRUFF Project at the University of Arizona. Two minerals were named in his honor — scottyite (IMA 2012-027) and rruffite (IMA 2009-077); scottyite's type locality is the Wessels mine in South Africa, not a US location. His other post-Apple venture was Starstruck, Inc. (1983–1988, Redwood City, CA), an early commercial hybrid-rocket company. He also supported the Seattle Opera and Caltech (Pasadena).

Bowers Museum (Santa Ana, CA) and Michael M. Scott's gem collection

The Bowers Museum (historically the Charles W. Bowers Memorial Museum) is a cultural-art and history museum in Santa Ana, Orange County, California, roughly four miles south of Disneyland and about 35 miles southeast of downtown Los Angeles. It opened in 1936 and became an independent nonprofit in 1986. Its core focus is cultural and decorative arts, not gems — the gem connection is a series of borrowed/touring exhibitions. The museum displayed Scott's collection in "Gems! The Art and Nature of Precious Stones" (through June 2, 2002) and later "Gems: Colors of Light and Stone," featuring 300+ rare gems including the world's largest faceted gemstone (220 lb / 500,000 carats), a 400-carat golden sapphire, a 64-carat royal blue Sri Lankan sapphire on a gold cobra stand, a 16-carat padparadscha sapphire, a benitoite necklace, and a 242-carat tanzanite tiara with 1,000 diamonds. Published sources describe a 400-carat golden sapphire and a 64-carat blue sapphire, not specifically a "120-carat" stone. The collection never had a permanent home at the Bowers; it toured (also to the Royal Ontario Museum, Toronto, 2008–09). After Scott's death (April 12, 2025), portions were dispersed via the 2025 Denver Mineral Show auction (The Arkenstone / mineralauctions.com).

Mike Scott's rare gem / mineral collection (2,600 specimens)

Beginning in 1987, Scott assembled over 2,600 mineral specimens plus 200+ exceptional gemstones, including a ~400-carat golden sapphire from Sri Lanka, a 1,730-carat ruby crystal from Myanmar, the world's largest tanzanite (242 ct) in a 1,000-diamond tiara, and the world's largest star rose quartz. The collection's primary public display venue was the Bowers Museum in Santa Ana ("Gems: Colors of Light and Stone"). His mineral specimens were largely kept at home and rarely exhibited; after his 2025 death they were sold by The Arkenstone (Robert Lavinsky's dealership, Richardson, TX) via Mineral Auctions at the 2025 Denver Show / Hard Rock Summit. Directly relevant to the hunt: The Arkenstone confirms it supplied "some of the fine minerals included in the treasure stash" for "There's Treasure Inside," including an emerald formerly owned by Andrew Carnegie, an octahedral diamond crystal, and 100+ carat tanzanite specimens — making The Arkenstone the common thread linking Scott's collection and the book's treasure items, though there is no explicit confirmation that a box specimen came from Scott's personal collection. Specific US mineral localities represented in the auctioned collection include Arizona (Grand Reef Mine/Klondyke, Potter-Cramer Mine), Utah (Lucin; Little Green Monster Variscite Mine, Fairfield), New Jersey (Sterling Hill; Basking Ridge), Illinois (Denton Mine), Oklahoma (Tri-State Mine, Cardin), and California (Elizabeth R. Mine, Pala).

Scottyite (cobalt-blue barium copper silicate, BaCu₂Si₂O₇)

Scottyite is a rare deep-blue (cobalt-blue) barium copper silicate, the natural analog of a previously synthetic compound. It was approved by the IMA in 2012 (IMA 2012-027) and described in 2013 in American Mineralogist by Hexiong Yang, Robert T. Downs, Stanley H. Evans and William W. Pinch. Its type locality is the Wessels mine, Kalahari Manganese Fields, Northern Cape Province, South Africa — not in the US; the only other confirmed occurrence is a xenolith at the Caspar quarry in Ettringen, Germany. (A claim that scottyite is "found in Utah" appears to be unsupported and is treated as a hallucination.) The mineral is named after Michael "Scotty" Scott, who privately funded the University of Arizona's RRUFF Project. The book's chapter calls the mineral "Scottybite"; the documented mineral name is scottyite.

Homebrew Computer Club (Menlo Park, CA garage, 1975)

The Homebrew Computer Club was an early personal-computer hobbyist group whose first meeting was held March 5, 1975, in Gordon French's garage in Menlo Park, San Mateo County, California, prompted by the arrival of the first MITS Altair 8800 kit. Co-founders Gordon French and Fred Moore hosted about 30 hobbyists. Several secondary sources give the garage address as 614 18th Avenue, Menlo Park (treat as moderately confirmed). The club is famous as the launchpad for Wozniak and Jobs; Wozniak first demonstrated the Apple I prototype at a meeting. Venues moved as the group grew: the Peninsula School (Menlo Park), the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center (SLAC, now Panofsky Auditorium) until 1978, then Stanford Medical School. The Computer History Museum in Mountain View, CA preserves club history. All associations are in the SF Peninsula / Silicon Valley.

Ratnapura, Sabaragamuwa Province, Sri Lanka

Ratnapura (Sinhala for "City of Gems") is the capital of Sabaragamuwa Province in south-central Sri Lanka, on the Kalu Ganga ("Black River") about 101 km southeast of Colombo. It is the historic heart of Sri Lanka's gem trade. Gems form in ancient high-grade metamorphic rock but are recovered mainly from secondary alluvial deposits — gem-bearing river gravels called "illam" buried in old flood plains and streambeds. Miners sink pits to the illam layer and wash the gravel in nearby stream/pond water, swirling away light mud and sand to leave heavier gem minerals. This matches the chapter's stream/water-weathering note: Ceylon sapphires are water-worn, river-transported stones. Ratnapura is the source of famous sapphires including the Logan Sapphire (~423 ct) and Star of Asia (~330 ct) — both now in the Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History in Washington, D.C.

Mary Oliver (poet) — author of "The Summer Day" (epigraph source)

Mary Oliver (1935–2019) was a Pulitzer Prize (1984) and National Book Award (1992) winning American nature poet. The epigraph line "one wild and precious life" comes from "The Summer Day," first published in her 1990 collection "House of Light." The poem names no specific place; its imagery reflects New England landscapes. Her US geographic anchors: born and raised in Maple Heights, Ohio (Cleveland suburb); worked as a teenager at Steepletop, the Edna St. Vincent Millay estate near Austerlitz, New York; lived 40+ years in Provincetown, Massachusetts (Cape Cod), where her walks to "Blackwater Pond" (generally identified with Clapps Pond / Beech Forest in the Province Lands of Cape Cod National Seashore) inspired several poems; later relocated to Hobe Sound, Florida, where she died; and taught at Bennington College, Vermont.

Eleanor Roosevelt

Eleanor Roosevelt (1884–1962) was the longest-serving US First Lady, a UN delegate, and chair of the commission that drafted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. The epigraph "It takes as much energy to wish as it does to plan" is widely attributed to her, but quote-research has not located a primary source in her own writings (earliest located print appearance is a 1995 diet book), so the attribution is popular but unverified. Her life is densely tied to Hyde Park, NY (Hudson River Valley): the Home of Franklin D. Roosevelt National Historic Site (Springwood, where she and FDR are buried in the Rose Garden) and the Eleanor Roosevelt National Historic Site at Val-Kill — the first National Historic Site dedicated to a First Lady. "Val-Kill" derives from Dutch for "waterfall-stream." Other associations: Roosevelt Campobello International Park (Campobello Island, New Brunswick, Canada, linked to Lubec, Maine), and the Eleanor Roosevelt statue at the FDR Memorial in West Potomac Park, Washington, D.C.

National Semiconductor

National Semiconductor was founded May 27, 1959 in Danbury, Connecticut, relocated its HQ to Santa Clara, California in 1968, and in 1970 moved to a 29-acre headquarters park in Sunnyvale, California, becoming a Silicon Valley anchor. It was the first semiconductor firm to reach $1 billion in annual sales (1981) and was acquired by Texas Instruments for $6.5 billion in 2011. US fabs included South Portland, ME; Arlington, TX; West Jordan, UT; and Tucson, AZ. The chapter's relevance runs through Mike Scott, who was National Semiconductor's director of manufacturing before becoming Apple's first CEO. The strongest hunt thread is not the corporation but Scott's gemstone/mineral legacy (Bowers Museum, Santa Ana; RRUFF Project, University of Arizona, Tucson), which mirrors the chapter's sapphire theme.
