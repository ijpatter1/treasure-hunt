Amelia's Autograph

Lesson: Explore More

Chapter 7 · book pages 70-75

Summary

Chapter 7 centers on aviator Amelia Earhart and the treasure item: a signed black-and-white photograph of Earhart standing beside her Lockheed Vega 5B airplane.

The chapter opens with two epigraphs — Frank Borman (“Exploration is really the essence of the human spirit.”) and Erno Rubik (“If you are curious, you'll find the puzzles around you. If you are determined, you will solve them.”) — then frames the lesson “Explore More” through the author's admiration for his two young children's boundless curiosity.

The author casts Earhart as “the consummate treasure hunter,” recounting her lifelong thirst for adventure: riding elephants at a world's fair at age 7, building a working homemade roller coaster at age 8, serving as a full-time volunteer WWI nurse in Canada at age 20 (after meeting an injured soldier while visiting her sister), and taking flying lessons at age 23 after her first airshow with her father on December 28, 1920. She drove a truck to save money for her first plane at a time when 95% of air travelers were men.

The treasure photograph is described in specific provenance terms: it was taken just after Earhart deplaned in Londonderry, Ireland, following her historic solo Atlantic crossing, and shows her on the Lockheed Vega 5B she flew. The author states the only other copies of the photo he knows of are at the Purdue and Harvard Libraries, and that those two are NOT signed — making the treasure copy uniquely valuable. The chapter catalogs her records: first woman to fly solo across the Atlantic, first woman to fly solo non-stop across the U.S., first to fly solo from Hawaii to the U.S. mainland, fastest person to fly Mexico City to Washington, DC, and first to fly Mexico City to New Jersey. She was president of the “Ninety-Nines” and an airplane mechanic.

A large portion of the chapter highlights Earhart as a writer: she published three books, edited at Cosmopolitan, wrote for The New York Times and National Geographic, secretly wrote short stories under fictional names, and loved poetry most. Three of her poems are quoted: “From an Airplane” (“watchful purple hills / That hold the lake,” “round, yellow eyes of the hamlet”), “Courage” (“Unless we dare”), and “Only Baggage,” about the 1928 flight where she was merely a passenger while Wilmer Stultz and Louis Gordon piloted — the poem calls her “Atalante of the Air.”

The narrative then describes her May 20, 1932 solo flight: lifting off from a small runway in Newfoundland, Canada, enduring fifteen harrowing hours of storms and mechanical failures, and landing on “the green backdrop of Londonderry, Ireland,” where the treasure photo was taken. Five years and one day later (May 21, 1937), she and navigator Fred Noonan departed on the round-the-world attempt that ended in her disappearance after 22 of a planned 30 days; the aircraft was never found.

The chapter closes with the author's most direct treasure-hunting guidance of the book so far: “I would advise you to go out and explore more if you want to find the treasures I have hidden... Go where others have not. We never know what's past the next tree line, over the next rise, or around the next corner unless we see it for ourselves.” The final paragraph transitions to Chapter 8 by previewing Charles Loloma — a man who also held a pilot's license, the most renowned Native American jewelry designer, born and living on the Hopi reservation in Arizona until his death, an elder in “the oldest tribe in the United States.”

The Treasure(s)

A signed black-and-white photograph of Amelia Earhart standing on/beside her Lockheed Vega 5B, taken just after her May 20, 1932 solo Atlantic landing in a pasture near Londonderry, Ireland. According to the author, this is the only signed copy known to exist; Purdue University and Harvard University each hold unsigned copies of the same image, which is what makes the treasure copy uniquely valuable.

The aircraft in the photo — the Lockheed Vega 5B, registration NR7952, which Earhart nicknamed her “Little Red Bus” — is itself a US aviation landmark. She sold it to the Franklin Institute in Philadelphia in 1933; the Smithsonian acquired it in 1966, and it has been displayed at the National Air and Space Museum in Washington, DC since 1976.

People

Amelia Earhart (aviator, 1897-1937) — The chapter's central figure. Born July 24, 1897 in Atchison, Kansas; the most famous female aviator in US history. First woman to fly solo across the Atlantic (1932) and the first solo Hawaii-to-California flight (1935). Co-founder and first president of the Ninety-Nines. She joined Purdue University as a visiting aeronautical faculty member in 1935; Purdue's “Amelia Earhart Fund” bought her Lockheed Electra 10E. She vanished July 2, 1937 over the central Pacific en route to Howland Island. Her life is the chapter's main reservoir of clues-by-association.

Fred Noonan (navigator) — Earhart's companion on the fatal 1937 round-the-world attempt. A former Pan Am navigator who charted the trans-Pacific Clipper routes out of San Francisco; figures heavily in the disappearance lore tied to Pacific geography (Howland Island).

Charles Loloma (1921-1991) — Named in the chapter-end transition to Beauty's Bespoken Treasures. A Hopi jeweler/artist born near Hotevilla on Third Mesa, Arizona, who also held a pilot's license. Described as the most renowned Native American jewelry designer and an elder of “the oldest tribe in the United States.”

Frank Borman (Apollo 8 commander) — Author of the first epigraph. The first human to orbit the Moon; ties to the exploration theme and potentially to Chapter 19 (Moon Rocks & Meteors).

Erno Rubik (Rubik's Cube inventor) — Author of the second epigraph, whose “puzzles around you... you will solve them” line doubles as a nudge that clues are present and solvable.

Wilmer Stultz and Louis Gordon — Pilots of the 1928 “Friendship” crossing on which Earhart was merely a passenger (the subject of her poem “Only Baggage”).

Places & Geographies

Honest framing first: the chapter's most quoted line (“past the next tree line, over the next rise, or around the next corner”) names no place at all — it is a search-method instruction, not a location. Every concrete US geography in this chapter comes from the surrounding Earhart biography, the photo's provenance, and the Chapter 8 transition.

  • Atchison, Kansas — Earhart's birthplace and the densest cluster of Earhart geography: the Amelia Earhart Birthplace Museum (223 N. Terrace St., on a bluff over the Missouri River), the Amelia Earhart Hangar Museum at the Amelia Earhart Memorial Airport, the Stan Herd one-acre crop “Earthwork” portrait on a hillside overlooking Warnock Lake, the International Forest of Friendship (a living memorial with trees from all 50 states), and the Amelia Earhart Memorial Bridge on US-59 over the Missouri River.
  • Amelia Earhart Peak, Yosemite National Park, California (~11,974 ft, Tuolumne County, named 1967) — the single named natural feature bearing her name on genuine public land. Note the constraint conflict below: it is high, strenuous, and far from a road.
  • Purdue University, West Lafayette, Indiana — holds the world's largest Earhart archive (the George Palmer Putnam Collection) including an unsigned copy of the treasure photo. State-university campus, not a public-land hide candidate.
  • Harvard University / Schlesinger Library, Cambridge, Massachusetts (3 James Street, Radcliffe Yard) — the other holder of an unsigned copy of the photo. Urban/institutional, not a hide candidate.
  • Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum, Washington, DC — displays the actual Lockheed Vega 5B since 1976 (conservation at the Udvar-Hazy Center, Chantilly, Virginia).
  • The Franklin Institute, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania — owned the Vega 1933-1966.
  • Burbank & Hollywood, California — where Lockheed built the Vega; and Kinner Field in South Gate (Los Angeles area), where Earhart learned to fly in 1921 and likely composed “From an Airplane.”
  • Los Angeles, California → Newark, New Jersey — endpoints of her 1932 first solo nonstop transcontinental flight (same Vega).
  • Washington, DC & New Jersey — endpoints of her Mexico City flight records the chapter explicitly lists.
  • Hopi Reservation, northeastern Arizona (Navajo & Coconino counties) — Hotevilla and Old Oraibi (Third Mesa, “oldest continuously inhabited village in the US”), Charles Loloma's home, named in the Chapter 8 transition. Sovereign tribal land, not open US public land.
  • Atlanta, Georgia — surfaced via the “Atalanta of the Air” mythology thread (the city's name traces to the huntress Atalanta). Reinforced by the book's dedicated Chapter 16 Atlanta Olympics content.
  • Non-US (provenance only): Harbour Grace, Newfoundland, Canada (1932 departure) and Ballyarnett / Gallagher's pasture near Culmore, Londonderry, Northern Ireland (where the treasure photo was taken). Neither can be a hide site under the US-public-land rule.

Themes & the Lesson

The subtitle “Explore More” is used as an explicit, literal instruction rather than just inspirational framing. The author ties Earhart's restless curiosity (and his children's) to the act of physically searching, then states it outright: explore more, “go where others have not,” and look “past the next tree line, over the next rise, or around the next corner.”

Read literally, this is a description of search behavior, not a named place. It reinforces the boots-on-the-ground requirement: the box is somewhere a casual visitor would overlook, reachable by physically pushing just beyond an obvious sightline — behind a band of trees, just over a low rise, or around a bend in a trail or road. This is consistent with the author's hard constraints (public land, not buried, within ~3 miles of a road, not dangerous). The Rubik epigraph (“you'll find the puzzles around you... you will solve them”) doubles as a nudge that clues are present and solvable. The “Atalante of the Air” / Atalanta huntress motif also resonates with the book's “the chase” theme — and, notably, the myth ends with Atalanta and her husband transformed into lions, a lexical echo of the “Lion's Share” box name.

"Next tree line, over the next rise, or around the next corner" (landscape hint)

This is the author's most explicit literal search-instruction in the chapter, embedded in treasure-hunting advice attached to the “Explore More” theme. Read literally, it describes search behavior rather than a named place: the box sits just out of normal sightline at a spot most people walk past — behind a band of trees, just over a low hill (“rise”), or around a bend in a trail or terrain feature. It reinforces the same physical-search guidance found elsewhere in the book and is consistent with the confirmed constraints (public land, not buried, within ~3 miles of a road, not dangerous). Web research found no published interview where the author elaborates on this sentence, and the community “Chapter 7 Notes” analysis is paywalled. There is no evidence it encodes a cipher or a specific geography. Note: in mountaineering “tree line” means timberline (the elevation above which trees stop growing); if read that strongly it would point to a high-elevation, near-summit setting — but the book's casual phrasing (“past the next tree line”) more plausibly means an ordinary edge of woods, so the treeline-elevation reading is speculative.

Charles Loloma / Hopi Reservation, Arizona

Charles Sequevya Loloma (1921-1991) was a Hopi jeweler/artist born near Hotevilla on Third Mesa, Arizona, of the Badger Clan. He trained under painter Fred Kabotie, assisted reproducing the Awatovi murals for MoMA, painted murals for the 1939 Golden Gate Exposition in San Francisco, and after WWII studied ceramics at Alfred University (NY) on the GI Bill. In 1954 he opened a pottery shop in Scottsdale, AZ as a first tenant of Lloyd Kiva New's Kiva Craft Center, turning to jewelry in 1955 and pioneering gold, gemstones, and exotic materials in Native jewelry. By 1966 he built a studio/gallery on a rocky knoll near Hotevilla. The Hopi Reservation sits in northeastern Arizona (Navajo & Coconino counties), entirely surrounded by the Navajo Nation. Old Oraibi (Third Mesa) is widely cited as the oldest continuously inhabited settlement in the US and is a National Historic Landmark — matching the chapter's “oldest tribe in the United States” framing.

Amelia Earhart poem "From an Airplane" — "purple hills that hold the lake"

“From an Airplane” is one of four short imagist poems the 23-year-old Earhart submitted to Poetry magazine on April 16, 1921, under the pseudonym “Emil A. Harte.” Editor Harriet Monroe rejected them (“Not quite—as yet. But we consider these unusually promising...”) and never published them; the originals survive in the Earhart Papers at Purdue. She wrote it during her first months as a student pilot at Kinner Field in South Gate (LA area) under instructor Anita “Neta” Snook. Scholars treat it as an evocative imagined/composite landscape, not a documented named lake; no source identifies a specific real lake, hamlet, or hill range. One real-world Earhart geography that does literally match “purple hills that hold the lake” is Atchison, Kansas: artist Stan Herd's one-acre living-portrait earthwork of Earhart (1997) sits on a hillside overlooking Warnock Lake (city-owned park land, with a hilltop viewing deck) — an actual hills-holding-a-lake memorial scene.

Purdue University Libraries (Amelia Earhart photo/collection)

Purdue Libraries holds the George Palmer Putnam Collection of Amelia Earhart Papers — the world's largest Earhart repository (~45.5 cubic feet; over 5,000 pieces), donated from 1940 by her husband/publisher. It contains photographs, flight logs, charts, correspondence, scrapbooks, and a previously-unknown 16mm film (donated 2015). Earhart served as a Purdue career counselor and aeronautics adviser 1935-1937; the Purdue Research Foundation's “Amelia Earhart Fund” bought the Lockheed Electra 10E she lost in 1937. Buildings named for her include Earhart Residence Hall and a 2024 airport terminal. Note: the chapter's treasure photo shows the Vega (not the Purdue-funded Electra); the Vega 5B is at the Smithsonian, not Purdue. The campus is state-university land, not a public-land hide candidate.

Harvard University Libraries / Schlesinger Library (Amelia Earhart photograph)

The “Harvard” reference points to the Arthur and Elizabeth Schlesinger Library on the History of Women in America (Harvard Radcliffe Institute), at 3 James Street, Cambridge, Massachusetts. It holds substantial Earhart material: the Papers of Amelia Earhart (much saved by her sister Muriel), her mother's papers, and related photo collections, largely as unsigned reference/archival prints — consistent with the chapter's “unsigned copy” detail. The signed-vs-unsigned distinction likely traces to the 1935 Lockheed publicity series shot by photographer Joe Washburn, who had Earhart sign copies for himself. The library itself is on Harvard's campus in dense urban Cambridge, so it is essentially ruled out as a hide site; its value is corroborative of Earhart as the chapter's anchor.

Lockheed Vega 5B ("Little Red Bus," reg. NR7952)

Earhart's specific aircraft was a Lockheed Vega Model 5B, registration NR7952, bought in 1930. In it she became the first woman to fly solo across the Atlantic, departing Harbour Grace, Newfoundland on May 20-21, 1932, and landing in a farmer's field at Culmore near Londonderry, Northern Ireland. In the same Vega she flew the first solo nonstop transcontinental flight by a woman (Aug 24-25, 1932), Los Angeles to Newark, New Jersey. The Vega was designed primarily by Jack Northrop for Lockheed; production moved from a Sycamore Street factory in Hollywood to a Burbank plant in 1928, launching the San Fernando Valley aviation industry. Earhart sold the Vega to the Franklin Institute (Philadelphia) in 1933; the Smithsonian acquired it in 1966, and it has been displayed at the National Air and Space Museum (Washington, DC) since 1976.

Atalanta / "Atalante of the Air" (Greek mythology)

Atalanta is a swift-footed virgin huntress of Greek myth: exposed on a mountain at birth, nursed by a she-bear, devoted to Artemis and the hunt. She drew first blood in the Calydonian boar hunt and refused to marry unless a suitor beat her in a footrace; Hippomenes won by dropping three golden apples. Critically for this hunt, after they defiled a sanctuary the couple were transformed into lions — a direct lexical echo of the “Lion's Share” box name. The author quotes Earhart's self-reference as an “Atalanta of the Air” (treat the exact epithet as the book's framing; her common press nicknames were “Lady Lindy,” “Queen of the Air”). The strongest geographic association is Atlanta, Georgia, whose name traces to “Atalanta” via Martha Atalanta Lumpkin / Marthasville (1842) — dovetailing with the book's Chapter 16 Atlanta Olympics content.

Londonderry, Ireland (Derry) — 1932 solo-flight landing site

On May 20-21, 1932, after ~14 hours 56 minutes, Earhart put her red Lockheed Vega down in a cow pasture belonging to the Gallagher family at Ballyarnett, near Culmore, north of Londonderry/Derry, Northern Ireland. The treasure photo (Earhart by her Vega against a green field) was taken here. This is in Northern Ireland (UK), NOT the United States, so the landing site itself cannot be a hide location. The site is memorialized in Derry (a small Amelia Earhart Cottage Museum opened 2003, closed 2008). The US clue-by-association runs through Earhart's hometown, Atchison, Kansas, with its dense cluster of Earhart memorials.

Newfoundland, Canada (Harbour Grace departure)

Harbour Grace is a town on Conception Bay, Newfoundland and Labrador, Canada. On May 20, 1932 (~7:30pm) Earhart took off from the hand-built Harbour Grace Airfield — the first registered airstrip in Newfoundland, favored as a “hop-off” point because it is the closest North American land to Europe. The date marked the 5-year anniversary of Lindbergh's flight. The departure point and its memorials (a waterfront Earhart statue and plaque) are in Canada, not the US, so it cannot be a hide site. The US-side associations are the same flight's aircraft (Vega 5B, now at the Smithsonian; built in Burbank) and Earhart's birthplace, Atchison, Kansas.

Amelia Earhart (aviator) — broader US footprint

Earhart was born July 24, 1897 in a Gothic Revival house at 223 N. Terrace St. in Atchison, Kansas, on a bluff over the Missouri River (now the Birthplace Museum, run by the Ninety-Nines, which she co-founded). She is heavily memorialized across the US: a bronze statue in the US Capitol's National Statuary Hall (2022, representing Kansas), the Amelia Earhart Hangar Museum and Memorial Airport in Atchison, statues and plaques in North Hollywood / Los Angeles, and a plaque at Diamond Head Lookout in Honolulu. Most significant for public-land relevance: Amelia Earhart Peak (~11,974 ft, Tuolumne County) in Yosemite National Park, officially named July 2, 1967. Her fatal 1937 world flight began June 1 from Miami, Florida and ended with her disappearance July 2, 1937 near Howland Island in the Pacific.
