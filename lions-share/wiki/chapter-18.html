Andrew Carnegie's Emerald

Lesson: The Science of Giving

Chapter 18 · book pages 159-164

Summary

Chapter 18 opens with the treasure item: a large green Colombian emerald crystal still embedded in dark matrix rock, labeled “EMERALD / Muzo Mine / Boyacá Dept., Colombia.” A red-italic sidebar confirms its provenance: “This green Colombian emerald in matrix was a part of Andrew Carnegie's rare mineral collection. After his death, it passed through a few owners before I acquired it.” The chapter epigraphs are two giving-themed quotes from Brian Tracy (“Always give without remembering and always receive without forgetting.”) and Steve Goodier (“...none of us can ever run out of something worthwhile to give.”).

The narrative frame is a question from the author's editor, who wondered whether giving away such extravagant, rare, expensive items has a “punchline,” because doing so “goes so strongly against human nature.” The author reflects on this and ties it back to the book's stated motivation (find joy and share it) and, more deeply, to his childhood and upbringing.

The author reveals autobiographical detail: his family did not save much money, but his parents taught him the value of giving. His parents met in seminary, and soon after graduation they helped start and lead a new church in Huntersville, NC, “built upon the ideal of service.” The church assisted the local homeless shelter, the soup kitchen, the battered women's shelter, and the local Habitat for Humanity chapter. His father sat on the board of directors of the local Food Bank and the Red Cross. From a young age, the author was “required to volunteer” (not always willingly).

The bulk of the chapter is a biography of Andrew Carnegie, framed as someone who approached giving “like a science.” Carnegie was born poor in Dunfermline, Scotland; the family immigrated to the United States in 1848, with his mother deciding to move them to Pittsburgh when Andrew was thirteen. His first American job was as a bobbin boy in a cotton factory (16 hours/day, 6 days/week, for pennies — an “illegal child laborer” by today's standards). He became a Western Union messenger boy, memorized telegraph sounds, and impressed Thomas A. Scott, who became his first mentor and later (as VP of the Pennsylvania Railroad) promoted him to superintendent of the western division. Carnegie invested, built wealth, and in 1889 wrote the “Gospel of Wealth,” arguing the wealthy have a moral obligation to give back (“The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced”). In 1901 he sold his steel companies to J.P. Morgan for $480 million, becoming the wealthiest man in the world (equivalent ~$310 billion today).

The chapter then details Carnegie's philanthropy: he formed the Carnegie Corporation in 1911 and pioneered the charitable endowment (compounding principal, designed to last in perpetuity). His library program funded nearly 1,700 U.S. libraries (800+ still operating) on the condition that local governments match funds for land and books. His named institutions include the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, Carnegie Institution of Science, Carnegie Hero Fund, Carnegie Foundation for the Advancement of Teaching, and Carnegie Mellon University. His endowments funded varied outcomes including the discovery of insulin, the production of Sesame Street, and the development of Pell Grants. He gave away over $350 million; his wife and children received only his home and estate.

The chapter closes with the author's wish that finders share their joy with others (“a more perfect punchline”), and a critical geographic statement: “One of the hundreds of science projects Andrew Carnegie funded was the Mount Wilson Observatory. It is located not far from my home, just to the northeast.” He notes the 100-inch Hooker telescope erected at Mount Wilson in 1917, which Edwin Hubble used to measure the size of the universe, detect galaxies beyond the Milky Way, and prove the universe is expanding. The final line: “The future is alive with a thousand adventures.”

The Treasure(s)

The featured item is Andrew Carnegie's emerald in matrix — a green Colombian emerald crystal still embedded in dark matrix rock, sourced from the Muzo Mine, Boyacá Department, Colombia. Per the book's red-italic sidebar, the stone was “a part of Andrew Carnegie's rare mineral collection,” passing through a few owners after his death before the author acquired it.

A research caveat worth flagging: open web sources did not confirm a specifically named “Carnegie emerald” gemstone. What is firmly verifiable is the Carnegie → mineral-collection pipeline: in 1904 Carnegie purchased the ~12,000-specimen William W. Jefferis mineral collection (of West Chester, PA) and donated it to the Carnegie Museum of Natural History in Pittsburgh, anchoring the Hillman Hall of Minerals and Gems. The dealer behind the book's caches — The Arkenstone / iRocks.com of Dallas, TX — describes sourcing “an emerald formerly owned by Andrew Carnegie,” but the exact cut, weight, and current twin specimens remain unverified from open sources. Treat the emerald's specific provenance as plausible but unconfirmed.

Themes & the Lesson

The subtitle “The Science of Giving” frames philanthropy as something that can be approached methodically and systematically rather than casually. The author contrasts giving-as-art (like picking a birthday gift) with Carnegie's giving-as-science: Carnegie studied charity and engineered a new paradigm — the perpetual, compounding endowment and matching-fund libraries — to maximize impact.

The author uses this to answer his editor's “punchline” question, explaining that deliberately giving away extraordinary treasure is not against human nature but a learned, practiced value (instilled by his pastor parents in Huntersville, NC) and a “catalyst for change and joy.” The implicit instruction to the reader/finder is to share the joy of the treasure with others rather than merely possess it — “that would be a more perfect punchline.” A secondary thread — science, lenses, seeing, and the “new frontier” / cosmos — runs through the Mount Wilson / Hooker Telescope / Hubble material that closes the chapter.

Huntersville, North Carolina (and the author's NC childhood church/geography)

Huntersville is a town in Mecklenburg County, NC, the first town north of Charlotte officially recognized (1873). Originally called “Craighead” after a local minister, it was renamed for landowner Robert Boston Hunter. It has 30+ historic landmarks including very old churches: Hopewell Presbyterian (est. 1762), St. Mark's Episcopal (1884), and Huntersville Presbyterian (1878). The book names Huntersville as where the author's parents started/led their church, tying Jon Collins-Black's family to the Charlotte/Mecklenburg region.

Sourcing caveat: “Huntersville” could not be independently confirmed in public web sources. Multiple profiles (AOL, CNBC) describe Collins-Black growing up “in the boondocks” of NC, with a minister father who hand-built a log cabin on land gifted by a congregant. The publicly cited geographic anchor for his childhood home is NOT Huntersville but Emerald Hollow Mine, described as “a short drive away,” where he spent hours sifting creeks/dirt for gems. The Huntersville-as-church-town detail appears to come only from the book text, so it is plausibly accurate but unverified against secondary sources.

Emerald Hollow Mine is in Hiddenite, Alexander County, NC, in the foothills of the Brushy Mountains — the only public-prospecting emerald mine in the US, with 60+ gem types (emerald, aquamarine, sapphire, the rare “hiddenite,” etc.). The 1,438-carat Stephenson Emerald (1969) came from this area. Huntersville is ~46 miles (~50 min) from Hiddenite. Notably, Hiddenite/Alexander County is the SAME county as the FAILED Rocky Face Mountain search.

Andrew Carnegie

Andrew Carnegie (1835–1919) was a Scottish-born American steel magnate and the era's foremost philanthropist. His family emigrated from Dunfermline, Scotland to Allegheny (now part of Pittsburgh), PA in 1848. He made his fortune in steel (Carnegie Steel, centered in the Pittsburgh region) and then gave most of it away, famously declaring “the man who dies rich dies disgraced.” His best-known philanthropy was funding ~1,681 public library buildings in the US (2,509 worldwide), plus institutions bearing his name (Carnegie Hall in NYC, Carnegie Mellon University and the Carnegie Museums in Pittsburgh, the Carnegie Endowment, etc.).

His personal/physical US footprint: he lived in Pittsburgh; built his famous NYC mansion on Fifth Avenue (the “Carnegie Mansion,” now the Cooper Hewitt museum, giving the surrounding Manhattan neighborhood the name “Carnegie Hill”); and spent his final summers (dying Aug 11, 1919) at Shadow Brook, a 54-room, ~600-acre estate in the Berkshire Hills of Lenox, Massachusetts. His remains traveled via the rail depot at Hillsdale, NY to White Plains, and he is buried at Sleepy Hollow Cemetery in Sleepy Hollow, New York.

Correction relevant to “clue by association”: the famous Carnegie estate Dungeness on Cumberland Island, Georgia, was built by his BROTHER Thomas Carnegie and wife Lucy, not Andrew himself; the wider Carnegie family long owned much of Cumberland Island. On the treasure item, web sources did not confirm a specific documented gemstone called the “Carnegie emerald”; Andrew Carnegie's documented gem/mineral legacy is the collection he funded at the Carnegie Museum of Natural History in Pittsburgh (Hillman Hall of Minerals and Gems), including his 1904 purchase of the William W. Jefferis mineral collection.

Muzo Mine, Boyacá Department, Colombia (origin of the Chapter 18 emerald)

The Muzo mine sits in western Boyacá Department, Colombia, roughly 60 miles northwest of Bogotá in the high Andes. It is the world's most famous emerald source, worked since pre-Columbian times (the Muzo people, “the Emerald People”). After Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada's people located the mines in the 1530s, Spain ran a “Mina Real” (Royal Mine) at Muzo. Muzo emeralds are prized for intense green color and large, transparent hexagonal crystals; Chivor is its sister-deposit. The mine is a foreign location with no US geography — its relevance is purely as the gem's birthplace/provenance.

The treasure-relevant link is the ITEM, not the mine. Per the book and the gem dealer behind the boxes (The Arkenstone / iRocks.com, Dallas TX), Collins-Black sourced “an emerald formerly owned by Andrew Carnegie.” No independent catalog entry for a specifically named “Carnegie emerald” was found, so its exact cut/weight remain unverified. What is firmly verifiable is the Andrew Carnegie → mineral-collection pipeline: in 1904 Carnegie purchased the 12,000-specimen William W. Jefferis mineral collection (of West Chester, PA) for ~$20,000 and donated it to the Carnegie Museum of Natural History in Pittsburgh. Famous Muzo/Colombian emeralds with fixed US homes (useful for cross-checks): the Mackay Emerald, Gachalá, and Chalk emeralds at the Smithsonian (Washington, DC); and the Patricia and Maximilian emeralds at the American Museum of Natural History (NYC).

Carnegie libraries (United States)

Between 1883 and 1929, Carnegie funded 2,509 library buildings worldwide; 1,795 were built in the US (1,687 public + 108 academic). Roughly half of the US Carnegie public-library buildings still serve as libraries today; many others survive repurposed as museums, offices, churches, or campus buildings. The buildings are typically Beaux-Arts/Classical Revival, located prominently in town centers (often beside a courthouse or central square).

North Carolina received 10 public Carnegie libraries (granted 1901–1917), in Charlotte, Greensboro (two), Winston-Salem, Rutherford College, Hendersonville, Andrews, Murphy, Durham, and Hickory. Most no longer function as libraries. The single most hunt-relevant Carnegie building is in Murphy, NC (Cherokee County, far western NC): a two-story Carnegie library at 87 Peachtree Street, next to the Cherokee County Courthouse. Since 1977 it has housed the Cherokee County Historical Museum, an official interpretive center for the Trail of Tears National Historic Trail; Murphy was the site of Fort Butler, a Cherokee removal holding area in the 1830s, and is a gateway to the Nantahala National Forest and Great Smoky Mountains.

100-inch Hooker Telescope (Mount Wilson Observatory)

The 100-inch Hooker Telescope is the historic reflecting telescope at Mount Wilson Observatory in Southern California. It saw “first light” on November 2, 1917, and was the largest-aperture telescope in the world from 1917 until 1949 (when surpassed by the 200-inch Hale Telescope at Palomar). It was named for Los Angeles industrialist John D. Hooker, who in 1906 gave a $45,000 grant for the 100-inch glass mirror blank (cast by Saint-Gobain in France). When Hooker declined further funding, Andrew Carnegie (via the Carnegie Institution of Washington) paid the remaining cost — the direct Carnegie tie that makes the instrument relevant to Chapter 18. The observatory was founded in 1904 by astronomer George Ellery Hale.

The telescope's fame rests on Edwin Hubble, who joined Mount Wilson in 1919 and used the 100-inch in 1922–1923 to prove the universe extends far beyond the Milky Way, and later to demonstrate the universe is expanding. The telescope is still in place and is open to the public: the site has a museum, viewing gallery, and weekend docent tours. Public access was a condition of the observatory's original 1904 land lease.

Edwin Hubble

Edwin Powell Hubble (Nov 20, 1889 – Sept 28, 1953) was the American astronomer who proved the universe extends far beyond the Milky Way and is expanding. Born in Marshfield, Missouri, he moved to Wheaton, Illinois at age 11, studied at the University of Chicago, then law at Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar, briefly taught high school in New Albany, Indiana, returned to Chicago for a PhD in astronomy at Yerkes Observatory (Williams Bay, Wisconsin), and served in the US Army in France in WWI. His landmark work was done at Mount Wilson Observatory (joined 1919): in 1923 he found Cepheid variables in the Andromeda “Nebula,” proving it a separate galaxy; in 1929 he published the redshift-distance relationship (Hubble's Law). He died in San Marino, California, and has no known public grave.

Carnegie convergence: The Mount Wilson Observatory was built and funded by the Carnegie Institution of Washington. So the chapter's Carnegie theme and the Hubble / “new frontier” / cosmos theme converge at one physical place: Mount Wilson. A Hubble Space Telescope scale model and historical marker stand on the Webster County Courthouse grounds in Marshfield, MO.

Carnegie Institution of Science

The Carnegie Institution of Science (founded 1902 by Andrew Carnegie with a $10M endowment; legally “Carnegie Institution of Washington,” now branded “Carnegie Science”) is the research endowment that links Chapter 18 to a concrete network of US scientific sites. In 1904 the Institution funded George Ellery Hale to found the Mount Wilson Solar Observatory near Pasadena, CA — site of the 60-inch (1908) and 100-inch Hooker (1917) telescopes. Carnegie operated Mount Wilson until 1986, when light pollution shifted primary observing to Las Campanas, Chile; the Observatories' main US offices remain on Santa Barbara Street in Pasadena.

The Institution's administrative HQ was a Beaux-Arts limestone building at 16th and P Streets NW, Washington, D.C. (built 1910), a National Historic Landmark. Beyond DC and Pasadena, Carnegie's US research footprint includes the Department of Embryology in Baltimore, MD, and plant/global-ecology research at Stanford/Palo Alto, CA. Most directly hunt-relevant: Mount Wilson sits inside the Angeles National Forest, federal public land.

Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania

Pittsburgh was the base of Andrew Carnegie's life and steel empire and is the dominant US geography associated with the chapter's treasure owner. His family settled in Allegheny City (now Pittsburgh's North Side) in 1848. He founded Carnegie Steel and sold it to J.P. Morgan in 1901 (creating U.S. Steel). His first mill, the Edgar Thomson Works, still operates; the Homestead Steel Works and Carrie Blast Furnaces (now National Historic Landmarks managed by Rivers of Steel) were core to his empire and the site of the 1892 Homestead Strike.

Carnegie poured his fortune back into Pittsburgh institutions: the Carnegie Institute (1895), Carnegie Music Hall, the Carnegie Library of Pittsburgh, and the Carnegie Technical Schools (later Carnegie Mellon University). The most direct link to the chapter's emerald is the Carnegie Museum of Natural History's Hillman Hall of Minerals and Gems (with the 2007 Wertz Gallery). Carnegie seeded the collection: ~2,600 gems donated in 1902, and the ~12,000-specimen William W. Jefferis collection (of West Chester, PA) purchased and donated in 1904. Other PA ties include the borough of Carnegie, PA; Cresson, PA (Allegheny Mountain summer retreat); and the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club near the South Fork Dam — near the NPS Johnstown Flood National Memorial.
