Rubies to Wear

Lesson: Inspiration Is Welcome

Chapter 6 · book pages 64-69

Summary

Chapter 6, "Rubies to Wear," is built around the theme of inspiration as a guiding force, framed through ruby symbolism and the stories of the chapter's three ruby treasure items. The author opens with two epigraphs on inspiration (from Quentin Blake and Madeleine L'Engle) and likens inspiration to "a wise breeze blowing in the direction I need to go" that, when followed, leads somewhere "almost always better than I imagined."

He then surveys cross-cultural ruby symbolism: across the world rubies are associated with success, health, longevity, and enlightenment. In Burma the ruby's red glow represents "the inextinguishable flame of immortality," and ancient Burmese warriors reportedly cut their skin to insert rubies for invincibility. In Thailand, the term Noppakao refers to nine gemstones corresponding to eight planets and the sun; the ruby corresponds with the sun and is the most powerful stone. He notes the treasure's ruby ring was created in the Sukhothai culture, where rubies were believed to hold magical powers. In Hindu tradition, offering a fine ruby to Krishna could inspire rebirth as an emperor or king, making rubies the most coveted stone; fine rubies were kept far from lower-quality stones to avoid "contamination." He explains rubies are today the most expensive gems by size, and that the treasure ring's "pigeon's blood" deep-red color is the most sought by collectors.

The narrative then turns to acquisition. After holding the Tuyet Nguyet ruby ring, the author wanted more ruby pieces but kept losing bidding wars for vintage rubies. He "aspired for inspiration," and two days later, while reading online about Art Smith's jewelry, an ad for contemporary jewelry artists at a prestigious auction house "popped up." Feeling "as if a gentle wind were nudging" him, he clicked and was drawn to two African American jewelry artists whose work was bold, colorful, and refined like Art Smith's. Realizing he could commission original pieces, he contacted Angie Marei and Lauren Harwell Godfrey. Angie Marei designs jewelry in Brooklyn, New York, is of Egyptian-Dominican heritage, references ancient Egypt, and keeps a blog on what inspires her; she agreed to make wing-shaped ruby earrings (valued above $20,000), and her pieces have been worn by Beyonce, Rihanna, and Zendaya. Lauren Harwell Godfrey draws on her African heritage and vivid African textiles, does extensive charity work (World Central Kitchen, No Kid Hungry, Futures Without Violence), and designed the ruby pendant necklace shown on page 64 (a 3-carat ruby with moonstones and diamonds, from her "Cleopatra's Vault" collection, valued over $45,000).

The chapter closes with autobiography and a treasure-hunting hint. As a kid the author couldn't afford posters, so he copied inspirational quotes from posters at Waldenbooks and taped them to his bedroom wall (he prints Vince Lombardi and Ralph Waldo Emerson quotes). Unable to afford comic books, he borrowed heroes from his parents — Martin Luther King Jr. and Mahatma Gandhi. Though his father was a pastor, he found no inspiration in "traditional white protestant church services" until his father began trading pulpits with local black ministers; Rev. James Henderson from "the black Baptist church down the street" preached to their congregation, and the author's first black Baptist service was transformative. As an adult he finds inspiration in fatherhood, music, writing, travel, adventures, nature, writing children's books, and a decade of meditation. He then addresses the reader directly: finding the treasure boxes may require inspiration; if discouraged, "you can aspire for a little help," and "Just like peering behind a rock to find a treasure box, inspiration can appear almost anywhere. Just feel for where its breeze nudges you." The chapter ends by transitioning to Chapter 7's subject, Amelia Earhart, first woman to fly solo across the Atlantic.

The Treasure(s)

This chapter features three ruby pieces, two of them newly commissioned by the author from contemporary designers:

  • Ruby pendant necklace by Lauren Harwell Godfrey — a 3-carat ruby with moonstones and diamonds, from her "Cleopatra's Vault" collection, valued over $45,000. Shown on page 64.
  • Wing-shaped ruby earrings by Angie Marei — valued above $20,000.
  • 16th-century Thailand ruby ring — from Tuyet Nguyet's 100-gold-ring collection (Sukhothai culture; a large round "pigeon's blood" ruby). The author calls it his favorite ring of hers in the treasure, linking this chapter back to Chapter 2.

Themes & the Lesson

The subtitle "Inspiration Is Welcome" frames inspiration as an invisible, wind-like force the author actively invites and follows. He uses it two ways: as autobiography (he sought inspiration from heroes, quotes, and a transformative black Baptist church service, and now from meditation), and as a meta-instruction to the searcher. The explicit application to the hunt is to not give up and to stay open and intuitive — "aspire for a little help" when discouraged and "feel for where its breeze nudges you."

Notably, the lesson is delivered alongside the chapter's most literal-sounding physical hint, comparing finding inspiration to "peering behind a rock to find a treasure box." Read literally (per the author's no-cipher guidance), this suggests the box may be tucked behind a rock rather than buried — consistent with the author's confirmed "not buried" rule.

"Peering behind a rock" hiding-method hint

This is a thematic/methodological clue, not a physical artifact. The exact "peering behind a rock" phrasing is an internal extraction from our Chapter 6 notes and could not be externally verified, so treat the phrase itself as unverified — but the underlying hiding-method facts are well sourced. All are on PUBLIC land, in "safe" places, none more than three miles from a road, with no high-water crossings, reachable within a couple of hours' walk. Weather exposure is relevant: the author reported having to check that a box was "still safe" after storms, and searchers believe boxes sit in a protective Pelican-type case — implying a box wedged behind, under an overhang of, or beside a natural rock rather than in a cave or structure.

Sukhothai (Thailand)

An ancient kingdom and city in north-central Thailand, founded in 1238 and considered the first national capital of the Siamese/Thai nation. The name derives from Sanskrit sukha (happiness) + udaya (dawn/arising) and is universally translated as "the Dawn of Happiness" — the most plausible hook for the book's sun/dawn theme. In the Noppakao/Navaratna "nine gems" tradition the ruby is the stone of the Sun, the genuine thematic link between a ruby ring "created in Sukhothai" and sun/dawn symbolism. No US geographic association was found: no US sister city, town, or replica. Any "pointing" would be purely thematic (sun/dawn/happiness place-names), which is speculation, not evidence.

Tuyet Nguyet ring collection (the "100 gold rings")

Tuyet Nguyet (1934-2020) was the Vietnamese-born founder, editor and publisher of Arts of Asia magazine. Over ~40 years she and her husband Stephen Markbreiter assembled about 100 antique gold rings spanning the 1st-19th centuries from across Asia (Java, Khmer Cambodia, Burma, Vietnam, Tibet, India, the Philippines, Thailand, Bali). After her death the collection was dispersed by Sotheby's in the online sale "Golden Splendour," held in Hong Kong, 21-28 July 2021. The Chapter 6 Thailand ruby ring almost certainly corresponds to "A gold 'Makara' ring with ruby-set eyes, Northern Thailand, 18th century or earlier." The collection has no US museum home. The only thin US biographical tie is Chicago, Illinois (Mundelein College, where she studied journalism). Confirms the Chapter 2 ↔ Chapter 6 provenance link, but not a location pointer.

Ralph Waldo Emerson

The central figure of American Transcendentalism, who spent nearly his entire adult life in Concord, Massachusetts. Emerson owned the woodland at Walden Pond and let Thoreau build his cabin there in 1845; in 1922 the Emerson family donated the surrounding land to Massachusetts, which is why Walden Pond is now a public state reservation. Important caveat: the "what lies within us" line widely attributed to Emerson (and likely the wall quote) is a documented misattribution traced to Henry Stanley Haskins's 1940 book — so don't over-read it as a literal clue beyond signaling the Emerson/Transcendentalist (and cross-chapter Thoreau) theme. Emerson points squarely at Concord, MA, with Walden Pond State Reservation as the strongest public-land target.

Amelia Earhart

Aviation pioneer (1897-1937), first woman to fly solo nonstop across the Atlantic (1932), named at the Chapter 6 → 7 transition and flagged as an author favorite. Born in Atchison, Kansas, which she considered her hometown. Atchison holds an unusually dense, accessible memorial cluster: the Birthplace Museum on a bluff over the Missouri River, the Hangar Museum (home of the surviving Lockheed Electra "Muriel"), the Stan Herd Amelia Earhart Earthwork on a public hillside next to Warnock Lake, and the International Forest of Friendship park (with a "Moon Tree" from an Apollo 14 seed — a possible echo of Chapter 19). The Earthwork / Forest of Friendship / Warnock Lake area fits the author's hard constraints (public, accessible, non-dangerous, no digging). Howland Island (her disappearance) is federal but a remote Pacific refuge — effectively a red herring.

Waldenbooks

The bookstore chain the author frequented as a kid was founded in 1933 in Bridgeport, Connecticut, by Lawrence Hoyt and Melvin T. Kafka. The name is a documented, direct reference to Thoreau's Walden: founder Hoyt stated he was "a lover of nature and especially of Thoreau, the naturalist, and his 'Walden'." This confirms the extraction's Thoreau hunch as fact, not speculation. The hunt-relevant geography is the namesake — Walden Pond State Reservation in Concord, Massachusetts (335-acre public DCR land, National Historic Landmark, bordered by Routes 2 and 126; not buried, not dangerous). Caveat: as a heavily trafficked, ranger-patrolled historic site under the one-box-per-state rule, Walden Pond is a poor practical hide — best treated as thematic confirmation of a deliberate Thoreau/Walden motif (cross-linking Chapter 14) rather than a literal hide location.
