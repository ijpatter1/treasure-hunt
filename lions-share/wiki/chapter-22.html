Picasso's Pendant

Lesson: A Love Story

Chapter 22 · book pages 189-194

Summary

Chapter 22, "Picasso's Pendant," is the book's final main treasure chapter. It opens with two epigraphs — Virgil's "Fortune favors the brave" and "The art of love is largely the art of persistence" (printed in the book as "Arthur Ellis," an apparent misprint of psychologist Albert Ellis) — which together announce the chapter's twin themes of bravery and persistence. The left page (189) reproduces the featured treasure: a 23-karat gold Picasso pendant titled "Jacqueline au chevalet" ("Jacqueline at the Easel"), depicting Picasso's wife painting at an easel. Only 32 such pendants were ever created, and only 20 were made available to the public.

The narrative begins with Picasso's birth in 1882 and the (likely apocryphal) story that his first word was "pit," referring to lápiz (Spanish for "pencil"), after which his mother placed a marker in his hand and he began to draw. The author summarizes Picasso's prolific career — more than 50,000 works including Guernica, The Old Guitarist, and Les Demoiselles d'Avignon — and his outsized, "larger-than-life" personality. The chapter then turns frankly critical of Picasso's treatment of women: despite marriage, he had numerous public mistresses, painted each as a "muse," and left a trail of "scandals, heartbreak, outcries," displaying an "egocentric attitude towards relationships of the heart."

The core love story unfolds "one summer day in Madoura, France," where Picasso, into his seventies, met Jacqueline Roque, a 25-year-old part-time employee in his ceramic studio (he was 47 years her elder). She declined his dinner invitation — she had recently divorced and knew his reputation. Unable to stop thinking about her, Picasso found her address, took a bag of chalk, and drew a large chalk mural of a dove on her house; she was flattered but again declined. He then returned each day to present a single red rose, and "this routine continued day after day. Days became weeks. Weeks turned into months." Over the course of half a year she kept saying no, until one day she accepted. They spent the rest of his life together — inseparable for his last two decades. Jacqueline became his wife, lover, muse, manager, accountant, caretaker, agent, and best friend, and he painted her in over 400 works. After Picasso died in April 1973 with Jacqueline at his side, she oversaw his estate for more than a decade, then lived her last thirteen years mostly in solitude before joining him in death.

The pendant's provenance: Picasso designed the gold pendant in 1956 and solicited goldsmith François Hugo of Hugo Ateliers in France to produce the pieces. François was the great-grandson of Victor Hugo (author of The Hunchback of Notre Dame and Les Misérables); François's grandson Nicolas still runs the family workshop, and the author acquired the pendant directly from Nicolas. In total, twenty-four different Picasso pendant designs were produced, each personal to Picasso, with only a small number of each made; "Jacqueline au chevalet" may have been the design dearest to his heart and was not offered for sale until after his death.

The author closes by drawing lessons for the treasure hunter — "Picasso was persistent and brave... taking the first step out your door might be the bravest action required of you here" — and then shares his own love story. It took him over forty years to find his wife, Kimberly, whom he met in 2013; they began their family in 2017 and were married "only a few months ago" as of writing. He notes Kimberly never questioned his choice to spend "close to five years and millions of dollars" building a treasure someone else will eventually own. He reveals Kimberly's favorite song, "Make Your Own Kind of Music" (originally recorded by Cass Elliot), was the last song played and sung at their wedding reception, and ties this to the theme of the final main chapter: "heading out on your own special path and collecting every possible treasure you can find along your way."

The Treasure(s)

The featured artifact is a single Picasso-designed gold pendant:

  • Picasso gold pendant "Jacqueline au chevalet" ("Jacqueline at the Easel") — 23-karat gold, depicting Jacqueline Roque painting at an easel. Designed by Picasso in 1956 and produced by the Hugo Ateliers workshop in France. Only 32 were created, with 20 made available to the public; it is one of 24 different Picasso pendant designs. The author states this design may have been the one dearest to Picasso's heart and that it was not offered for sale until after his death. He acquired the piece directly from Nicolas Hugo, the third-generation runner of the family atelier.

Note on the catalogue record (see Research): the broader Picasso–Hugo gold-medallion series is widely documented as 23k gold medallions roughly 4.8–5.4 cm across, struck in editions of about 20 plus artist/author proofs, each presented in a simple wooden Hugo-atelier box. Picasso reportedly kept them secret, "as though they constituted some private treasure," and only authorized a numbered sale edition in 1967. The book's specific "32 created / 20 public / 24 designs" figures align broadly with this series, though exact mintage counts vary across published sources.

People

Pablo Picasso (1881–1973; the book gives his birth as 1882) — born in Málaga, Spain; co-founder of Cubism and designer of the treasure pendant. He never lived in the United States; his life was European (Paris/Montmartre, the Côte d'Azur, Vallauris). His named works map to specific US museums (The Old Guitarist at the Art Institute of Chicago; Les Demoiselles d'Avignon at MoMA, New York; Guernica was held at MoMA from 1939–1981 before returning to Spain). His most famous US public artwork is the untitled "Chicago Picasso" sculpture in Daley Plaza. All are urban/institutional, not public-land hide sites.

Jacqueline Roque (1926–1986) — Picasso's second wife and final great muse, subject of the pendant. He met her at the Madoura pottery shop in Vallauris around 1952; they married secretly in the Vallauris village hall in 1961, and he painted her 400+ times ("L'Epoque Jacqueline"). Her entire documented life was in France (Paris, Golfe-Juan, Cannes, Mougins). She has essentially no personal US biography beyond a single 1980 visit to Picasso's MoMA retrospective in New York.

Kimberly (Collins-Black) — the author's wife. He met her in 2013, began a family in 2017, and married "only a few months ago" as of writing. Her favorite song, "Make Your Own Kind of Music," closed their wedding reception and anchors the chapter's final theme. No geographic detail about her is given in the chapter.

François Hugo (1899–1981) — one of France's leading 20th-century gold- and silversmiths; son of Georges Hugo and great-grandson of Victor Hugo. He executed Picasso's designs (1956–1967) in precious metal via the repoussé technique, working from an atelier he built just outside Aix-en-Provence, Provence. He is the literal provenance link in the pendant's chain.

Nicolas Hugo (b. c. 1990) — third-generation runner of Ateliers Hugo, grandson of François and son of Pierre Hugo. Based between London and Aix-en-Provence. He is the most concrete real-world contact named in the chapter: the author bought the pendant from him directly.

Victor Hugo (1802–1885) — French Romantic novelist (The Hunchback of Notre Dame, Les Misérables), great-grandfather of François Hugo. The author chose to highlight this lineage explicitly. Victor Hugo has no direct US biography, but at least one US town — Hugo, Oklahoma — is documented as named directly after him (see Places & Signals).

Cass Elliot ("Mama Cass") (1941–1974) — born Ellen Naomi Cohen; the singer who recorded "Make Your Own Kind of Music." Born in Baltimore, Maryland; raised between Baltimore and Alexandria, Virginia; rose to fame with The Mamas & the Papas and the Los Angeles / Laurel Canyon scene. Her US biographical anchors are urban/institutional (see Places).

Virgil (70–19 BC) — Roman poet, source of the first epigraph "Fortune favors the brave" (audentes Fortuna iuvat, Aeneid 10.284). A classical figure with no US biography; the namesake town Virgil, NY exists (see Places).

Albert Ellis (1913–2007) — American psychologist (founder of Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy), source of the second epigraph on persistence in love (the book prints the attribution as "Arthur Ellis," an apparent misprint). Thematic framing; no geographic clue.

David Douglas Duncan (1916–2018) — not named in the chapter but the strongest US thread by association: a Kansas City, Missouri-born war photojournalist and Eagle Scout who became Picasso and Jacqueline's intimate friend (1956–1973). His Jacqueline photographs are held by the Nelson-Atkins Museum of Art in Kansas City. Surfaced in research, not in the book text.

Places & Geographies

Madoura / Vallauris, France — the one specific named location in the chapter; the ceramics workshop (founded 1938 by Suzanne Douly and Georges Ramié) where Picasso met Jacqueline. Vallauris derives from Latin Vallis Aurea, "Golden Valley" / "Valley of Gold," tied to its prized pottery clay. French, with no US namesake — but the "Golden Valley" translation is a thematic thread (see Signals).

Hugo, Oklahoma — the strongest concrete US lead in this chapter. County seat of Choctaw County in SE Oklahoma, explicitly named after the novelist Victor Hugo at the suggestion of surveyor W.H. Darrough's wife, a fan of the writer. Crucially, it is surrounded by accessible public land that fits the author's hard constraints: Hugo Lake (US Army Corps of Engineers, ~13,250 acres), Hugo Lake State Park / Kiamichi Park (on the Kiamichi River ~7 mi east of town), and the large Hugo Wildlife Management Area (~19,227 acres). All public, road-accessible, and non-dangerous.

Other "Hugo" US toponyms — Hugo, Minnesota (Washington County; naming contested, possibly Victor Hugo or a railroad official) and Hugo, Colorado (named for stagecoach agent Hugo Richards, not the novelist — a dead end). Hugo Lake / Hugo Lake State Park in Oklahoma remain the only "Hugo" public land with a verified Victor Hugo naming.

Kansas City, Missouri — hometown of photographer David Douglas Duncan (Picasso/Jacqueline's close friend) and home of the Nelson-Atkins Museum of Art, which holds 161 Duncan photographs of Picasso and Jacqueline. An urban-museum association, not a public-land site.

Picasso / Jacqueline US museum holdings — Art Institute of Chicago (The Old Guitarist; a 1956 "Portrait of Jacqueline" lithograph); Museum of Modern Art, New York (Les Demoiselles d'Avignon; "Jacqueline's Portrait," 1956; the Dove lithograph; custodian of Guernica 1939–1981); Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York (a stylized Jacqueline portrait); Smithsonian / National Gallery of Art, Washington DC. The "Chicago Picasso" sculpture stands in Daley Plaza, Chicago (fabricated by U.S. Steel in Gary, Indiana). The largest US display of Picasso "Madoura" ceramics is at the Park West Museum, Southfield, Michigan. All urban/institutional — provenance context, not hide sites.

Cass Elliot US anchors — Los Angeles, California (Laurel Canyon home base; ashes interred at Mount Sinai Memorial Park, Hollywood Hills; cremation at Hollywood Forever; Hollywood Walk of Fame star); Baltimore, Maryland (birthplace); Alexandria, Virginia (childhood); Washington, DC (American University, early performing); Greenwich Village, New York City (early clubs; the song's songwriters Mann & Weil were NYC "Brill Building"); Monterey, California (the group closed the 1967 Monterey Pop Festival). All urban/institutional or private-memorial; none is a strong public-land hide candidate.

Aix-en-Provence & Rovezzano (Europe) — the Hugo atelier sits just outside Aix-en-Provence, Provence; François Hugo was born in Rovezzano, near Florence, Italy. The pendant's provenance chain is entirely European, with no US footprint of its own.

Virgil, New York (associative, from the epigraph) — a Cortland County town named directly for the Roman poet as part of the Central New York Military Tract's classical-naming scheme (neighbors Homer, Cicero, Ovid, Cincinnatus). It borders James Kennedy State Forest (~4,422 acres of public land with Finger Lakes Trail / North Country Trail access) and Virgil Mountain (2,132 ft, highest point in Cortland County, ~4.7-mi loop from the Odell/Baldwin Rd trailhead). Public, road-accessible, non-dangerous — but a long-shot association.

Themes & the Lesson

The subtitle "A Love Story" frames the chapter on three levels. Literally, it is the story of Picasso winning Jacqueline through persistence (six months of daily roses) and bravery (the chalk dove on her house) — virtues underscored by the Virgil and Albert Ellis epigraphs.

For the hunter, the author transplants those same virtues onto the act of treasure hunting: "Picasso was persistent and brave. These are not bad traits to have when hunting for a treasure... taking the first step out your door might be the bravest action required of you here." The explicit moral is an encouragement to physically commit — to stop deliberating and actually go search ("the first step out your door"), persisting even after repeated "no"s. This is a behavioral signpost (go, persist, be brave), not a coordinate.

Personally, it is the author's own love story with his wife Kimberly, recasting the whole five-year project as something done out of joy/love and tying directly into the next and final chapter's theme of walking "your own special path" — echoing the song "Make Your Own Kind of Music" and Chapter 23's title, "Sing Your Own Special Song."

Poem / Epigraph

Fortune favors the brave. — Virgil The art of love is largely the art of persistence. — Arthur Ellis

Research Findings

"Make Your Own Kind of Music" (Cass Elliot song)

Written by the husband-and-wife team Barry Mann and Cynthia Weil, produced by Steve Barri, and released by Cass Elliot ("Mama Cass") in September 1969 on Dunhill Records (the NY trio the Will-o-Bees recorded it first, in August 1969). A modest hit (#36 on the Billboard Hot 100), it gained far wider recognition decades later via TV (Lost, Dexter), film (Free Guy, 2021), and TikTok. The lyrics are an anthem of going your own path ("you gotta make your own kind of music, sing your own special song") — mapping directly onto Chapter 23's title "Sing Your Own Special Song." Critically, the lyrics contain no place names — no towns, rivers, mountains, or geographic references — so the song itself offers no literal geographic clue, consistent with the author's no-cipher rule. Any geography runs only by association through the performer (see below). Honest assessment: LOW direct geographic value, HIGH thematic value — it is an author signpost about philosophy, not a coordinate.

Cass Elliot (Mama Cass)

Born Ellen Naomi Cohen (Sept 19, 1941 – July 29, 1974), best known as a member of the folk-rock group The Mamas & the Papas. Born in Baltimore, Maryland; childhood split between Baltimore and Alexandria, Virginia (a DC suburb). Elliot formally joined the group while members vacationed in the U.S. Virgin Islands. The group became central to the Los Angeles Laurel Canyon scene (she was dubbed "Queen of Laurel Canyon"); they closed the 1967 Monterey Pop Festival (Monterey, California). She died in London in 1974; cremated at Hollywood Forever, ashes interred at Mount Sinai Memorial Park (Hollywood Hills, LA). Posthumous Rock and Roll Hall of Fame induction (1998, Cleveland, OH) and a Hollywood Walk of Fame star (2022). Honest assessment: her associations are almost all urban/institutional or private memorial — none is an obvious public-land hide site. The single most plausible public-land lead by association is Monterey, CA (via the festival), but that is more tied to John Phillips than to Elliot. She mainly functions as a Los Angeles / California "Laurel Canyon" pointer.

Jacqueline Roque

(24 Feb 1926, Paris – 15 Oct 1986, Mougins) Picasso's second wife and final muse. He met her in summer 1952 at the Madoura Pottery shop in Vallauris, where she was a salesperson; they lived together from October 1954 and married secretly in the Vallauris village hall in 1961. He painted/drew her 400+ times. Her life was entirely in France (Paris, Golfe-Juan, Cannes/Villa La Californie, Mougins/Notre-Dame-de-Vie); she was buried with Picasso at the Château de Vauvenargues. The chapter's pendant corresponds to the "Profil de Jacqueline" gold medallion conceived in 1956. Honest assessment: Jacqueline offers NO direct US geographic lead — her only US footprint is a single 1980 NYC museum visit. The lone US tendril is by association via photographer David Douglas Duncan → Kansas City, Missouri (the Nelson-Atkins Museum holds 161 of his Picasso/Jacqueline photographs), but there is no evidence tying it to a hide site; treat as low confidence.

Madoura (pottery studio, Vallauris, France)

A ceramics workshop in Vallauris (Côte d'Azur, near Cannes), founded April 29, 1938 by Suzanne Douly and Georges Ramié. The name is an acronym: MAison + DOUly + RAmie. Picasso visited Vallauris in July 1946, met the Ramiés, and began a ~20-year collaboration producing ~4,000 ceramic works; he met Jacqueline there ~1952–53. Etymology worth flagging: Vallauris derives from Latin Vallis Aurea — "Golden Valley" / "Valley of Gold" — tied to its prized clay and 2,000-year pottery heritage. No US town, mountain, or park is named "Madoura" or "Vallauris." Honest assessment: LOW-to-MODERATE, mostly thematic. The most exploitable thread is the "Golden Valley / Valley of Gold" translation (there are several US "Golden Valley" places across CA, NV, AZ, MT), dovetailing with the book's gold motif — but this is unconfirmed and brushes against the no-cipher rule. US museum holdings of Madoura ceramics (Park West Museum, Southfield MI; NSU Art Museum, Fort Lauderdale FL; the Met, NYC) are private institutions, not hide sites.

Pablo Picasso

(1881–1973) Born in Málaga, Spain; lived and worked in France; never lived in the US, so his direct geography is European. The three named works map to US museums: The Old Guitarist (Art Institute of Chicago), Les Demoiselles d'Avignon (MoMA, NY), and Guernica (now Madrid, but held at MoMA 1939–1981). On the pendant: Picasso collaborated with goldsmith François Hugo (from 1955), producing gold medallions (editions of ~20 plus proofs) authorized for sale in 1967, each in a wooden Hugo box; motifs include faces, bulls, dancers, and the profile of Jacqueline. Honest assessment: low direct relevance — every concrete Picasso US location is an urban museum or downtown plaza (Chicago/Daley Plaza, NYC, DC), none a public-land hide site. The Chicago Picasso sculpture was fabricated by U.S. Steel in Gary, Indiana, but neither is wild public land. Treat Picasso as flavor/provenance for the pendant, not a map pin. (Note: Picacho, California is "big peak" in Spanish and is NOT related to the artist.)

Hugo Ateliers / François Hugo (goldsmith)

François Hugo (b. 1899, Rovezzano, Italy; d. 1981) was a leading 20th-century French gold- and silversmith, son of Georges Hugo and great-grandson of Victor Hugo. He worked from a house he built just outside Aix-en-Provence, Provence; the atelier continues through son Pierre and grandson Nicolas Hugo. Picasso and Hugo reconnected on 25 September 1956 via art historian Douglas Cooper. From 1956–1967 Hugo translated Picasso's ceramic designs into precious-metal platters, dishes, statuettes, and medallions via repoussé. There were 24 silver-plate designs (editions of 20 plus proofs); after seeing the plates, Jacqueline suggested making jewelry "for a girl to wear," and Picasso created gold medallions struck in 23k gold (~4.8–5.4 cm) with a hook added so they could be worn as pendants. Picasso kept the gold medallions secret, "as though they constituted some private treasure," authorizing a small numbered sale edition only in 1967. Honest assessment: weak as a direct US lead — firmly rooted in Aix-en-Provence and Rovezzano. The only US anchor is institutional (the Hirshhorn/Smithsonian, DC, holds the 1977 catalogue). The "Hugo" place-name angle (see Signals) runs through Victor Hugo, not the workshop's geography.

Nicolas Hugo (Ateliers Hugo)

A French art dealer and goldsmith (b. c. 1990) who runs Ateliers Hugo, now in its third generation. Founded by grandfather François Hugo, continued by father Pierre. The atelier has operated from the same studio "in the countryside of Aix-en-Provence" since the early 1950s; Nicolas is based between London and Aix-en-Provence. He is the most concrete real-world contact in Chapter 22 — the author bought the pendant from him directly. The workshop has produced editions for Max Ernst, Cocteau, Dalí, Dubuffet, and others. Honest assessment: low direct relevance to a US hide; the person and workshop are French/British with no US footprint. The only US anchor in this object's sale history is Beverly Hills, California (the Feingarten Galleries sold the medallions; collector Gail Feingarten Oppenheimer held a complete set) — a dense urban area, not public land.

The dove (Picasso "Dove of Peace" motif)

Picasso created the original "Dove" lithograph on January 9, 1949, in Fernand Mourlot's Paris studio; it was adopted by Louis Aragon as the emblem of the 1949 First World Peace Congress, becoming a 20th-century peace symbol. (His daughter, born the day before the congress, was named Paloma — Spanish for "dove.") The Chapter 22 hook is biographical: courting Jacqueline, Picasso drew a dove in chalk on her house. The dove is overwhelmingly European in origin with no US natural feature or monument; its only US footholds are museum holdings of the lithograph (MoMA NY; National Gallery of Art, DC; Art Institute of Chicago; Norton Simon Museum, Pasadena). Honest assessment: low/weak — a thematic/biographical motif reinforcing the "love story," not a US location pointer. Generic "Dove"/"Peace" US place-names exist but have no documented link to Picasso's dove.

Virgil (epigraph "Fortune favors the brave")

Virgil (70–19 BC), Roman poet of the Aeneid. The epigraph renders the Latin audentes Fortuna iuvat (Aeneid 10.284, spoken by Turnus); the sentiment predates Virgil (Terence's Phormio, 151 BC) and is widely reused as a motto (several US Navy vessels; a USAF wing patch). The most concrete US geographic angle is the town of Virgil, New York (Cortland County), named directly for the poet within the Central New York Military Tract's classical naming (neighbors Homer, Cicero, Ovid). It borders James Kennedy State Forest (~4,422 acres, public, with marked Finger Lakes / North Country Trail access) and Virgil Mountain (2,132 ft, highest in Cortland County; ~4.7-mi loop from the Odell/Baldwin Rd trailhead). Honest assessment: direct relevance low; association low-confidence. The epigraph is a common motto chosen to set a bravery theme; Virgil, NY satisfies the hard constraints well but is evidence only if another chapter independently points to upstate New York.
