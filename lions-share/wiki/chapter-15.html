1960 Rome Olympic Gold Medal

Lesson: Don't Give Up

Chapter 15 · book pages 133-140

Summary

Chapter 15 opens Part Two of the book and is the author's self-described "favorite story in the book." The featured treasure is a gold medal from the 1960 Rome Olympic Games (XVII Olympiad), bearing the inscription "GIOCHI DELLA XVII OLIMPIADE ROMA MCMLX" and the event word "GINNASTICA" (gymnastics) with a classical figure design. A red-italic sidebar states the medal is in the treasure and notes that both Wilma Rudolph and Cassius Clay (Muhammad Ali) won gold in Rome. The chapter opens with two epigraphs: Harriet Beecher Stowe ("Never give up, for that is just the place and time that the tide will turn") and Wilma Rudolph herself ("I ran and ran and ran every day, and I acquired this sense of determination... I would never, never give up, no matter what else happened"). It briefly frames the Olympics (five interlocking rings = five inhabited continents; first modern Games at Athens, 1896) before turning to Rudolph.

The bulk of the chapter is a biography of Wilma Rudolph. Born in 1940 in the small town of Clarksville, Tennessee, to Blanche Rudolph (a fall induced early labor), she was two months premature at 4.5 pounds and chronically ill (pneumonia twice, measles, mumps, whooping cough, scarlet fever). The family's only affordable doctor was Dr. Coleman, the lone Black doctor in Clarksville, who made house calls. At age five, polio paralyzed her left leg; she was fitted with a hard steel brace and told she might never walk again. For four years, twice a week, she rode the bus an hour, 50 miles to Nashville, to rehabilitate at Meharry Medical School (founded by two Black doctors), undergoing traction, massage, and pool therapy four hours per session. The 20th of 22 children, she was bullied and lonely, calling herself the "gimpy-legged cheerleader." Through sheer will she taught herself to walk without a limp; at age nine she walked into church without her brace; at twelve she removed it permanently, fully recovered.

She then turned to basketball under Coach Clinton Grey, sitting on the bench three years before becoming a nearly six-foot starting guard by 10th grade. Grey formed an informal offseason track team where Wilma won every local race. At her first real meet against trained competition in Tuskegee, Alabama, she lost every race and was devastated, responding with heightened determination. Local referee Ed Temple, who coached track at nearby Tennessee State College and scouted for his AAU developmental program, recruited her. After a year of rigorous training she qualified at the U.S. Olympic trials in Seattle, Washington, at barely sixteen, for the 1956 Melbourne Olympics (200m and 400m relay). Without Ed Temple on the coaching staff, she failed to advance in the 200m but earned a surprise bronze in the 400m relay, then watched 18-year-old Australian Betty Cuthbert win the 200m gold, inspiring her to aim for Rome 1960. She discovered she was pregnant in her senior year; Temple made an exception to his strict no-mothers policy, and weeks after daughter Yolanda was born she enrolled at Tennessee State. She traveled to Texas for the national AAU meet, set a world record in the 200m, and qualified for the 100m, 200m, and 400m relay for 1960.

In Rome, the day before her first meet, while cooling off in sprinklers in 100-degree heat, she landed in a hole and sprained her ankle (it swelled and turned blue); rumors said she'd withdraw. She competed anyway and won gold in the 100m, 200m, and 400m relay, holding world records in all three and becoming the first American woman to win three golds in a single Olympics. The chapter recounts her personal struggles drawn from her autobiography (the brace was "psychologically devastating"; "I lived in mortal fear of being disliked"; she once pulled up to let friend Mae Faggs win at the 1956 trials), and her experiences of racism (mother cleaned white homes; segregated grocery stores and buses; a woman in Hawaii; being spat on; a bus driver refusing to drive the team). Economically she earned $70/week at Tennessee State jobs, couldn't afford childcare, and drove through the night to St. Louis to retrieve Yolanda from her sister Yvonne. Her post-Olympic activism for racial equality led to full racial business integration in Clarksville, TN.

The chapter closes with the author's treasure-hunting reflection — that Wilma "might find all the treasures I have hidden by her sheer will alone... looked everywhere, leaving no stone unturned" — and the explicit lesson that "how much can be accomplished with determination." It then bridges to Chapter 16 via Cassius Clay, who at age 18 also won boxing gold in Rome. Brother Rahman Ali recounts that after being refused service at a restaurant, Ali threw his medal off the Second Street Bridge into the river (historians dispute this, but the medal disappeared). Ali, later diagnosed with Parkinson's in 1984, lit the torch at the 1996 Atlanta Olympics and received a replacement medal — setting up the next chapter on the 1996 Games.

The Treasure(s)

The featured object is a 1960 Rome Olympic gold medal from the Games of the XVII Olympiad. Its bezel reads "GINNASTICA" (Italian for gymnastics) and the medal carries the inscription "GIOCHI DELLA XVII OLIMPIADE ROMA MCMLX," with a classical figure design. A red-italic sidebar in the book confirms: "This is a gold medal from the Rome 1960 Olympic Games that is now in our treasure. Wilma Rudolph and Cassius Clay both won gold medals in Rome."

Research clarifies an important point about provenance: the winner's medals were designed by Giuseppe Cassioli (gilt silver, 69mm, ~102g), and Rome 1960 distinctively seated each medal in a cast bronze olive-leaf wreath bezel engraved with the event/discipline name — here "Ginnastica." However, the gymnastics golds at Rome 1960 were almost entirely won by Soviet and Japanese athletes (the USSR took 15 of 26 gymnastics golds; the US men's team finished 9th). The specific winner of an identical "Ginnastica" medal in the auction market is listed as unknown. So the medal itself is best read as a high-value Olympic collectible pointing to Rome, Italy — not as a pointer to any particular athlete's US hometown. Any US-geography signal in this chapter comes "by association" with the event's celebrated American champions (Wilma Rudolph, Muhammad Ali), not from the medal's own provenance.

Themes & the Lesson

The subtitle and lesson is "Don't Give Up," reinforced by both epigraphs — Stowe's "Never give up... the tide will turn" and Rudolph's own "I would never, never give up." The author uses Wilma Rudolph's life — premature birth, polio, a steel leg brace, poverty, racism, single motherhood, a sprained ankle the day before competition — as the ultimate model of perseverance and determination. He turns this directly into treasure-hunting advice: he says Rudolph could have found "all the treasures I have hidden by her sheer will alone," by "looking everywhere, leaving no stone unturned."

The practical message to the searcher is that locating the treasure requires relentless, thorough, never-quit physical searching ("boots on the ground"), not cleverness alone — "it was her mind, when wielded with purpose, that shaped her destiny." Read against the author's hard constraints, the chapter encourages persistence on accessible public land rather than the hunt for a hidden cipher.

Research Findings

Clarksville, Tennessee

Clarksville is a city in north-central Tennessee on the Cumberland River, near the Kentucky border and Fort Campbell. It is the hometown of Olympic sprinter Wilma Rudolph, born June 23, 1940, in an area then called St. Bethlehem (now part of Clarksville). She won three gold medals at the 1960 Rome Olympics; after her death she was buried at Edgefield Missionary Baptist Church in Clarksville, near the Mason Rudolph golf course. June 23 is "Wilma Rudolph Day" in Tennessee.

Clarksville memorializes Rudolph in public, free-access locations: a life-sized bronze statue (finish-line pose, by local artist Howard Brown) stands at the entrance to Liberty Park outside the Wilma Rudolph Event Center, 1190 Cumberland Drive. Liberty Park is the city's largest park (~146 acres) along the Cumberland River, with a 10-acre fishing pond, the Liberty Nature Walk trail loop (~1.8 to 3 miles), Freedom Point, and the adjacent Clarksville Marina and Cumberland Riverwalk. The park also houses the Pat Head Summitt Legacy Plaza at Freedom Point. Wilma Rudolph Boulevard (named 1994) runs through the city. A permanent Rudolph exhibit is in the "Challenges and Champions Sports Gallery" at the Customs House Museum & Cultural Center, 200 S. Second Street.

The most notable protected public land in Clarksville is Dunbar Cave State Park (~110–144 acres), centered on Dunbar Cave, which contains 30+ Mississippian-era (c. 700–1300 CE) Native American pictographs and petroglyphs rediscovered in 2005. The Mississippians regarded the cave as a sacred portal to the Underworld; Paleo-Indian and Archaic artifacts have also been found there. The park has easy short- and long-loop hiking trails and is a Tennessee State Park (publicly owned/managed).

Wilma Rudolph

Wilma Glodean Rudolph (1940–1994) was an American sprinter who won three gold medals at the 1960 Rome Olympics (100m, 200m, 4×100m relay), becoming the first American woman to win three golds in a single Games. Born June 23, 1940 in the community then called St. Bethlehem, Tennessee (now part of Clarksville), she contracted polio and pneumonia as a child, wore a leg brace, and was treated at Meharry Medical College in Nashville. She attended Cobb Elementary and Burt High School in Clarksville, then Tennessee State University, running for Ed Temple's TSU Tigerbelles. She died November 12, 1994 in Brentwood, TN of a brain tumor and is buried in Clarksville (sources differ between Edgefield Missionary Baptist Church and Foster Memorial Garden Cemetery).

Her memorialization is concentrated in Clarksville and Nashville: a life-sized bronze statue (by Howard Brown) at the Wilma Rudolph Event Center at the entrance to Liberty Park; a section of U.S. Highway 79 renamed Wilma Rudolph Boulevard in 1994; the Customs House Museum exhibit; the Wilma G. Rudolph Residence Center at TSU (1995); and TSU's indoor track named for her in 1980.

Note on the suggested "eternal flame" angle: research could NOT confirm any eternal flame, torch monument, or perpetual flame associated with Wilma Rudolph anywhere. The Liberty Park grouping includes a separate Pat Head Summitt Legacy Plaza at Freedom Point. The "eternal flame" reference appears unverified and should be treated as a likely conflation.

1960 Rome Olympics (XVII Olympiad)

The 1960 Summer Olympics (Games of the XVII Olympiad) were held in Rome, Italy, August 25 to September 11, 1960 — widely called the first "modern" (fully televised) Olympics. The winner's medal was designed by Giuseppe Cassioli: gilt silver, 69mm, ~102g, with a victorious athlete on the obverse and a seated Victory with the Colosseum and "GIOCHI DELLA XVII OLIMPIADE ROMA MCMLX" on the reverse. Distinctively, each medal sat in a cast bronze olive-leaf wreath bezel engraved with the event name. The Chapter 15 treasure item's bezel reads "GINNASTICA" (gymnastics), confirming the book's medal is a gymnastics gold.

The gymnastics medal almost certainly carries NO famous-American provenance. Of the 26 gymnastics golds at Rome 1960, the Soviet Union won 15; the dominant individual champions were Soviet (Boris Shakhlin, Larisa Latynina) and Japanese (Takashi Ono). The US men's gymnastics team finished 9th, and the RR Auction listing for an identical "Ginnastica" gold lists the winner/provenance as unknown. The medal is best read as a generic high-value Olympic collectible, not a pointer to a particular athlete's hometown.

The US-geography of Rome 1960 comes instead from its celebrated American champions (clues "by association," if any): Wilma Rudolph (3 track golds; Clarksville/St. Bethlehem, TN; TSU Tigerbelles, Nashville), Cassius Clay / Muhammad Ali (boxing gold; Louisville, KY), and Rafer Johnson (decathlon gold; Kingsburg, California; UCLA). These are track/boxing/decathlon stars, NOT gymnasts, so they are only thematically — not by provenance — linked to the chapter's gymnastics medal.

Meharry Medical College, Nashville, Tennessee

Meharry Medical College was founded in 1876 in Nashville as the Medical Department of Central Tennessee College, the first medical school for African Americans in the South. Its first classes met in the basement of the Clark Memorial Methodist Episcopal Church; the "Meharry" name honors Samuel Meharry and four brothers, Irish-American salt traders who donated ~$15,000 in 1875. Chartered independently in 1915, it is today the largest private historically Black institution in the US dedicated solely to health-care education. Its campus is at 1005 Dr. D.B. Todd Jr. Blvd., North Nashville (~36.1671, -86.8062), affiliated with Nashville General Hospital.

The treasure-hunt relevance is via Wilma Rudolph: as a child in Clarksville she contracted polio, and because medical care for Black residents was scarce, she and her mother traveled roughly 50 miles weekly to Meharry, where African-American doctors fitted her with a leg brace and prescribed massage and heat treatments. This rehabilitation enabled her to walk unaided by about age 12 and is repeatedly cited as the turning point in her recovery.

Tennessee State University (Tennessee State College / Tennessee A&I State) and the Tigerbelles

Tennessee State University (TSU) is a public historically Black university in Nashville. Founded in 1912, its women's track program — the "Tigerbelles" — became one of the most dominant programs in Olympic history under head coach Edward S. "Ed" Temple, producing ~40 Olympians and roughly 13 gold, 6 silver, and 4 bronze medals. Wilma Rudolph was discovered by Temple at age 14, trained in his TSU summer program, enrolled in 1958, and at Rome 1960 became the first American woman to win three golds in a single Games.

TSU's main campus sits in west Nashville along John A. Merritt Boulevard, intersected by an "Ed Temple Boulevard." The campus features an Olympic Plaza with a statue in Rudolph's running form holding an Olympic torch and a TSU diploma. An Edward Stanley Temple historical marker stands at John A. Merritt Blvd and Ed Temple Blvd. A separate 9-foot bronze statue of Temple was unveiled in 2015 at First Tennessee Park (now First Horizon Park) downtown. The strongest natural/public-land anchors near these sites are the Cumberland River, the Cumberland Riverwalk, and the Upland Trail in Clarksville. TSU itself is urban, with no obvious wilderness hide site on campus.

Tuskegee, Alabama

Tuskegee is a city in Macon County, Alabama, ~40 miles east of Montgomery, home of Tuskegee Institute (now Tuskegee University), founded in 1881 by Booker T. Washington and later home to George Washington Carver. It was the site of Rudolph's first major track meet: as a sophomore she raced at the Tuskegee Relays, lost, and was motivated by that defeat. The Tuskegee Relays (founded 1927 by coach Cleve "Major" Abbott) were the oldest African American relay meet and for years the third-largest US track event.

Tuskegee anchors an unusually dense cluster of protected/public sites: two National Park Service units (Tuskegee Institute National Historic Site and the Tuskegee Airmen National Historic Site at Moton Field) and the Tuskegee National Forest — the smallest national forest in the US (~11,000 acres, entirely within Macon County), containing the Bartram National Recreation Trail (named for explorer William Bartram), the Pleasant Hill Trail, the Bartram Botanical Area, and Choctafaula Creek.

Ed Temple

Edward Stanley Temple (1927–2016) was the women's track coach at Tennessee State University in Nashville from 1950 to 1994 (44 years). Born in Harrisburg, Pennsylvania, he built the Tigerbelles dynasty (40 Olympians, 23 medals, 34 national titles) and was U.S. Olympic women's head track coach in 1960 (Rome) and 1964 (Tokyo). He scouted and coached Wilma Rudolph, directly anchoring Chapter 15's Rome 1960 story to Nashville. He is heavily memorialized in Nashville: a 9-foot bronze statue along a greenway near First Horizon Park (connecting the Cumberland River Greenway to the Bicentennial Mall Greenway), the TSU Olympic Plaza statue, and the Ed Temple Track (1977) on campus.

The most hunt-relevant public-land tie under the author's constraints: the Bicentennial Mall Greenway connects directly to Bicentennial Capitol Mall State Park in Nashville — a genuine Tennessee state park (public land) adjacent to the First Horizon Park Temple statue and within easy walking distance of roads. Note that campus statues and the named track sit on university property, weaker candidates than the adjoining state park / greenway corridor.

Seattle, Washington

Seattle appears in Chapter 15 via the widely-repeated claim that at sixteen Rudolph attended the "1956 U.S. Olympic track and field team trials in Seattle, Washington" and qualified for Melbourne. IMPORTANT FACTUAL DISCREPANCY: this detail is almost certainly an error propagated through Rudolph biographies. The authoritative record shows NO part of the 1956 trials in Seattle — the 1956 women's track-and-field Olympic trials were held at American University (Reeves Field) in Washington, D.C. on August 25, 1956 (men's trials at the Los Angeles Memorial Coliseum). Seattle does not appear in the primary trials record, so the chapter's "Seattle" association rests on a likely-inaccurate biographical detail rather than a verifiable event there.
