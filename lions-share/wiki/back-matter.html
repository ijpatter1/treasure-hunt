Back Matter — Postscript, Confirmed Rules & Back-Cover Poem

Lesson: The Author's Rules of the Hunt

Back matter · book pages 199-211

Summary

The back matter (book pages 199-211) bundles together four distinct, highly clue-dense sections: (1) "Some More of Our Treasure Items," a gallery of eight additional treasure objects not given their own chapters; (2) "Joy's Serenade," a 7-stanza poem that the repository flags as the single most important navigational clue for the Lion's Share; (3) the Postscript, which lays out the author's literal, non-coded rules of the hunt; and (4) the closing quotes, acknowledgements, author-bio references, and the back-cover logo.

The additional-treasures gallery (pp. 199-204) catalogs: a 116.7-carat pear-cut Tanzanite (discovered 1967 in Tanzania, made the December birthstone in 2002, deposits expected mined out around 2043, valued $3,000+/carat, called perfect for a "dazzling pendant"); a 9.65-carat near-perfect octahedral natural diamond (uncut, vetted by mineral dealer Rob Lavinsky); two 19th-century gold/emerald/ruby "floral" rings from South India (Tuyet Nguyet collection); two ornate Bali priest rings (19th-20th c., bird's-beak prongs, one with a large pink ruby, Tuyet Nguyet); a Southeast Asian gold box carved with a deer on the front and a chicken on the back (one-of-a-kind); a 351.65-gram, 98%-pure Australian gold nugget the author calls "one of my favorite gold pieces"; a titanium physical Bitcoin coin minted by BTCC in 2016 (one of only 1,350, private keys for 1 BTC under the hologram); an olive-green Chinese jade bracelet dated 3000-4000 BC ("jingqi"/life force, tied to five Confucian virtues); a jade-and-gold amulet necklace from the Nicoya civilization of Costa Rica (200-600 AD, jade believed to protect the wearer and mediate with the spiritual world); a one-of-a-kind citrine-and-diamond necklace by Italian designer (and trained operatic tenor) Michele della Valle; and a 9th-century Viking gold ring whose motif is "dozens of circles stamped into eight serpentine forms." The Viking entry carries two pointed authorial asides: that the Vikings, not the Spanish, were the first to sail to North America, and that J.R.R. Tolkien's Lord of the Rings (a "favorite childhood book") was inspired by the Norse myth of Andvari's ring — which the author recommends reading.

"Joy's Serenade" (pp. 205-206) is the chapter's navigational centerpiece. Across seven stanzas it frames the hunt as music ("we may find music in a poem"), promises directional guidance from a "conductor" with a "baton," and then issues a string of place-evocative images: "Use will's straight edge, as the turning square or any arc may align at a proper point"; the explicit assurance "This is no imaginary wonderland. Now look"; "an X leads the way / or simply follow shimmering circles of gold"; "There may be magic in the water, but the pike are rather all around"; "If you get warm, you may find shady oaks"; "the haiku curls a little further on / where few have seen it"; "if you don't go down you may never know how high you ever want to be"; "The sky smiles on you. / Dancers are on the land"; "the flame of starlight"; and "sat upon a rock, / allowing nature's wide embrace." These images strongly imply a real outdoor location with oaks, water, a rock, elevation/a high vantage, and possibly named features ("pike," "Dancers," "haiku," "circles of gold").

The Postscript (pp. 207-208) is the rulebook and is explicitly the opposite of the poem: "There is no subterfuge... no double meanings, no misdirection, no innuendo, no subtext, no clues, no code. Take what I say here as literally as you possibly can." It confirms FIVE total boxes; none in dangerous places; none under water (no raft/canoe needed, none near swift current); none requiring scaling a cliff or rock face; do NOT search in winter snow; "No treasure is hidden more than three miles from any road"; NO box on private property (a strict rule) and no need to walk through private land (sole exception: a public-easement trail crossing private land); nothing buried and no digging needed (which could anger a park ranger). It advises hunting-season awareness/bright clothing, searching with a companion, carrying a phone, and researching wildlife (bears, poisonous snakes, buffalo, moose). Critically, it states the author gave NO clues to family, friends, or anyone named in the book — "they can't help you, even if they tried."

The closing pages (pp. 209-211 + back cover) carry three epigraph quotes — Eddie Cantor ("Slow down and enjoy life... you also miss the sense of where you are going and why"), Mother Teresa ("Profound joy of the heart is like a magnet that indicates the path of life"), and Maya Angelou ("We need joy as we need air... the earth we share") — followed by acknowledgements. The thanked include researchers (Ileana, Rosemarie, Thaddeus, Valeria, Zoe), photographer Jamie Biver, creators "Seth, Angie, and Lauren," mineral expert Rob Lavinsky, antiquities experts Allan Anawati and Nicolas Hugo (great-great-grandson of Victor Hugo, source of the Picasso pendant), publisher Susan Szecsi, Brian and Bryce, Yohanna ("for helping me locate my north star"), editor Don ("your guidance gave me the surety to mark a path"... "steer this ship so that it did not run aground"), designer Richard, the Baker & Taylor team (Jeff, Mark, Matt), the author's Mom and Dad, Zach, and — most clue-relevant — LeElaine and Kimberly D., thanked "for showing me how much ground one searcher can cover in a single day." Finally come children Aiden and Londyn and wife Kimberly. The back cover shows a red circle containing a crossed hammer and pickaxe (mining/prospecting logo) on a black field.

Places & Geographies

The back matter names no place outright — but the poem's imagery and the Viking/Andvari asides open a wide map of candidate US public lands, all developed in Research Findings below. Surfacing every concrete US location surfaced by research:

  • Pikes Peak & Pike National Forest, Colorado (El Paso / Teller counties, west of Colorado Springs) — the most semantically direct read of "the pike are rather all around"; a summit road and 225+ miles of trail keep it well inside the "within 3 miles of a road, not dangerous" rules. Strongest single geographic lead from this section.
  • Garden of the Gods, Manitou Springs, Red Rock Canyon Open Space, Palmer Park (Colorado Springs, CO) — the matching oak/water/rock/elevation cluster: famous sitting rocks, effervescent ("magic") mineral springs, Gambel-oak shade, dark-sky stargazing.
  • Pando ("The Trembling Giant"), Fishlake National Forest, Utah (near Fish Lake, off State Hwy 25 / FR 1483) — the most literal embodiment of "shimmering circles of gold" (a 106-acre aspen clone); public land, road-accessible, no digging.
  • Aspen country generally — Colorado & Utah high country (5,000-12,000 ft) — the botanical read of "circles of gold" leans Western and is effectively absent from the failed NC foothills.
  • Pike State Forest (Ohio, Pike County); Pike Lake State Park (Bainbridge, OH); Pike State Forest (Indiana); Pike County, Pennsylvania (17,860 acres of Delaware Water Gap NRA, oak ridges) — clean literal "Pike"-named public-land alternatives.
  • Northern-tier lake states (Wisconsin, Michigan, Minnesota, northern Indiana, New York/New England, Upper Mississippi) — if "pike" is read as the fish, native northern-pike range steers north rather than south.
  • Long X Trail, Theodore Roosevelt National Park North Unit / Little Missouri National Grassland, near Watford City, North Dakota — the literal "X" trail; flagged as a likely red herring (no aspen).
  • Heavener Runestone Park, Le Flore County, Oklahoma (Poteau Mountain, Ouachita foothills) — the cleanest US "Norse" site for the Andvari/serpentine-rune aside: a carved rune read as "Glome's Valley," on public land, reachable by a short park road.

  • Leif Erikson Park, Duluth, MN; Shilshole Bay, Ballard/Seattle, WA; Commonwealth Ave., Boston, MA — accessible public Leif Erikson sites (statue-park options, not museum interiors).
  • Death Valley NP "Racetrack Playa" (Inyo County, CA); Stronghold Table, Badlands NP, SD; Dancing Rock Preserve, Klickitat Co., WA; Dancing Prairie Preserve, MT — "Dancers are on the land" name-matches; all fail either the public-land or the 3-mile-from-road rule, so deprioritized.

Themes & the Lesson

"The Author's Rules of the Hunt" is the book's hard boundary. Where the rest of the book hides clues subtly, the Postscript deliberately strips all artifice: "There is no subterfuge... no double meanings, no misdirection, no innuendo, no subtext, no clues, no code," and the reader should "take what I say here as literally as you possibly can." Its purpose is to constrain the search realistically and safely.

The confirmed constraints function as a validation filter for any location hypothesis. A candidate site can be ruled out if it violates any of them: five boxes total, one per state; each on accessible PUBLIC land (never private, with the sole exception of a public-easement trail crossing private land); within three miles of a road; never buried (no digging); never underwater (no raft/canoe, no swift current); never requiring a cliff or rock-face climb; not searched when snow is on the ground. The companion epigraphs (Cantor, Mother Teresa, Angelou) reinforce the "joy as compass" theme that ties the whole book together, and "Joy's Serenade" is presented as the actual navigational poem the rules are meant to safely guide the reader through.

The crucial corollary: the author gave no clues to anyone named in the book — "they can't help you, even if they tried" — and reporting indicates he kept the hiding spots secret even from his wife. That means the acknowledgement names (LeElaine, Kimberly D., Yohanna, Don, etc.) should be treated as constraint-confirming color, not as encoded leads.

Poem / Epigraph

JOY'S SERENADE A note, the molten tone of a tune, rests inside all of us who want a song to follow, a treasure to hold; we may find music in a poem. Our conductor, as with a trace of a baton through the air, gives direction. While the coda is incomplete, there's a flutter of excitement around. See how far to go. Use will's straight edge, as the turning square or any arc may align at a proper point. Never despair, never doubt. This is no imaginary wonderland. Now look. You can take your own path an X leads the way or simply follow shimmering circles of gold. There may be magic in the water, but the pike are rather all around. If you get warm, you may find shady oaks. When close, the haiku curls a little further on where few have seen it. And if you don't go down you may never know how high you ever want to be. The sky smiles on you. Dancers are on the land. And if you stay to see the night it will ignite with the flame of starlight. Now that you're here all that is left remains for you. And if you spy others warming with the sunlight, or sat upon a rock, allowing nature's wide embrace to sustain their bliss, maybe ask them to assist you. You can sing harmonies as you travel home.

Research Findings

"Joy's Serenade" poem imagery (oaks, water, rock, high vantage)

"Joy's Serenade" is the closing "Final Poem" of the book and is widely treated by the hunting community as the master navigational poem for the Lion's Share. Its literal landscape images form a tight, six-part geographic profile: oak trees ("shady oaks"), a water feature ("magic in the water"), a sitting rock ("sat upon a rock"), peaked terrain ("the pike... all around"), elevation ("don't go down" / "how high"), and a clear-sky/stargazing vantage ("flame of starlight"). A self-referential embedded haiku ("the haiku curls a little further on / where few have seen it") points to the dust-jacket near-haiku ("Be solid, have grit; / sparkle even as you pine. / Here lies a joy divined"). "This is no imaginary wonderland" instructs that the images describe a real place. The community has NOT converged: advocates exist for Michigan/Lake Michigan, Lake Tahoe, the Olympic Peninsula (WA), the Great Smoky Mountains (NC), and the Black Hills (SD). This repository's phase-2 analysis instead builds a Colorado Springs / Pikes Peak case ("pike" = Pikes Peak, "magic in the water" = Manitou Springs, "shady oaks" = Gambel oak, "sat upon a rock" = Garden of the Gods, "circles of gold" = quaking aspen). None is author-confirmed. SPECULATION flagged: this generic imagery profile matches many US public lands — it failed to pinpoint Rocky Face NC — so the poem is best used as a literal checklist to screen candidates, weighted most where an independent non-cipher clue corroborates the same geography.

"the pike are rather all around"

Verified stanza-4 text (repo chapter file): "You can take your own path / an X leads the way / or simply follow shimmering circles of gold. / There may be magic in the water, but the pike / are rather all around." The load-bearing word "pike" has four real, non-cipher US readings: (1) PIKE = PEAK — in Lake District dialect a "pike" is a sharp-topped hill; the famous US echo is Pikes Peak (14,115 ft) inside Pike National Forest, west of Colorado Springs, where "pike all around" reads as Pike-named geography literally surrounding you; a summit road and 225+ miles of trail satisfy the access rules. (2) PIKE = FISH — northern pike's native range is the northern tier, arguing for a northern lake/river state and against the Southern Appalachians. (3) PIKE = PLACE-NAME — Pike State Forest (OH/IN), Pike Lake State Park (OH), Pike County, PA. (4) PIKE = TURNPIKE/ROAD — weakest. Fresh finding: the Forrest Fenn box was confirmed in Wyoming, NOT Colorado — so the one-box-per-state objection no longer blocks a Colorado Lion's Share, REOPENING Pikes Peak. CORRECTION: an earlier note here claimed "Joy's Serenade IS Part 2" and used a secondhand quote ("no information in the poem or related to Part 2") to call every poem-driven solve invalid. That is false. Per the book, Part Two is the four-chapter section dedicated to the four smaller boxes (p.16-17: "the four chapters in Part Two contain primarily all you need to discover the location of the other four treasure boxes"); Joy's Serenade is the Final Poem in the back matter / Postscript (pp.205-206), entirely outside Part Two. The poem is a primary clue vehicle for the Lion's Share — the largest box's clues "may be found anywhere inside this book," and a separate author attribution calls the poem "pretty important." Treat the poem as valid.

"Dancers are on the land"

Line 2 of stanza 6. The author has said he does not think there is hidden information in the poem itself, so its weight as a geographic pointer is uncertain. The most direct in-book "dancers" reference is the page-61 illustration (Ch. 5, Art Smith), depicting stage/modern-dance performers — Art Smith made jewelry for choreographer Talley Beatty's dancers (Beatty worked with Duke Ellington). The dedicated community analyses reach the same conclusion: they tie the line only to that NYC dance illustration and offer no real-world place name. Treated as a possible named-place pointer, the strongest real US name-matches are "Dancing Rocks" (the sailing stones of Racetrack Playa, Death Valley NP, CA), Stronghold Table (Badlands NP, SD, last Lakota Ghost Dance site), Dancing Rock Preserve (Klickitat Co., WA), and Dancing Prairie Preserve (MT). LOW confidence: none is named in the book. Against the hard rules, Stronghold Table fails (no roads, foot-access only, crosses private land); Racetrack Playa is a ranger-patrolled playa >3 miles from a maintained road; the WA/MT "Dancing" preserves are private conservancy land. I found no feature that both matches the line AND satisfies the rules — the defensible reading keeps the phrase symbolic, tied to the Art Smith dancers.

"shimmering circles of gold" + "an X leads the way"

Grammar matters: the lines present two ALTERNATIVE ways to navigate ("you can take your own path... OR simply follow..."), so this is trail/landscape navigation imagery, not a buried-X. "Shimmering circles of gold" reads most literally as the quaking aspen (Populus tremuloides) — round leaves on flattened petioles that tremble/"shimmer" and turn brilliant gold in fall. Aspen is concentrated in the Western US (Utah and Colorado heartland, 5,000-12,000 ft) and is effectively absent from the low NC foothills where the prior search FAILED. The single most famous aspen feature is Pando ("The Trembling Giant"), a 106-acre clonal organism in Fishlake National Forest, Utah — visible from State Hwy 25, public, no digging. "An X leads the way" most literally means a trail-junction/crossing or an X-shaped blaze; the one notable US public-land trail literally named with an X is the Long X Trail (Theodore Roosevelt NP North Unit / Little Missouri National Grassland, near Watford City, ND). SUPPORTIVE / region-narrowing, not a pinpoint: aspen leans Western and corroborates the repo note that this line "actively fails in NC." Pando is a clean constraint-fit worth testing against the other poem lines, but there is NO direct evidence the box is there. The Long X Trail conflicts with the aspen read (ND badlands lack aspen) and is likely a red herring. No letter/syllable games, per the author's literal rule.

Viking myth of Andvari's ring / J.R.R. Tolkien's Lord of the Rings

Andvari is a Norse dwarf/shapeshifter (often a pike/fish) who crafted the magic ring Andvaranaut and cursed his hoard; the curse passes through Hreidmar, the dragon Fafnir, Sigurd, Brynhild, and Gudrun. The earliest depictions are the Swedish Sigurd stones (e.g., Ramsund), where the dragon's body coils as a runic "serpentine" band — the visual source of the ring's "eight serpentine forms" motif. Tolkien reworked this material; the One Ring widely echoes Andvaranaut. So the author's two asides point at one motif: cursed gold guarded by a serpent-dragon in a valley. No US town/landmark is named Andvari, Fafnir, Sigurd, or Volsung. The only concrete US geography tied to the broader Norse-rune theme: Heavener Runestone Park, OK — a carved Norse rune on Poteau Mountain read as "Glome's Valley," on PUBLIC land, reachable by a short park road (satisfies the hard rules); the "serpentine forms" cue maps onto Sigurd-stone iconography (check for serpentine rock, switchbacks, a creek/valley). Also Kensington Runestone, MN. THEMATIC, not a coordinate: the Heavener stone has no genuine Viking provenance; Tolkien's real-world landscape inspiration was Switzerland, not the US. Treat Heavener as plausible-but-unconfirmed and cross-reference other chapters for any independent Oklahoma/Ouachita or "valley/ring/serpent/dragon" hit before weighting it.

Viking "first to sail to North America" aside

Reflects the established fact that Norse explorers (Leif Erikson) reached North America around 1000 CE and named it "Vinland." But the only archaeologically confirmed Norse site, L'Anse aux Meadows, is in Canada, not the US — weakening any literal US pointer. Within the US the only genuine Norse artifact is the Maine Penny (Goddard coin), an 11th-c. Norwegian coin found at the Goddard Site, Naskeag Point, Brooklin, Maine (now in the Maine State Museum, Augusta). Famous US "Viking" claims (Kensington Runestone, MN; Newport Tower, RI) are widely considered hoaxes/colonial. The legacy survives in Leif Erikson statues/parks (Boston; St. Paul/Duluth, MN; Shilshole Bay/Seattle). Likely THEMATIC reinforcement, not a location: the "north" association may just echo the book's "locate my north star" / high-and-north motif. SPECULATION if read as a place-nudge: best candidates are the Maine Penny region (Blue Hill peninsula, near Acadia NP) or accessible Leif Erikson parks (Duluth, Seattle, Boston) — museum interiors are lore-only. None has strong independent corroboration; treat as a low-confidence theme cue.

Postscript hard rules (3 miles from road, public land, not buried)

Verified across multiple secondary sources reporting the author's own statements. The hard constraints: (1) Distance — "There is no treasure hidden more than three miles from any road"; reaching a box should take "no more than a couple of hours," usually far less. (2) Land — none on or across private property; all on open/public land. (3) Not buried — "treasure boxes are NOT BURIED — they are hidden"; no shovel or metal detector. (4) Safety — all safe; never wade/cross high water; no boats, climbing, or special gear. (5) State rule — one box per state. (6) Environment — all outdoors (not inside structures); do not search in snow. Critically: a searcher "does not have to be a genius or solve any grand cipher," directly supporting the literal-clue directive (a deliberate, already-solved cryptogram and word-search exist as set pieces, not as a requirement). These rules are a validation FILTER, not a directional clue: eliminate any candidate that violates one. SPECULATION flagged: the not-buried/near-road/no-snow profile favors a durable surface cache (rock ledge, crevice, base of a landmark tree) on National Forest / state-park / BLM land. The one-box-per-state rule means a confirmed NC box anywhere else would exclude NC for the Lion's Share.
