Antiquities of Alexander

Lesson: Make It Make Sense

Chapter 21 · book pages 179-188

Summary

Chapter 21, "Antiquities of Alexander," opens with a Baroque painting of the Apotheosis of Hercules and an Alan Watts epigraph: "A myth is an image in terms of which we try to make sense of the world." The author frames the chapter around his children's endless questions and humanity's universal urge to explain the world, noting that cultures throughout history have used mythology to fill gaps in understanding, and that Greek myth in particular served politics and power as much as explanation.

The narrative then turns to Alexander the Great, first-born son of Philip II of Macedonia. After Philip II was murdered by a rival, Alexander assumed the crown in 336 BC, ruled thirteen years until his death at age 32, conquered roughly 3,000 miles from Macedonia to India, and renamed more than twenty cities "Alexandria." His mother Olympias fueled his god-complex by claiming his birth was a virgin conception by Zeus. Alexander's conquests flooded Greece with gold, spurring a goldsmithing boom. The author acquired four gold antiquities from this era for the treasure, each "steeped in legends."

Each treasure object is presented with its mythological symbolism. A Greek gold wreath of solid olive and oak leaves ties to the inaugural Olympic Games of 776 BC, to the Apollo/Daphne laurel myth and the origin of "Poet Laureate," and to Zeus (priests heard the god in rustling oak leaves). A matching pair of gold ibex-headed bracelets carries a "couple's provenance" of famous collectors and galleries and ties to Dionysus, his Maenad followers, Pan, the cult of Aphrodite, and the Zeus-Amalthea she-goat origin myth. An Egyptian gold snake bracelet from around Cleopatra's reign traces Alexander's conquest of Egypt and the Ptolemaic lineage; the author jokes that a similar bracelet in the Getty Museum in Los Angeles is, per his wife Kimberly, "not as stylish." An amethyst and gold beaded necklace bears a Club of Hercules pendant tied to the twelve labors, the Nemean lion, and the amethyst origin myth.

Crucially, the author then delivers an explicit treasure hint: a chapter titled "Make It Make Sense" "might also be a reference to figuring out the solution for the location of the treasure I have hidden," and "you might have to look beyond just ancient myths and legends and consider more." He pivots to modern "myths," debunking minor ones (we use only 10% of our brains; mice prefer cheese) before attacking the harmful myth that race, religion, culture, or political affiliation make people fundamentally different. He argues "we and our enemies, if we have them, are mostly the same," warns his children against the "mirage" of tribal group-thinking, and urges empathy and listening. The chapter closes with "Here is where love resides in all of us" and a transition — "Our next chapter is a love story" (Picasso's Pendant) — beside an aerial photo of people forming a heart on a brick/stone surface.

The Treasure(s)

The author describes acquiring four gold antiquities of Alexander's era for the treasure, each "steeped in legends":

  • Greek gold wreath of solid gold olive and oak leaves (leaves created 3rd–4th c. BC; the leaves are extremely rare and the band was constructed later to present how the wreath might have originally looked). Wreaths trace to the inaugural Olympic Games of 776 BC; the laurel ties to the Apollo/Daphne myth and the origin of "Poet Laureate"; olive and oak tie to Zeus. Gold wreaths were marks of honor, thought to cure hangovers, and worn to the grave for their "immortal" qualities.
  • Matching pair of gold ibex-headed bracelets (ancient Greece, 3rd–4th c. BC) whose "couple's provenance" lists famous collectors and galleries. The ibex/goat ties to Dionysus (fertility, wine, pleasure), his Maenads, Pan, the cult of Aphrodite, and the Zeus-Amalthea she-goat origin myth.
  • Egyptian gold snake bracelet (100 BC–100 AD), made around Cleopatra's reign. A similar bracelet sits in the Getty Museum in Los Angeles. Snakes symbolized healing (the snake-rod of Asklepios, today the American Medical Association symbol).
  • Amethyst and gold beaded necklace with a Club of Hercules pendant (2nd–3rd c. BC), symbolizing health and protection. The Hercules myth (strangling snakes as an infant, the twelve labors, killing the Nemean lion with an olive-wood club) and the amethyst origin myth (Dionysus, a maiden turned to quartz, his tears coloring it purple) are recounted.

People

Alexander the Great (356–323 BC) — King of Macedonia from 336 BC; conquered roughly 3,000 miles from Macedonia to India in about thirteen years before dying at age 32. He founded or renamed more than twenty cities "Alexandria," most famously Alexandria, Egypt (331 BC). His conquests flooded Greece with gold and spurred the Hellenistic goldsmithing boom behind the chapter's four antiquities. A critical caution for this hunt: nearly all US "Alexander"/"Alexandria" place names honor unrelated settlers and politicians, not the conqueror.

Cleopatra and Ptolemy — Ptolemy I Soter, a Macedonian general and one of Alexander's most trusted companions, took control of Egypt after Alexander's death in 323 BC and founded the Ptolemaic dynasty centered on Alexandria. That lineage ruled ~300 years until Cleopatra VII, the last Ptolemaic ruler, died in 30 BC. This is the direct provenance era of the chapter's Egyptian gold snake bracelet; snake bangles became iconically associated with Cleopatra.

Dionysus (with Pan, the Maenads, and the cult of Aphrodite) — The Greek god of wine and fertility whose sacred animal is the goat/ibex; the mythological cluster behind the ibex-headed bracelets and the amethyst's sobriety symbolism. The author signals this is "myth" to look past.

Alan Watts — Author of the chapter epigraph on myth. Watts lived and worked in California (Druid Heights / Sausalito), a possible "beyond the myth" biographical thread.

Kimberly — The author's wife, name-checked in the joke that the Getty's snake bracelet is "not as stylish" as the one in the treasure. A recurring family reference across chapters, including The Six-Figure Birthstone and Picasso's Pendant.

J. Paul Getty (1892–1976) — Oil tycoon and founder of the museum the chapter names. Born in Minneapolis, Minnesota; family moved to Bartlesville, Oklahoma around 1903; he made his first oil fortune near Tulsa, Oklahoma by 1916, then relocated to California. Notably, he never visited the completed Getty Villa.

Themes & the Lesson

"Make It Make Sense" works on two levels. On the surface it is about the human urge to explain the world through myth and the author's warning that convenient explanations — ancient or modern — can be false or harmful, with the moral being to seek genuine understanding and reject tribal group-think. But the author explicitly converts the lesson into a literal treasure instruction: the chapter title "might also be a reference to figuring out the solution for the location of the treasure," the chapter "can help you do just that," and "you might have to look beyond just ancient myths and legends and consider more."

The clear nudge is to apply logic to the solution and to look past the Greek mythology toward the items' real-world provenance and other concrete details — exactly the kind of "beyond the myth" move that points at named, visitable institutions (the Getty, the Met) and real US geology and place names rather than allegory. Consistent with the author's stated rules, the clues are literal: there is no cipher, so no number games (776, the Star of Life's six points) or letter puzzles should be read into the chapter.

Research Findings

Getty Museum (J. Paul Getty Museum / Getty Villa)

The J. Paul Getty Museum operates two LA campuses founded by oil tycoon J. Paul Getty. The Getty Villa — the campus relevant to this chapter — is dedicated to ancient Greece, Rome, and Etruria. Getty bought a 64-acre Malibu parcel in 1945, broke ground in 1970, and the Villa opened in 1974, modeled on the Villa of the Papyri at Herculaneum; Getty died in England in 1976 and never visited it. The Getty holds a Romano-Egyptian gold "Snake Bracelet" (1st century AD) and a "Collection of Ptolemaic Jewelry" (Greek, c. 225–175 BC) including coiled-snake bracelets — believed made by Greek goldsmiths in Alexandria, Egypt, directly tying the chapter title to Alexander (who conquered Egypt in 332 BC) and the Ptolemaic dynasty. Getty's US biography: born Minneapolis, MN (1892); boyhood Bartlesville, OK (~1903); first oil fortune near Tulsa, OK by 1916; relocated to California.

Hellenistic provenance of the gold ibex-headed bracelets — famous collectors & galleries

The "gold ibex-headed bracelets" belong to a well-attested class of Hellenistic/Ptolemaic gold jewelry (animal-head terminals once called "gazelle," now read as ibex), made by Greek goldsmiths in Alexandria after Alexander's conquests. (The book describes the pair as sharing a "couple's provenance" — a list of famous collectors and galleries — not a "Coptic" one; these are Hellenistic-Ptolemaic, predating the Coptic Christian era.) Two US-museum holdings dominate the documented provenance trail. The Getty's "Collection of Ptolemaic Jewelry (16)" is the closest match to the book's four-item group (ibex-head finials + coiled-snake bracelets + a Herakles-knot diadem), with provenance through American collectors Barbara and Lawrence Fleischman (acquired 1990, sold to the Getty 1992), touring the Getty, the Cleveland Museum of Art (1995), and the Metropolitan Museum of Art (2016). The Metropolitan Museum of Art holds the "Ganymede jewelry" parure with animal-head bracelets (found near Thessaloniki, to the Met via the Harris Brisbane Dick Fund) and a separate Eretria bracelet. No specific celebrity-collector ibex-bracelet sale was reliably documented.

Olympic Games (ancient, 776 BC) and the gold/olive wreath tradition

The ancient Olympic Games are traditionally dated to 776 BC, held every four years at Olympia, Greece, in honor of Zeus. The sole prize was the kotinos, a wreath of wild-olive leaves cut with a golden sickle from a sacred tree and woven into a circle symbolizing unity and peace. The chapter title links to the wreath through Macedonia: Alexander's father Philip II was a three-time Olympic victor and built the Philippeion at Olympia, and gold funerary wreaths (e.g., from the Vergina tomb attributed to Philip II) surged in the Hellenistic age as Alexander's conquests flooded Greece with gold. The Olympic theme recurs across the book (Ch. 15 Rome medal, Ch. 16 Atlanta medal). The strongest constraint-compliant US name cluster is Washington State's Olympic National Park / Forest and Mount Olympus — genuine accessible public land.

Asklepios and the Rod of Asclepius (AMA symbol)

The Rod of Asclepius — a single serpent coiled around a wooden staff — is the ancient Greco-Roman emblem of the healing god Asclepius, son of Apollo, whose chief sanctuary was at Epidaurus. The American connection is institutional and modern: the American Medical Association adopted the rod as its insignia in 1910 (the AMA was founded in Philadelphia in 1847, incorporated in Illinois 1897, and is headquartered in Chicago). The rod also sits at the center of the "Star of Life" on US ambulances, registered by NHTSA in 1977. No naturally occurring US place named for Asclepius surfaced — this is a symbol/theme, not a location. Per the no-cipher rule, no number (six points) or letter games are read into it.

Club of Hercules / Nemean lion / olive-wood club

In Greek myth, Heracles armed himself with a club hewn from a wild olive tree to confront the Nemean lion (his first labor), stunning it before strangling it and wearing its impenetrable pelt. The olive-wood club + lion skin became Hercules' standard iconography; the lion gives its name to the constellation Leo. "Club of Hercules" pendants are a real ancient jewelry form — Hellenistic amethyst-and-gold necklaces with a central Hercules-club pendant match the book's description, and Roman gold "Hercules' Club" amulets were apotropaic protective charms found chiefly in female graves. The defining resonance for this hunt is the lion (Nemean lion → "Lion's Share" box) — an authorial wink, not a coordinate. The most accessible "Lion"-named public-land candidate is Lion Mountain Trail in Flathead National Forest, MT.

Amethyst (gemstone) and its Dionysus origin myth

Amethyst is purple quartz; its name comes from Greek "amethystos" ("not intoxicated"), and the sobriety belief is genuinely ancient. The popular maiden-turned-to-stone romance, however, was invented in 1576 by French poet Remy Belleau — an Early Modern literary fiction dressed as classical myth. This "beyond the myth" distinction matters: the real hook is mineralogy and US occurrence. Amethyst is the official state gemstone of South Carolina (1969); the South Carolina State Museum in Columbia displays a record 118-lb cluster from the Diamond Hill Mine. The most distinctive US locality is the Four Peaks Amethyst Mine in Tonto National Forest, Arizona — the only US commercial amethyst mine, but private and remote. Maine's Deer Hill (White Mountain National Forest) is the most rule-compatible public amethyst site.

Alexander the Great

Alexander III of Macedon (356–323 BC) became king in 336 BC and in ~12–13 years conquered from the Mediterranean to India, founding or renaming 20–70+ cities (most famously Alexandria, Egypt). His conquests funneled vast gold into Greece, the basis for the chapter's four antiquities. Critical for the hunt: nearly all US "Alexander" place names are NOT named for Alexander the Great — Alexandria, VA honors the local Alexander family (George Washington surveyed the town); Alexander County, NC (the prior failed search) honors a colonial/political family. Cleopatra and Ptolemy (Ptolemaic Egypt)

Ptolemy I Soter, a Macedonian general and Alexander's companion, took Egypt after Alexander's death (323 BC) and founded the Ptolemaic dynasty centered on Alexandria, which ruled ~300 years until Cleopatra VII died in 30 BC — the direct provenance era of the chapter's snake bracelet. Snake/serpent bracelets are Hellenistic, appearing in Egypt only in the Ptolemaic period; the snake bangle became iconically associated with Cleopatra. The Met holds several relevant Ptolemaic snake bracelets and a possible Cleopatra VII statue. The most literal physical US "Cleopatra/Ptolemaic" landmark is Cleopatra's Needle in Central Park, NY — the only authentic ancient Egyptian obelisk in the US — but it is urban and an implausible hide. The Egyptian-revival/obelisk motif (e.g., Needles in Canyonlands NP, Pharaoh Mountain in the Adirondacks) is speculation requiring cross-chapter corroboration.
