Moon Rocks & Meteors

Lesson: The Next Frontier

Chapter 19 · book pages 165-170

Summary

Chapter 19, "Moon Rocks & Meteors" (subtitle "The Next Frontier"), opens with a polished spherical moon rock photo and an epigraph from Arthur C. Clarke about distinguished scientists being right that something is possible but probably wrong when they call it impossible. The author confesses to being "a bit of a nerd," naming Star Trek: The Next Generation as his favorite show from ages 16 to 22 and Captain Jean-Luc Picard as his television hero, quoting the show's "Space: the final frontier" opening narration. He frames the present moment (2024) as humanity standing on the threshold of a "galactic treasure hunt" with space tourism beginning, a moon colony impending, and crewed Mars flights being planned.

The author then recounts a formative childhood memory: in the countryside where he grew up, the Milky Way was visible almost every night, something he now misses living near a large city. With a small telescope received for Christmas, he would climb onto the roof of his parents' minivan, set up a tripod, lie on his stomach, and gaze at planets and stars, often wishing he could "reach up and touch the moon." He states, "Forty years later, I figured out a way to do just that" — by acquiring a moon rock. He explains that moon rock is extremely rare (all of it on Earth would fill "roughly four gym lockers"), mostly returned by the Apollo missions, but that in 2017 one of the largest lunar meteorites ever found was discovered in the Western Sahara Desert and named NWA 12691. A few "moon globes" were carved from it and fought over by collectors; the author says he was a "more careful buyer." He reflects on the 1969 Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin moon landing as proof that "what can be imagined, can be done," and argues that exploration is human nature, imprinted in our DNA, which is part of what makes us treasure hunters.

The chapter then turns to meteorite hunting as a global cottage industry — hunters search the Sahara on camelback, walk grids in the Mohave (Mojave) Desert, and arrive by jeep in the jungles of Central America, with flat desert expanses being the easiest places to find fallen meteorites. He tells the story of the Aguas Zarcas meteorite, which in 2019 landed on a doghouse in a small village in Costa Rica. Once it was determined to possibly carry rare amino acids tied to the formation of life billions of years ago, a "meteor hunt free-for-all" ensued; one hunter even negotiated a deal for the main meteorite plus the doghouse. Sidebars confirm both treasure items: the moon globe is a piece of NWA 12691 (a similar lunar sphere sold for over $500,000 at a 2021 auction), and a piece of the Aguas Zarcas meteorite — famous for its carbonaceous chondrite classification and "foundational amino acids... precursors of life on our planet" — is in the treasure.

The final pages compare Aguas Zarcas to the Murchison meteorite, which landed in Australia in 1969 and is now nearly impossible to acquire, noting the author was "fortunate to acquire a piece when I did." He introduces the Overview Effect — the powerful emotional and perspective shift astronauts experience seeing Earth from space, including interconnectedness, deep appreciation, love, spiritual awakenings, and sometimes sadness at visible destruction. He ties this to his habit of stargazing for "a fresh standpoint" and delivers the chapter's core treasure-hunting lesson: "Perspective is worth considering when treasure hunting," that "embracing new perspectives is often the essential key to finding a treasure we are pursuing," and that "a simple change in perspective can be all that's required to fill that gap between us and something we desire." He confesses he held "misguided views" — being "rigid and obstinate" — about two personal matters now represented by two favorite treasure objects, which he will discuss in the next chapter, The Six-Figure Birthstone. The right page closes with an illustration of a person sitting on a hillside looking up at a shooting star and the Milky Way.

Themes & the Lesson

The subtitle "The Next Frontier" plays on Star Trek's "Space: the final frontier," casting both space exploration and treasure hunting as acts of pushing past perceived limits. The author uses the chapter to deliver an explicit, repeated treasure-hunting instruction: perspective matters. He argues — via the astronauts' "Overview Effect" and his own childhood habit of stargazing for "a fresh standpoint" — that "embracing new perspectives is often the essential key to finding a treasure we are pursuing" and that "a simple change in perspective can be all that's required to fill that gap between us and something we desire."

Practically, this reads as a hint to the solver: if you are stuck, look at the clues (or the landscape/map) from a different angle, elevation, or vantage point. He underscores it by admitting he was "rigid and obstinate" about two viewpoints that nearly kept two favorite items out of the treasure — a setup explicitly handed off to Chapter 20 ("Choosing a New Perspective").

Research Findings

NWA 12691 lunar meteorite

NWA 12691 ("Northwest Africa 12691") is a lunar feldspathic breccia recovered from the Western Sahara / Algeria border region around January 2017. It is one of the largest lunar meteorites ever found: recovered pieces total ~103.8 kg, and the paired stone NWA 12760 (58.1 kg main mass) is recognized by Guinness World Records as the largest single lunar meteorite on Earth, larger than any single Apollo return sample. Small polished spheres and segments cut from it have sold at auction (e.g., a 30 mm, 41.16 g polished sphere sold for $6,873 at RR Auction in Oct 2023; other pieces sold via Sotheby's and Christie's).

The chain behind it has strong US ties. It was classified by Dr. Anthony "Tony" Irving, an affiliate professor in Earth and Space Sciences at the University of Washington (Seattle), the world's go-to meteorite classifier (earlier at the University of Chicago and NASA's Lunar and Planetary Institute in Houston). NWA 12691 and NWA 12760 were purchased by Darryl Pitt, the New York-based curator of the Macovich Collection of Meteorites and a pioneer of meteorite auctions. Specimens are documented as coming from "The Stifler Collection of Meteorites" — the centerpiece of the Maine Mineral & Gem Museum in Bethel, Maine, which holds the world's largest collection of lunar and Martian meteorites, including the five largest known pieces of the Moon on Earth.

Murchison meteorite

The Murchison meteorite is a CM2-type carbonaceous chondrite that fell as a witnessed fall on September 28, 1969, near Murchison, Victoria, Australia (about 100 miles north of Melbourne). Over 100 kg was recovered, making it one of the most-studied carbonaceous chondrites in history, famous for containing 80-90+ amino acids plus other organics. In 2020 a team led by Philipp Heck dated presolar silicon-carbide grains in it at up to ~7 billion years old — the oldest solid material ever found on Earth.

Its US ties center on Chicago. Shortly after the 1969 fall, the Field Museum of Natural History (through curator Edward J. Olsen and Chicago-area donors) acquired the bulk of the fall (~38-50 kg) and holds the largest US/world fraction; the 2020 stardust study was a joint University of Chicago / Field Museum effort. A second major US fraction (~20 kg) is held at the Smithsonian's National Museum of Natural History in Washington, D.C. In its native country, pieces are at Museums Victoria (Melbourne). In Chapter 19 the book pairs Murchison with Aguas Zarcas as two celebrated "amino acid" carbonaceous chondrites — a science framing rather than an obvious geographic one.

Overview Effect (Frank White)

The "Overview Effect" is the cognitive/perceptual shift astronauts report when they see Earth whole from space — a sense of fragility, interconnectedness, and a literal change of vantage point. The term was coined by space philosopher Frank White, who had a "mild" version while flying across the US, then formalized it while working with physicist Gerard K. O'Neill at the Space Studies Institute in Princeton, NJ. It debuted publicly on a 1985 poster at an SSI meeting; White published the foundational book in 1987 (4th ed., 2021). White is a Harvard graduate and Rhodes Scholar who lives outside Boston, MA. The concept is rooted in the astronaut corps and Apollo-era Earth images ("Earthrise," "Blue Marble"); its natural institutional homes are NASA's Johnson Space Center (Houston, TX) and Kennedy Space Center (Merritt Island, FL).

Arthur C. Clarke

Sir Arthur C. Clarke (1917-2008) was a British science-fiction author and futurist, born in Minehead, Somerset, England. The Chapter 19 epigraph (printed as "Clark") is Clarke's First Law, from his 1962 book Profiles of the Future. He is famed for 2001: A Space Odyssey, Clarke's three laws, and the 1945 paper that popularized the geostationary communications satellite (the geostationary orbit is officially the "Clarke Orbit"). He lived most of his life in Sri Lanka, not the US. His US ties are honorific: induction into the International Space Hall of Fame (New Mexico Museum of Space History, Alamogordo, NM, 1989), holdings and papers at the Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum (Washington, D.C.), CBS Apollo broadcasts with Walter Cronkite, and Monument Valley filming for 2001.

Buzz Aldrin

Mojave (Mohave) Desert

The Mojave Desert spans California, Nevada, Arizona, and Utah and is one of the most productive meteorite-recovery regions in the US because its exposed dry lake beds (playas) make dark space rocks easy to spot. Hunters "walk a grid" across the playas with magnets and detectors. On most BLM-administered Mojave public land, casual limited meteorite collecting is allowed; some areas (national preserves, military reservations) are off-limits.

The most chapter-relevant Mojave meteorite is the Old Woman Meteorite — the largest meteorite ever found in California (~6,070 lb), found in 1976 in the Old Woman Mountains (San Bernardino County) on BLM public land. Its main mass is now displayed at the BLM's Desert Discovery Center in Barstow, CA (just off I-15), with a ~177 lb slab at the UCLA Meteorite Gallery in Los Angeles. This ties the chapter's lone concrete US geography to a real find site (Old Woman Mountains Wilderness) and a real public museum, plus a UCLA/LA link near the author's reported residence.

Apollo program / NASA (moon rock samples and US public displays)

NASA's six Apollo landings (1969-1972) returned 842 pounds (382 kg) of lunar material — essentially all the crewed-mission moon rock on Earth, the basis for the book's "four gym lockers" analogy. NASA retains ~85% at the Lunar Sample Laboratory Facility, Johnson Space Center, Houston, TX; Space Center Houston's Lunar Vault is the largest public display. Most relevant to the hunt is the "Goodwill Moon Rock" program: after Apollo 11 and Apollo 17, President Nixon gave a small encased lunar fragment plus a flag to all 50 US states (and territories/nations), creating exactly one designated lunar-sample display per US state — a near-perfect parallel to the hunt's "one box per state" framing. Many are displayed in state capitols, museums, and planetariums; several have been lost or stolen. Only about eight lunar samples worldwide can be touched by the public, the first unveiled when the Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum opened in 1976.
