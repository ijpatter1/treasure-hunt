A Puzzle Box, a Magnifying Glass, & the Mysterious Egg

Lesson: Joy Is in the Details

Chapter 3 · book pages 38–45

Summary

Chapter 3 opens with two epigraphs about attention to small things — one from photographer Henri Cartier-Bresson (“In photography, the smallest thing can be a great subject. The little human detail can become a Leitmotiv.”) and one from Gosho Aoyama, creator of the Detective Conan manga (“With a keen eye for details, one truth prevails!”). The author then recounts a late-2018 trip to Japan, taken to recover from music-industry burnout. He fell in love with Tokyo, then traveled with his partner Kimberly to the small mountain town of Hakone — famous for mineral baths, scenic beauty below lush green mountains, and finely crafted wooden puzzle boxes (Himitsu-Bako). They bought several puzzle boxes as gifts and brought them back to Los Angeles.

The narrative then pivots geographically with a precise hinge sentence: “about 6,000 nautical miles east of Japan and forty miles northeast of Asheville, North Carolina, lies another small town tucked away into the mountains. This place is named Bakersville.” The author notes that Bakersville has “scenic green mountains not too dissimilar to Hakone.” In Bakersville lives blacksmith Seth Gould, an artisan whose handcrafted tools are sought worldwide. Gould spent more than a year designing and crafting the five treasure boxes that hold the entire treasure.

After failing to find suitable antique boxes through weeks of sourcing auction houses and brokers, the author discovered Gould's masterwork — a nearly two-foot, iron/brass/steel forged puzzle box that resembles a small fortress, named “Coffer” — on display at the Smithsonian American Art Museum. Gould had himself visited Hakone and, on returning to North Carolina, reinvented the wooden Japanese puzzle box in metal. In their first conversation the author “made sure he knew I'd grown up in North Carolina and that I had lots of family who lived not too far from him.”

The author then describes the largest treasure box (the Lion's Share box), which combines design elements from the other four: yellow brass sides, a steel top edge, and a textured iron center locking panel. It is a puzzle box requiring SEVEN actions to open; instructions for the seven steps are left with the box so finders need not damage it. One box required no fewer than a quarter-million chisel marks. Readers are pointed to Seth Gould's Instagram for behind-the-scenes images.

The chapter then introduces a featured treasure item: a gold-and-jade magnifying glass made around 1900, valued over $25,000, crafted by Michael Perkhin, head workmaster at Fabergé — included as a “Sherlock Holmesian symbol, reminding us of the importance of paying attention to the smallest things.” Perkhin's biography parallels Gould's: born poor, became a blacksmith at 16, a goldsmith at 18, was hired by Carl Fabergé, and was named workmaster (the shop's highest position) by age 26. Perkhin reimagined everyday objects (ink bottles, umbrella handles, table clocks, ashtrays) as jeweled art and designed and crafted more than half of the 54 Imperial Fabergé eggs.

The chapter's centerpiece story is the Third Imperial Egg, commissioned by Tsar Alexander III for his wife Maria Feodorovna for Easter 1886. It vanished after the 1917 Russian Revolution for decades, resurfacing in 2004 at an antiques show in the Midwest United States, where a scrap-metal dealer bought it intending to melt it down. It sat on his kitchen counter for years until one morning he examined the watch inside and noticed the tiny engraving “Vacheron Constantin.” A web search led him to identify it as the lost Third Imperial Egg; Fabergé experts in London authenticated it, and it was valued at roughly $33 million and sold to a private collector.

The author uses this as the chapter's lesson on noticing details, explicitly stating “When reading this book, noticing details will help you find our treasure,” and admitting that details are his own weakness. He thanks Seth Gould, notes that both Gould and Perkhin “blazed their own paths,” and transitions to The 96 Carat Chivor Emerald by introducing his friend Rob Lavinsky and a famous emerald mine and 96-carat emerald.

Themes & the Lesson

The subtitle “Joy Is in the Details” frames the entire chapter as an instruction to pay close attention to small things. The author reinforces it through Japanese craft culture, Seth Gould's quarter-million chisel marks, Perkhin's mastery of tiny jeweled objects, and especially the Third Imperial Egg parable — a $33M treasure that hid in plain sight for years until someone noticed a tiny engraving.

The author makes the instruction explicit and literal: “When reading this book, noticing details will help you find our treasure.” For the hunt this is a direct meta-clue that small textual details — precise distances, place names, numbers — in the book are intentional and load-bearing. Research Findings

Bakersville, North Carolina

Bakersville is a small mountain town in Mitchell County, western North Carolina, in the Blue Ridge Mountains roughly 40 miles northeast of Asheville. It was named for settler David Baker, recognized as a town in 1852, made the Mitchell County seat in 1868, and incorporated in 1870. Its history is deeply tied to mining: the Cherokee mined mica, emeralds, aquamarine, quartz, feldspar, and kaolin in the region long before European settlement, and a mica-mining boom in the 1870s drove the town's growth. Today it is known as an arts town (the “Gateway to the Roan”), for the nearby Penland School of Craft, and for its annual Rhododendron Festival (started 1946). The chapter's relevance comes through box-maker Seth Gould, who is based in/affiliated with Bakersville and was a Penland resident artist. The dominant public-land feature near town is Roan Mountain / the Roan Highlands (~13 miles north via NC Hwy 261), part of Pisgah National Forest, including the world's largest natural rhododendron gardens, Roan High Knob (6,286 ft), Carvers Gap, and the Appalachian Trail balds.

Seth Gould (blacksmith)

Seth Gould is a contemporary American metalsmith and toolmaker who hand-forged all five treasure boxes, including the Lion's Share box. The author found him after seeing his hand-forged all-metal puzzle box “Coffer” (2018; iron/steel/brass; ~2 years to build) at the Smithsonian American Art Museum, then commissioned the boxes. He grew up on Cape Cod, earned a BFA in Metalsmithing & Jewelry Design from Maine College of Art (2009), drew formative inspiration from an antique-lockwork museum in Rouen, France, and later opened a shop in Cleveland, Ohio. His core NC connection is the Penland School of Craft (Mitchell County, Bakersville mailing address), where he was a Core Fellow (~2011–2013) and Resident Artist (from fall 2015). Importantly, Gould no longer lives in North Carolina — he now works from a home studio in Harwich, Massachusetts. His work is held by the Renwick Gallery / SAAM (Washington, D.C.), the National Ornamental Metal Museum (Memphis, TN), and the Mint Museum (Charlotte, NC).

Coffer (Seth Gould artwork at Smithsonian American Art Museum)

“Coffer” (2018) is a hand-forged iron, steel, and brass strongbox/puzzle box by Seth Gould, measuring 22.5″ × 14.25″ × 11.125″. It took roughly two years to make; every component — including screws, springs, and three-piece keys — was individually fabricated. It belongs to the Smithsonian American Art Museum / Renwick Gallery in Washington, D.C. (object no. 2019.22; currently “Not on view”), acquired in 2019. Coffer was conceived and forged during Gould's 2016–2018 Resident Artist residency at Penland School of Craft in Mitchell County, NC (campus mailing address: 3135 Conley Ridge Rd, Bakersville, NC 28705; nearest towns Spruce Pine ~6 mi and Bakersville ~9 mi). Coffer is the demonstrated stylistic/technical prototype for the five treasure boxes (brass/steel/iron; the Lion's Share box requires 7 actions to open).

Mount Mitchell / Roan Mountain / Pisgah & Blue Ridge area near Bakersville

Bakersville is the seat of Mitchell County, NC (founded 1868; the county is named for scientist Elisha Mitchell). Hakone, Japan

Hakone is a town in far-southwestern Kanagawa Prefecture, Honshu, Japan, on the southern shore of Lake Ashi inside the caldera of Mount Hakone, within Fuji-Hakone-Izu National Park. It is famous for scenic green mountains, Mount Fuji views, and many geothermal hot springs (the “mineral baths”). Hakone is the birthplace of the Japanese puzzle box (himitsu-bako, “secret box”) tradition, which emerged in the Hakone-Odawara region around the 1870s, decorated with yosegi-zaiku marquetry. The most concrete US tie to the “Hakone” name is Hakone Estate and Gardens in Saratoga, California — the oldest Japanese-style residential garden in the Western Hemisphere (established 1915), now a city park on the National Register of Historic Places. Note: I found NO documented sister-city or institutional link between Hakone and Bakersville; the “twin-town parallel” is the author's own literary framing, not a real-world relationship.

Michael Perkhin (Mikhail Evlampievich Perkhin)

Michael Perkhin (1860–1903) was the head workmaster of the House of Fabergé from 1886 until his death, and the maker of the treasure's gold-and-jade (nephrite) magnifying glass. He was born May 22, 1860 in the village of Okulovskaya in the Olonets Governorate (now Republic of Karelia, Russia), to a peasant family. He first worked metal under the village blacksmith, arrived in St. Petersburg around age 16–18 reportedly self-taught, apprenticed under Erik August Kollin, qualified as master (1886), and was appointed Fabergé's head workmaster. He personally supervised and signed the most famous Imperial Easter Eggs of the era and made many utilitarian luxury objects including gold-mounted nephrite magnifying glasses. (Note: I could not confirm the “priest taught him to read/write” detail from these sources; the verified through-line is peasant/blacksmith-to-goldsmith.) US holdings of Perkhin/Fabergé work: the Virginia Museum of Fine Arts (Richmond), Hillwood Estate (Washington, D.C.), and the Walters Art Museum (Baltimore).

Third Imperial Fabergé Egg

The Third (Imperial) Fabergé Egg is a gold Easter egg made in 1887 in Fabergé's St. Petersburg workshop (goldsmith August Holmström) for Tsar Alexander III, who gave it to Empress Maria Feodorovna. Its “surprise” is a lady's watch by Swiss maker Vacheron Constantin. Confiscated after the 1917 Revolution and then lost, it surfaced at a Parke-Bernet (now Sotheby's) auction in New York on March 7, 1964, sold unrecognized for $2,450. Decades later an anonymous scrap-metal dealer bought it for roughly $13,000–14,000 at a flea market in the American Midwest (around 2004), intending to melt it for gold. He kept it in his kitchen cupboard for years until, in 2012, he Googled the legible names “egg” and “Vacheron Constantin” and found a 2011 Telegraph article. London dealer Wartski authenticated it and in 2014 sold it (reportedly ~$33 million) to an anonymous private collector. Crucially: the Midwest finder's identity and exact location were deliberately concealed — reporting only describes “a very modest home in the Midwest, next to a highway and a Dunkin' Donuts.”

Vacheron Constantin

Vacheron Constantin is a Swiss luxury watch manufacturer founded in 1755 in Geneva by Jean-Marc Vacheron — the oldest continuously operating watch manufacturer in the world. It is based in the Canton of Geneva and the Vallée de Joux, Switzerland (a Richemont Group subsidiary since 1996). It has NO US founding, birthplace, or headquarters. Its relevance to Chapter 3 is precise and singular: “Vacheron Constantin” is the maker's name engraved on the small lady's watch hidden inside the Third Imperial Egg — literally “the detail that unlocked the egg's identity” when the scrap dealer Googled it. Vacheron Constantin pieces are held in US institutional collections, notably the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History (Washington, D.C.) and the Metropolitan Museum of Art (New York).
