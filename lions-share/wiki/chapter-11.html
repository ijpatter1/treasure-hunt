Treasures From a Famous Shipwreck

Lesson: The Temptress Greed

Chapter 11 · book pages 101-108

Summary

Chapter 11 opens with a Gandhi epigraph on greed, then dramatizes the loss of the Spanish/Portuguese treasure ship Nuestra Señora de la Luz ("Our Lady of the Light"). The narrative is set in Montevideo, Uruguay, on July 2, 1752 at 10am. Captain Felicio de Fonseca sits in a makeshift cabin along Montevideo's main dirt road drinking rum, having spent ten months in port. His ship has been at sea for over four years since departing Portugal under an elaborate company arrangement with the Spanish crown (the book names "King Ferdinand VII of Spain").

The chapter details the cargo, which is the heart of the greed theme. Officially the La Luz carries 12,500 freshly minted gold coins for the King under a 50/50 split contract. Secretly, hidden in barrels deep in the ship's powder hold, are an additional 200,000 pesos worth of gold coins plus forty large gold bars and disks that the King does not know about — spoils that would not be shared. Fonseca's friend Pedro De Lea, the company's official representative in Buenos Aires, had spent three years coordinating the arrival of gold transported from Lima and Santiago (Lima used traditional hand-pressed coinage; Santiago used the new "machine minted" escudos). De Lea repeatedly worried that the ship — burdened with 12,500 coins, 185 passengers, cargo, animals, months of rations, and twelve cannons (disguising the vessel as a "warship") — was dangerously over capacity. Fonseca dismissed his concerns.

The disaster strikes when De Lea bursts in shouting "Pampero! Pampero!" A pampero is a violent wind that can blow down the Río de la Plata, reaching up to 180 km/h in summer. With over 130 people aboard the La Luz anchored some three miles offshore, Fonseca races to the pier but cannot reach his ship; twelve-foot waves and a fierce swell force his small boat back to shore. The crew hoists anchor and sails into the Río de la Plata, but the overloaded ship is doomed. Through his telescope, Fonseca watches "his Lady of the Light drifting away."

The historical/factual section follows. On July 2, 1752 the La Luz sank in a violent storm near Montevideo. (The book text says "Brazil," which the notes flag as an error — Montevideo is in Uruguay.) Wreckage was strewn over fifty miles; none of the passengers survived, with many washing ashore weeks later with gold coins in their pockets. Fonseca recovered over ninety percent of the cargo within a year, but the hidden powder-hold treasure was never recovered because it was never supposed to exist. The Río de la Plata is described as one of the deadliest shipwreck graveyards in the world, with more than a thousand ships lost in its depths. In 1993 (the book's figure; documented salvage began 1992), the wreck was rediscovered while a salvor searched for a different shipwreck, El Preciado, at a site some fifty miles from the bay of Montevideo, recovering dozens of gold ingots and thousands of gold coins.

Three items from the La Luz now reside in the author's treasure. The chapter closes with the author's moral and an explicit safety message. Greed is "a seductress, luring us into bad decisions." He states one of his biggest concerns in designing the project was that the treasure might tempt people toward irrational, dangerous action, and stresses, "I placed all our boxes in safe locations." He directs readers to a summary of best practices in the postscript, calling it "a must read," with the goal of keeping seekers in good spirits, fine health, and away from misfortune. He notes greed often grows from a fear of scarcity, admitting he has been guilty of it himself. The chapter ends by previewing Chapter 12 on the California Gold Rush (see Massive Gold Rush Nugget) — "the largest treasure hunt in our country's history" — a "narrative of exuberant irrationality" whose scale shifted the demographics of and shaped America.

The Treasure(s)

Three artifacts recovered from the Nuestra Señora de la Luz wreck reside in the author's treasure:

  • La Luz gold bar — weighing slightly over 22 ounces, considered by many the most beautiful of those recovered, with a small half-circle where extra gold was added to meet minimum weight. Valued over $50,000 and bearing the Roman numeral XXVI (26).
  • La Luz gold disk — over 22 troy ounces, .975 purity (23.4 carats), the purest gold disk recorded from the La Luz with only one finer ingot, encrusted by the ocean and bearing the Roman numeral XIV (14). The author jokes it would make a decent "fashionable paper weight."
  • Eight-escudo gold coin — hand-pressed at the 1750 Lima mint, depicting the Spanish coat of arms, NGC-graded MS 63. Only fifteen such 1750 coins have ever been officially rated; the single higher-graded MS 64 example (not from a shipwreck) sold for over $25,000. The coin's NGC label references the "Reaudulfia Collection."

The chapter also notes that 245 limited-edition commemorative boxed sets were made (each containing a description, an eight-escudo coin, and a gilded silver replica of the gold bar), one of which was offered online for around $10,000.

Themes & the Lesson

The subtitle "The Temptress Greed" personifies greed as a seductress who lures people into bad decisions. The author uses Captain Fonseca as a cautionary tale: his (and the owners') avarice led them to overload the La Luz with hidden, unmanifested gold and keep it anchored for months in the deadly Río de la Plata, ultimately sinking the ship and killing all aboard.

Crucially, the lesson is turned into a safety instruction for treasure hunters. The author worries the value of his treasure could tempt seekers into "irrational action" and a "needlessly dangerous situation," so he reassures that all boxes are in SAFE locations and emphatically directs readers to the postscript's best-practices summary as "a must read." This reinforces the hunt's hard constraints (not dangerous, accessible, public land) and is more a behavioral/safety framing than a geographic clue. The "Lady of the Light" name also extends the book's recurring light/illumination motif.

Research Findings

Nuestra Señora de la Luz (Our Lady of the Light) — 1752 shipwreck

The Nuestra Señora de la Luz departed Buenos Aires in the summer of 1752 carrying New World gold and silver bound for Spain. While anchored at Montevideo taking on provisions, a storm drove her into the shoreline; all aboard were lost. Roughly 90% was salvaged after 1752, but the powder hold (~200,000 pesos, mostly silver) was not found until the wreck was relocated in April 1992 and salvaged 1992–93. The name is a Marian devotion ("Our Lady of the Light," a title of the Virgin Mary), not a lighthouse reference. Recovered gold was largely milled bust-type 8-escudos from the Santiago, Chile mint, with some Lima/Peru and Potosí pieces. The treasure was split with Uruguay and sold via Sotheby's. The single clear US connection of the actual artifacts is the sale venue: the coins/ingots were auctioned by Sotheby's in New York City in 1993, and now circulate in the US numismatic/shipwreck-coin market (dealers such as Pirate Gold Coins, Stack's Bowers) rather than residing in a fixed museum. Separately, the ship's name is shared by real US places, most notably the village of La Luz, New Mexico, named for a 1719 Franciscan chapel to Nuestra Señora de la Luz, with a local legend tying the name to a literal "light."

Ruben Corella (correct name: Ruben Collado)

The extraction's "Ruben Corella" appears to be an error. The documented salvor is Rubén Collado, an Argentine treasure hunter, and the discovery year was 1992 (1993 is the auction). Collado located the wreck (alias "El Preciado") in April 1992 off Montevideo in the Río de la Plata estuary near the Carrasco and Punta Gorda zones. His operation brought up ~800 coins dated 1751, 100–150 dated 1750, and several gold ingots — most notably milled 8 Escudos from the Santiago de Chile mint, plus gold cobs from Lima and silver cobs from Lima, Bogotá, and Potosí. The treasure was owned jointly with Uruguay (50% split) and sold at the Sotheby's New York auction of March 24–25, 1993 (~$3–3.5 million), with a second sale at Castells & Castells in Montevideo. Uruguay used its proceeds to build a school in San Carlos and equip its National Navy. Collado later spent 14 years pursuing the British warship Lord Clive (sunk 1763 off Colonia del Sacramento), which he found in 2004.

Roman numerals XXVI (26) and XIV (14) on the bar and disk

On Spanish colonial gold, Roman numerals stamped in rectangular cartouches denote fineness expressed as parts per 24 (the colonial equivalent of karat) — e.g., XVII = 17-karat, XXII = 22-karat. The book itself states the XIV disk is .975 purity ("23.4 carats"), a slight mismatch since "XIV" literally = 14, suggesting the stamp may record a different fineness scale, a tax/assay mark, or that the book conflated stamp and lab-tested purity. Independent auction records (Stack's Bowers) describe near-identical La Luz pieces: a gold disc inscribed "XIV," and a gold oblong bar inscribed "XXXVI." So the numerals are foundry/assay fineness-and-inventory marks, not a hidden message. Reading XXVI/XIV as a syllable count, lat/long, or letter game would violate the author's stated no-cipher rule.

Lima Mint, Peru (eight-escudo gold coin)

The Casa de Moneda de Lima struck gold cobs almost continuously from 1696 to 1750. The 8-escudo coin (the "doubloon") was the largest denomination, ~27g of 22k gold, hand-pressed "cobs" bearing the crowned Spanish coat of arms quartered with the castles of Castile and lions of Leon — matching the book's "hand-pressed eight-escudo coin bearing the Spanish coat of arms" exactly. About 3.24 million were minted 1659–1750, but only ~4,000 survive (~1%). The most important US connection: the surviving market supply of Lima 8-escudos comes overwhelmingly from the 1715 Spanish Plate Fleet, which sank in a hurricane on July 30–31, 1715 along Florida's Atlantic coast (the "Treasure Coast"). Recovered Lima 8-escudos and salvage operations cluster around named public-land sites such as the McLarty Treasure Museum inside Sebastian Inlet State Park, Fort Pierce, and Vero Beach.

Río de la Plata

A large estuary in South America formed where the Paraná and Uruguay rivers meet, forming the Argentina–Uruguay border. It is the explicit geographic anchor of the chapter's shipwreck and a reputed shipwreck "graveyard" (the Banco Inglés sandbar alone has 500+ reported wrecks). The river was named "Río de la Plata" by Sebastian Cabot (c. 1526) after silver legends; "plate" was Early Modern English for silver. So the subject's core association is silver — reinforcing the chapter's greed/gold theme. The "La Plata" name family recurs across US geography (see Signals).

El Preciado shipwreck

El Preciado was a Spanish treasure galleon reputedly sunk in 1792 in the Río de la Plata. In 1992 Rubén Collado obtained Uruguayan permission to salvage it, set up a base at Punta Gorda (~7 miles east of Montevideo), and — while hunting El Preciado — found a different, older wreck nearby beginning April 10, 1992. Numismatic dating (coins of 1750–1751, pre-dating 1792) settled the identity dispute: the treasure actually came from the Nuestra Señora de la Luz. The Uruguayan government commissioned Sotheby's to sell the recovery in New York on March 24–25, 1993, the entry point for "La Luz Shipwreck" coins into the US trade.
