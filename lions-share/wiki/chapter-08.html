Beauty's Bespoken Treasures

Lesson: Know the Past, See the Future

Chapter 8 · book pages 76-85

Summary

Chapter 8 profiles the Hopi master jeweler Charles Loloma and his niece and protégée Sonwai (Verma Nequatewa), whose pieces make up the chapter's treasure items. The chapter opens with an epigraph from inspirational author Orison Swett Marden, then delivers one of the book's most explicit meta-clues: author Jon Collins-Black reveals that he wrote a children's picture book, Our Unbreakable Thread, published by Marble Press the same year as There's Treasure Inside, and that “for fun” he “decided to leave an extra clue or two within my children's book about the location of our treasure.” The children's book is described as a story of generational love connecting parent, child, and grandchild, written as an homage to his children, wife, and parents.

The chapter then turns to Hopi history as context for Loloma. It states the Hopi are the oldest North American tribe, having migrated north from Mexico around 500 BC; that their village of Oraibi is the oldest continuously occupied Native American settlement; that “Hopi” means “peaceful” and “polite”; and that they retain only about 9% of their original land (referencing the American Indian Removal Act of 1830). Charles Loloma was born in 1921 into the Badger clan, lived his entire life on the Hopi reservation following the Hopi calendar and farming the land, and served as an elder, musician, mime/clown, Snake Priest, and leader of the Hopi Bean Dance ceremonies.

Loloma became the most prominent and famous Native American jewelry designer in the world; his pieces are described as the rarest and most sought-after Native American jewelry anywhere. He began as a painter, decorating his high school with murals that drew the attention of artist Fred Kabotie, who invited him to San Francisco. On Treasure Island in San Francisco Bay, Loloma was one of three Native Americans to paint installations in the Indian Court of the island's Federal Building, seen by over half a million people; he later painted murals at the Museum of Modern Art (MoMA) in New York City. The Gallup Inter-Tribal Ceremonial initially rejected his jewelry as “not Indian enough.”

Loloma's career milestones followed: he won first prize seven consecutive years at the Scottsdale National Indian Art Exhibition; in 1962 he created an ironwood bracelet whose stacking/inlay techniques were inspired by trips to New York City and were “inlaid in a way that looked like a cityscape” (these ironwood bracelets became his most prized pieces); in 1963 his jewelry appeared on Parisian fashion runways; and in 1964 President Lyndon Johnson gifted one of Loloma's necklaces to the Queen of the Netherlands. Olgivanna Wright, wife of architect Frank Lloyd Wright, commissioned two bracelets; her suggestion to fill a casting mistake-hole with a stone inspired Loloma's signature stone-inlay technique. Despite his fame, Loloma kept living in Hotevilla, in a tightly clustered home with no running water, phones, or electricity. He said he wished to be remembered “for beauty,” and “Loloma” means beauty. Hopi tribe member Debbie Drye is quoted that his house “sits right on the edge of Hopi, so when you look out there you see the vastness.”

His niece Sonwai (Verma Nequatewa) — “Sonwai” being the feminine form of “beauty” — began training with him at 17, worked with him for over 20 years, and continues his style today with a limited annual output. The chapter describes both Loloma and Sonwai as pilots who flew together over Hopi land and Canyon de Chelly to study rock formations from above, which inspired their inlay designs (“It's a bird's eye view”). (See Research Findings below: independent sources confirm Loloma's flying/bird's-eye-view of the Hopi mesas but do NOT corroborate a Sonwai pilot claim or a Canyon de Chelly tie.) The “More About the Hopi” section recounts smallpox devastation after Spanish/European arrival in the 1600s, U.S. land seizures and cultural-eradication efforts in the 1800s (forced boarding-school education, forced haircuts, pressured Christian conversion), and that resisting chief Loma-hongvoma was imprisoned at Alcatraz — noting “Alcatraz stands less than three miles from Treasure Island where Charles Loloma's murals later blazed.” It also describes Hopi dry farming (corn, melons, beans, squash) and the Tutuveni petroglyphs near the Grand Canyon (over 150 giant rocks, more than 5,000 Hopi drawings, a record over 1,000 years old).

The chapter closes by tying its lesson to a warning: learning other cultures keeps the mind “free and open,” whereas believing there is “just one right way” leads to confirmation bias — which the book prints as “a treasure hunter's most dreaded foil” — explicitly setting up Chapter 9. The Sonwai ring and the two Loloma rings were exhibited at the “Loloma” exhibit at the Wheelwright Museum of Native American Art in Santa Fe. The final page is a full circular Hopi calendar diagram dividing the year into Katsina and Non-Katsina seasons at the winter and summer solstices, with named ceremonies (Powamuya/Bean Dance, Talangva/Niman Home Dance, Tala'paamuya/Snake-Antelope or Flute, Kyamuya/Soystangwu, etc.).

The Treasure(s)

The chapter's treasure is a set of four pieces of Hopi jewelry — one bracelet and two stone rings by Charles Loloma plus one ring by Sonwai. Per the book, the Sonwai ring along with the two Loloma rings was exhibited (not purchased) at the “Loloma” exhibit at the Wheelwright Museum of Native American Art in Santa Fe, New Mexico:

  • Charles Loloma ironwood bracelet — ironwood, turquoise, lapis lazuli, coral, and gold; estimated value over $27,000. The ironwood bracelets, with their stacked inlay resembling a cityscape, were Loloma's most prized work.
  • Charles Loloma stone ring (the “blue ring,” shown on page 76) — a free-form turquoise stone with a rim inlaid with fossilized ivory, ironwood, and coral.
  • Charles Loloma stone ring (the “purple ring”) — a sugilite stone with lapis lazuli, coral, and turquoise.
  • Sonwai (Verma Nequatewa) ring — sugilite, turquoise, coral, lapis lazuli, and gold; estimated value over $20,000.

People

Charles Loloma (1921–1991) — Hopi master jeweler of the Badger clan, born near Hotevilla on Third Mesa of the Hopi Reservation in northeastern Arizona. His name means “Beauty” in Hopi, a direct echo of the chapter's subtitle. He is regarded as the most influential 20th-century Native American jeweler. He began as a muralist (Treasure Island's Federal Building, MoMA), then turned to jewelry around 1955, pioneering gold, gemstones, ivory, and exotic woods. He won the Scottsdale National Indian Art Exhibition seven years running, supplied clients including Mamie Eisenhower and Olgivanna Wright, and had work gifted by President Lyndon Johnson. He built a studio/gallery on a rocky knoll near Hotevilla overlooking the mesas.

Sonwai / Verma Nequatewa (b. 1949) — Loloma's niece, born in Hotevilla on Third Mesa, where she still maintains her studio. She apprenticed under Loloma (sources say 1966–1986) and carries his inlay tradition forward in 18k gold with high-grade turquoise, coral, lapis, sugilite, and ironwood. “Sonwai” is the Hopi feminine word for “beauty.”

Jon Collins-Black — the author, who in this chapter discloses that he hid “an extra clue or two” about the treasure's location inside his children's picture book Our Unbreakable Thread.

Fred Kabotie — Hopi artist who discovered Loloma's high-school murals and brought him to San Francisco; Kabotie's own work ties to the Grand Canyon (the Desert View Watchtower murals).

Frank Lloyd Wright & Olgivanna Wright — Olgivanna commissioned two Loloma bracelets, and her suggestion to fill a casting flaw with a stone inspired his signature inlay technique. The Wright connection links to Taliesin West in Scottsdale, Arizona.

Loma-hongvoma (Lomahongiwma / Lomahongyoma) — the resisting Hopi chief of the “Hostile” faction at Oraibi who, with eighteen other Hopi men, was imprisoned at Alcatraz in 1895 for refusing forced boarding-school assimilation. His imprisonment is the hinge for the chapter's Alcatraz / Treasure Island “less than three miles” statement.

Orison Swett Marden — New Hampshire-born self-help author whose quote forms the chapter's epigraph.

Themes & the Lesson

“Know the Past, See the Future” argues that honoring tradition and history is the wellspring of future vision: Loloma and Sonwai take ancient Hopi tradition and from it create “new visions of the future” in their jewelry. The author broadens it into a thinking-discipline lesson — studying many cultures keeps the mind “free and open,” and the failure mode of a narrow, single-truth mindset is confirmation bias, which he names “a treasure hunter's most dreaded foil” (the book prints “foil”).

So the lesson does double duty: thematically (past informs future) and practically (a direct caution to the hunter not to lock onto one location theory), explicitly teeing up Chapter 9 (“Confirmation Bias”). The “unbreakable threads that bind generations” phrasing deliberately echoes the title of the author's children's book, reinforcing this chapter's meta-clue. Given that a prior boots-on-the-ground search at Rocky Face, NC already failed, this chapter's own warning is worth taking literally: let evidence point first, rather than forcing clues to fit a pre-chosen location.

Wheelwright Museum of the American Indian, Santa Fe, New Mexico

A private nonprofit museum at 704 Camino Lejo on Santa Fe's “Museum Hill” (~35.6625 N, 105.9272 W). Founded in 1937 by Boston anthropologist Mary Cabot Wheelwright with Navajo singer/medicine man Hastiin Klah; its building was designed in the shape of a Navajo hogan. Jewelry makes up nearly half its collection (the Jim and Lauris Phillips Center holds 700+ Navajo and Pueblo pieces), and its on-site Case Trading Post (opened 1975) sells art bought directly from artists. (Note: the book itself names this institution “the Wheelwright Museum of Native American Art” and states the Sonwai ring and two Loloma rings were exhibited here at the “Loloma” exhibit — not purchased here.) Founder associations also link to Boston, Massachusetts and Mount Desert Island, Maine, and to the Grand Canyon (the trip that first brought Wheelwright west). The museum itself is a private building, not a public-land hide site — best read as a region anchor for New Mexico.

Charles Loloma (Hotevilla, Third Mesa, Hopi Reservation, Arizona)

Charles Sequevya Loloma (Jan 7, 1921 – Jun 9, 1991), Badger clan, born near Hotevilla on Third Mesa. “Loloma” means “Beauty.” He helped reproduce the Awatovi murals for MoMA and painted murals at the Federal Building on Treasure Island (1939 GGIE); served in the Aleutians during WWII; studied ceramics at Alfred University, New York; opened a Scottsdale pottery shop in 1954; shifted to jewelry around 1955; and was recruited to the Institute of American Indian Arts in Santa Fe in 1962. By 1964–1966 he built a studio/gallery on a rocky knoll near Hotevilla overlooking Third Mesa. His clientele included Mamie Eisenhower and Olgivanna Wright; LBJ gifted his work to foreign dignitaries. He won the Scottsdale National Indian Art Exhibition seven consecutive years and had a 1971 Heard Museum one-man show.

Sonwai / Verma Nequatewa

Verma Nequatewa, who signs as “Sonwai” (Hopi feminine word for “beauty”), was born in 1949 in Hotevilla on Third Mesa, where she still keeps her studio. She apprenticed under her uncle Charles Loloma from 1966–1986 and carried his inlay-bracelet tradition forward after his death. Her work is held by the Museum of Northern Arizona (Flagstaff) and the Heard Museum (Phoenix), where her 2018 retrospective ran. Important corrections: no source supports the chapter's “pilot” claim for Sonwai (or, separately, confirms a Sonwai–Canyon de Chelly design tie); her consistently documented muse is the Hopi mesas near Hotevilla. The aerial/“bird's-eye” inspiration is sourced to Loloma flying over the Hopi mesas.

Hotevilla, Arizona

Hotevilla (formally part of the Hotevilla-Bacavi CDP) is a Hopi village on Third Mesa, Hopi Reservation, Navajo County, NE Arizona (~35.9139 N, 110.6406 W; elev. ~6,270 ft). Founded 1906 after the “Oraibi Split.” Arizona State Route 264 runs through Third Mesa near it, connecting to Bacavi, Kykotsmovi, and Old Oraibi. Loloma was born nearby and built a studio/gallery on a rocky knoll commanding panoramic views of the valleys and mesas — matching the book's “on the edge of Hopi…vastness” description. Constraint conflict: Hotevilla is sovereign tribal trust land with restricted outsider access and photography bans — it almost certainly violates the author's “public, accessible land” rule and is best read as a thematic/biographical anchor, not a hide site.

Canyon de Chelly National Monument, Arizona

A real US public-land unit in NE Arizona (Apache County; entrance at Chinle), established 1931 and NPS-administered, but entirely owned by the Navajo Nation. Its signature landmark is Spider Rock; other named sites include White House Ruin, Antelope House Ruin, and Mummy Cave. Caveat: visitors may not enter the canyon floor except on the single White House Ruin Trail; all other travel legally requires a Navajo guide. Documented sources tie Loloma's aerial “bird's-eye” design inspiration to the Hopi mesas, not specifically to Canyon de Chelly, which is Navajo land farther east — so the chapter's Canyon de Chelly attribution is not corroborated and access constraints make it a poor literal hide candidate.

Tutuveni petroglyphs (“Newspaper Rock”), near Tuba City / Cameron, Arizona

A prehistoric Hopi petroglyph site at the base of the Echo Cliffs, Coconino County (~36.157 N, 111.380 W), off State Highway 89 near Cameron and Tuba City. Eight large sandstone boulders bear 5,000+ Hopi clan symbols carved from ~1200 CE through the 1950s, tied to the Hopi Salt Pilgrimage along the ~100-mile Salt Trail to the Grand Canyon. Constraint conflict: a 2006 resolution placed the site on Navajo Nation land; the Salt Trail crosses tribal land (permit required) and involves dangerous cliff scrambling and a river gorge — failing both the “public land” and “not dangerous” rules. Thematic value only (carved meaning on rock faces; a long pilgrimage to a canyon); not an encoded map per the author's no-cipher stance.

Treasure Island, San Francisco Bay

A 393-acre artificial island built 1936–1937 in San Francisco Bay for the Golden Gate International Exposition (1939–1940), connected via causeway to Yerba Buena Island and Interstate 80 on the Bay Bridge. Its name was a deliberate literary nod to Robert Louis Stevenson's novel — on-the-nose label, not a buried-treasure reference. Within the fair's Federal Building, the “Indian Court” (Indian Arts and Crafts Board, organized by René d'Harnoncourt) is where ~18-year-old Loloma painted murals alongside Fred Kabotie. The Navy seized the island in 1942; the city acquired it in 2009 for redevelopment. Caveat: today it is dense, redeveloped municipal/urban land — not accessible undeveloped public land — so it fails as an actual hide site and reads as misdirection/flavor pointing back to Loloma and Arizona.

Alcatraz Island, San Francisco Bay

A 22-acre island ~1.25 miles offshore from San Francisco; a federal penitentiary (1934–1963), now a unit of Golden Gate National Recreation Area (NPS). Nineteen Hopi men, including the “Hostile”-faction leader Lomahongiwma (the book's “Loma-hongvoma”), were imprisoned here Jan–Aug 1895 for resisting forced assimilation at Oraibi. On the “less than three miles” claim: Alcatraz-to-Treasure-Island is an over-water, point-to-point distance of roughly 2–2.5 miles — NOT a distance to a road, and Alcatraz is reachable only by ferry. The superficial echo of the author's “3 miles from a road” rule is therefore almost certainly a coincidence/flourish. The durable lead is the clue-by-association to the Hopi prisoners, which again points to northern Arizona.

Oraibi, Arizona (Third Mesa)

Old Oraibi (Orayvi) is a Hopi village on Third Mesa, Navajo County (~35.8758 N, 110.6403 W; elev. ~6,057 ft), reached by a short access road off Arizona State Route 264. Founded before ~1100–1150 AD, it is widely cited as possibly the oldest continuously inhabited settlement in the US; a National Historic Landmark (1964). The 1906 “Oraibi Split” (a non-violent “push-of-war”) sent the traditionalist faction to found Hotevilla and Bacavi. Constraint conflict: Oraibi and the whole Hopi Reservation are sovereign tribal land with strict prohibitions on photography, sketching, and note-taking; very unlikely to be the hide. Useful only as a Hopi/Third Mesa/Coronado-Cibola thematic thread.
