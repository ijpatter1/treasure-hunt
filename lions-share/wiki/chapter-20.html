The Six-Figure Birthstone

Lesson: Choosing a New Perspective

Chapter 20 · book pages 171-178

Summary

Chapter 20, "The Six-Figure Birthstone," opens with two epigraphs about perspective: one attributed in the text to "Lscrae" (almost certainly the Christian hip-hop artist Lecrae) — "Instead of complaining that the rose bush is full of thorns, be grateful the thorn bush has roses. Perspective." — and one from psychotherapist Steve de Shazer — "Where you stand determines what you see and what you do not see; it determines also the angle you see it from; a change in where you stand changes everything." The author frames the entire treasure as "intensely personal," saying each of the thousands of considered items had to carry personal significance, "as if pieces of my soul are now nested inside our five boxes" (reconfirming five boxes total).

The chapter is built around two stones the author initially refused to include because of old, biased perspectives. The first is a 201-carat smokey quartz cut in a custom design called "Imperial Brilliant" by gem cutter Mark Oros, sourced from "the state of Rio de" (truncated in the text, almost certainly Rio de Janeiro, Brazil) and described as "arguably the most velvet tone in our treasure." The author carried a childhood negative association with smokey quartz: in a callback to The 96 Carat Chivor Emerald, he recalls digging for crystals in an empty lot as a kid and finding mostly "small chunks of dark smokey quartz," which made the stone represent "disappointment and unrewarded toil." He notes that fine Asian art, Fabérge pieces, and Rob Lavinsky's signature mineral collections use smokey quartz, yet he still resisted it. His perspective changed when, browsing a private collection for something unrelated, he was struck by the beauty of a large gem before learning it was smokey quartz, and so "saw beauty in smokey quartz for the very first time." He uses the way Mark Oros's cut angles light to reflect into the stone as a metaphor for how shifting perspective lets clarity pour in. A sidebar notes a separate fancy smokey quartz titled "Breath of Heaven" by Derek Grasso is explicitly NOT in the treasure.

The second item is a 12-carat tsavorite garnet (a green garnet), valued at six figures — similar-carat tsavorites have sold at auction for six figures, and very few exist in this size. This ties to the author's birthstone story: born in January, his birthstone is garnet. At age nine he learned the red specks in the asphalt road in front of his house were "crushed pieces of garnet," and a friend's laminated birthstone chart revealed January's stone was that "very same red rock that was in our asphalt!" His friend had emerald, his father and sister had rubies, his baby brother had aquamarine, and "all I got was pavement!" — breeding nearly forty years of disgust for garnets. After his smokey-quartz epiphany, he researched garnets and discovered the rare green tsavorite, found by miner Campbell Bridges, who (per the book's telling) was charged by a buffalo in the Tanzanian countryside, dove into a ditch, and spotted a glimmer of green in an outcrop; he searched the area for seven more years before the official discovery. The book states this area of Tanzania "is still the only area where tsavorite has ever been found," that the best tsavorites resemble Chivor emeralds in color yet are "a thousand times more rare," and that Tiffany & Co. was the first brand jewelry designer to introduce tsavorite to the mainstream.

The chapter closes with the author embracing both stones and a broader meditation on perspective. He confirms his wife is Kimberly and that they regularly teach their children to see situations from different points of view. The capstone scene: walking with his wife in a "local park," they came upon a "massive rose garden," with "several acres of park terrain" painted by blooming roses. He spins an extended parable of a small, malcontent rose, speaking from ground level, warning a walker that the other roses are evil — but the walker, standing tall, sees the whole garden's beauty; "if only this rose could shift its vantage point." The lesson: our senses are limited, "we can only see so far in any one direction from any point at which we are stationed," so finding a new standpoint forms a more complete picture. The chapter ends by previewing Antiquities of Alexander: four antiquities forged as a result of the conquests of Alexander the Great, described as among the most impressive items in the treasure, on the theme of mythology and reality.

The Treasure(s)

Two gemstones the author originally rejected, then embraced once he "changed where he stood":

  • 201-carat smokey quartz — cut in a custom "Imperial Brilliant" design by faceter Mark Oros, sourced from Rio (Brazil). The author calls it "arguably the most velvet tone in our treasure." Its angled facets, reflecting light into the stone, serve as the chapter's central metaphor for perspective. (Note: Oros's publicly documented signature smoky-quartz piece is titled "Beyond Brilliant," ~199.33 carats, cut from Brazilian rough and sold at Bonhams; the book's "Imperial Brilliant" label could be a variant name worth re-checking against the page image.)
  • 12-carat tsavorite garnet — a vivid green grossular garnet, six-figure value, from the Kenya–Tanzania border region. The author's January birthstone (garnet) reframed: the rare green variant turned a stone he associated with "pavement" into a prized treasure item. The book compares its color to Chivor emeralds (a Chapter 4 callback) but says it is "a thousand times more rare."

A sidebar item, the fancy smokey quartz "Breath of Heaven" by Derek Grasso, is named only as a beauty/perspective example and is explicitly not in the treasure — useful for inventory hygiene when distinguishing in-treasure from illustrative pieces.

Themes & the Lesson

The subtitle "Choosing a New Perspective" is the chapter's organizing principle. The author argues perspective is a choice and that "developing the will to shift perspectives is one of the singular most powerful skills." He demonstrates it autobiographically: two stones he despised because of childhood associations (smokey quartz = unrewarded toil; garnet = worthless pavement) became treasured once he literally and figuratively changed where he stood. The Steve de Shazer epigraph — "a change in where you stand changes everything" — and the Oros cut "allowing light to reflect into" the stone reinforce that vantage point determines what you can see. The rose-garden parable extends this to tolerance and seeing common humanity across differences.

For a treasure hunter, the heavily repeated emphasis on physical standpoint, vantage, angle of view, "where you stand," and standing up to see "several acres" of a garden could be read as a soft hint to consider the searcher's literal viewpoint or elevation in the field. The author keeps it explicitly metaphorical (us-vs-them, common humanity), and per the project's hard rule that clues are literal with no cipher, this should be treated as an interpretive nudge at most, not a code.

Tiffany & Co.

Founded 1837 in NYC by Charles Lewis Tiffany; flagship at 727 Fifth Avenue. Tiffany president Henry B. Platt named "tsavorite" (after Kenya's Tsavo) and ran the 1974 US launch. Tanzania / Tsavo (tsavorite source region)

Tsavorite is a vivid green grossular garnet discovered by Campbell Bridges (1967 Tanzania; 1970–71 Kenya, near Tsavo National Park). Henry B. Platt of Tiffany named it in 1973–74 for Kenya's Tsavo, and Tiffany's 1974 US campaign made it famous. Gem-quality tsavorite is found essentially only along the Kenya–Tanzania border. The strongest US thread is the "Lion of Merelani," the world's largest faceted tsavorite (116.76 carats), precision-cut in 2018 by Victor Tuzlukov at Bridges Tsavorite's facility in Tucson, Arizona — the largest fine gemstone ever cut in the US — then donated to the Smithsonian NMNH (on display since April 2023). The "Lion" name derives from the Merelani district, not a lion legend.
