George Washington's Jelly Glass

Lesson: Share Your Story

Chapter 17 · book pages 151-158

Summary

Chapter 17 features George Washington's crystal jelly (dessert) glass and uses Washington's life as a meditation on the value of documenting and sharing one's own story. The author opens with two epigraph quotes (Madeleine Taylor and Philip Nunu) about storytelling, then argues we should all share our stories because doing so empowers us, inspires others, and preserves history for future generations: "Do not leave the history of your life for others to tell."

The author recalls fourth-grade American History class, where a heavy, "whitewashed" textbook taught the cherry tree story — young George chopping down a cherry tree and confessing because he "could never tell a lie." He reveals this is a "boldfaced lie" invented by Mason Locke Weems, who wrote the first Washington biography a year after Washington died and added the cherry tree tale only in the fifth edition to sell more books. One hundred and eighty-three years later, that marketing fiction had become "fact" in the textbook. The author contrasts this with the real, well-documented Washington, whom he calls "arguably the most meticulous writer of all" the founding fathers — Washington documented "every part he could for future generations."

The chapter does not flatter Washington: he owned or managed 317 slaves, possibly wore slaves' teeth as dentures, neither he nor Martha could understand why slaves wanted to flee, he used his power to acquire vast property, was an "untalented and unsuccessful farmer," took on heavy debts for fine furnishings, and was consumed by appearance and reputation. Yet his 67-year life "bent the trajectory of our modern world." Statistically he should have died young — his father and five brothers died young, and he survived polio, scarlet fever, yellow fever, malaria, smallpox, tuberculosis, dysentery, and other illnesses, with doctors repeatedly making "final preparations" for his death.

The military narrative covers Washington's lack of formal training, the bad early losses against the British at New York in 1776, and the famous Delaware River crossing on Christmas night. Washington divided his troops into three groups to recross the river; two groups could not cross because the water "had become a sheet of ice," and only Washington's boat made it. Pro-English locals warned the German (Hessian) mercenaries, but the plan was so improbable the Germans dismissed the spies as "sheer madness," leading to an astonishing victory — "the first of many improbable triumphs." Over eight years of war Washington was never hit despite standing within cannon shot in full-color regalia; newspapers "from New York to London" wrote of his mythic invincibility. At the Battle of Monmouth (late June 1778), after second-in-command Charles Lee's half-hearted attack collapsed into retreat, Washington rode his large white steed to the front in an open farmer's field and rallied his men, turning a rout into victory (his horse later died from the heat). He became the lone consensus choice for first President.

The treasure-item section explains that when Washington moved to Philadelphia as President, items were purchased for dinners and entertaining; the crystal dessert glass shown is one of those, "now in our treasure." It was used during his presidency, then brought back to Mount Vernon, Virginia after his two terms. He marked finer possessions with a wax seal — the "Washington family green wax seal" is on the bottom of the jelly glass. It is one of only two known to exist; this glass has been displayed in more than ten museums, including the Gerald Ford and Herbert Hoover presidential libraries, and its sister glass resides at the Mount Vernon Museum. A facing-page sidebar reads: "This English rose patterned jelly glass was owned by George Washington." Washington died at Mount Vernon, leaving tens of thousands of pages of journals and letters. The author recommends Ron Chernow's "Washington: A Life" (Chernow also wrote the Alexander Hamilton biography that inspired the Broadway musical "Hamilton").

The chapter closes with direct treasure-hunting advice: hunters should share their stories with one another, document their adventures, and journal important discoveries and experiences. Storytelling "weaves a quilt that blankets us all," and the treasures we seek "are not a zero-sum game." It then previews Chapter 18 on Andrew Carnegie, "the wealthiest man in world," whose innovations in philanthropy created a "blueprint for giving" lasting over a hundred years.

The Treasure(s)

George Washington's crystal jelly (dessert) glass. A small, trumpet-shaped late-18th-century cut lead-crystal dessert glass (c. 1790-1800), described by its dealer as the "English rose pattern" and bearing the Washington family green wax seal on the bottom. It is one of only two such glasses known to still exist. It was used during Washington's presidency in Philadelphia, then brought back to his home at Mount Vernon, Virginia. The facing-page sidebar reads: "This English rose patterned jelly glass was owned by George Washington."

Such glasses were part of the elaborate "pyramid" dessert centerpieces fashionable among elite households, in which jellies were served in individual cut-glass vessels stacked on tiered salvers. Washington ordered jelly glasses from London as early as August 1759. The book states this glass has been displayed in more than ten museums, including the Gerald R. Ford and Herbert Hoover presidential libraries, while its "sister glass" resides at the Mount Vernon Museum. Notably, the authoritative Mount Vernon catalog lists the maker as "Unknown" and origin as "Possibly England" or "Possibly Ireland" — so "English rose" is a modern dealer/marketing label rather than a documented maker or town.

Themes & the Lesson

The subtitle "Share Your Story" works on two levels. As a life philosophy it celebrates Washington's meticulous self-documentation — preferring an honest, human record over "whitewashed" textbooks and inventions like the cherry tree myth. As direct instruction to treasure hunters it asks them to share their stories with one another, document their adventures, and journal their discoveries: storytelling "weaves a quilt that blankets us all," and the treasures sought "are not a zero-sum game."

A secondary theme is epistemic skepticism: the Weems cherry tree fable warns readers to verify "received facts" against primary sources rather than trust marketing fiction that has hardened into accepted truth. This is community-building and methodology advice, not a geographic riddle — though it does reinforce the book's stated approach of literal clues and "clues by association" rather than ciphers.

Research Findings

Mount Vernon, Virginia (and the Mount Vernon Museum)

Mount Vernon is Washington's former plantation home in Fairfax County, Virginia, on the Potomac River ~15 miles south of Washington, D.C. (3200 Mount Vernon Memorial Highway). It is the most-visited historic estate in the U.S., owned and operated since 1858 by the private Mount Vernon Ladies' Association. Its on-site Donald W. Reynolds Museum and Education Center holds 700+ artifacts. Jellies were elite individual desserts served in cut-glass jelly glasses; Washington ordered such glassware from London as early as August 1759. Mount Vernon's own example (c. 1790-1800, trumpet bowl, cut bellflowers/husks) was acquired in 1892 via a Thomas Birch's Sons auction in Philadelphia and is currently "not on view." The "sister glass" descended from Martha Washington's will to her granddaughter Eleanor "Nelly" Parke Custis Lewis, who moved to nearby Woodlawn Plantation; that glass passed through Philadelphia (1891), New York (Parke-Bernet, 1950), collectors, and is now offered by M.S. Rau Antiques in New Orleans, having been exhibited at eleven institutions including the Ford Museum, Hoover Library, and Brandywine River Museum.

Gerald R. Ford Presidential Library (and Museum)

The Ford institution is split into two physically separate sites under one director: the LIBRARY (archives) on the University of Michigan North Campus in Ann Arbor, MI, and the MUSEUM (public exhibits) in Ford's hometown of Grand Rapids, MI (303 Pearl St NW), about 130 miles apart — the only NARA presidential library geographically separated from its museum. The c. 1790 "English Rose pattern" Washington jelly glass was exhibited at the Ford Museum & Library among many other institutions. There is no evidence the glass is currently held there or that the Ford institution has any special tie to the treasure beyond being one of many borrowing venues; it points faintly to Michigan, but it is just one node in a long exhibition list.

Herbert Hoover Presidential Library

A real institution at 210 Parkside Drive, West Branch, Iowa (Cedar County, east of Iowa City off I-80), on the grounds of the Herbert Hoover National Historic Site (NPS) — birthplace cottage, Quaker meetinghouse, gravesite, and restored tallgrass prairie. (Currently closed for renovations until summer 2026.) The Washington jelly glass was exhibited here on three occasions. The Hoover National Historic Site's tallgrass prairie with walking paths is the closest plausible "boots on the ground" public-land feature near this name, but any focus there is speculation; the library is best read as one corroborating exhibition stop that introduces Iowa as a faint geographic flag.

George Washington

Documented owner of the treasure item. He began as a professional surveyor (commissioned 1749 for Culpeper County, VA; ~200 surveys / 60,000+ acres by 1752) via the Fairfax family of Belvoir, and became one of America's largest land speculators (~60,000-70,000 acres, much in the trans-Appalachian West). His meticulously documented western tracts lie along the Ohio and Great Kanawha rivers in present-day West Virginia, including "Round Bottom" (surveyed 1771) and a 250-acre tract on "Burning Springs Run." Crucially for a public-land hunt, the George Washington National Forest (renamed from Shenandoah National Forest in 1932; now part of the George Washington and Jefferson National Forests, ~1.8M acres in VA/WV/KY) is named directly for him — the strongest constraint-compliant association from this chapter.

Battle of Monmouth (June 1778)

Fought June 28, 1778, in Monmouth County, New Jersey, between Washington's Continental Army and Sir Henry Clinton's British rearguard — the largest single-day battle of the Revolution (~25,000 men), fought in brutal heat across open farmland. After Charles Lee's attack faltered, Washington rode forward and personally rallied the retreating troops, matching the chapter's "large open farmer's field." The battlefield is preserved as Monmouth Battlefield State Park (1,818 acres, ~40.273°N, 74.314°W) on the Manalapan / Freehold Township border. Three original 18th-century farmhouses survive (Sutfin, Rhea-Applegate, Craig). Molly Pitcher (Mary Ludwig Hays McCauley) is memorialized at the park but buried in Carlisle, Cumberland County, Pennsylvania.

Mason Locke Weems ("Parson Weems")

Episcopal minister, traveling book peddler, and author of the first Washington biography ("The Life of Washington," 1800). The cherry tree anecdote did not appear until the 5th edition in 1806 — the archetype of an invented-but-believed historical "fact." Born in Anne Arundel County, Maryland; educated at the Kent County Free School in Chestertown (later Washington College); settled in Dumfries, Virginia, where he ran a bookstore (the surviving Weems-Botts Museum). He died in Beaufort, South Carolina (1825). Grant Wood's 1939 painting "Parson Weems' Fable" hangs at the Amon Carter Museum in Fort Worth, Texas. Adjacent Prince William Forest Park (NPS) near Dumfries is genuine public land (speculative association).

Philadelphia, Pennsylvania (Washington's presidential residence / "President's House")

Philadelphia was the national capital 1790-1800. Washington lived at the "President's House" (524-530 Market Street, one block north of Independence Hall) from November 1790 to March 1797 — the setting for the elite presidential dining where the jelly glass was used. The glass carries a documented Philadelphia provenance (Thomas Birch's Son Sales Catalogue 683, 5-6 April 1892, Lot 848). In 2010 the NPS opened the open-air "President's House: Freedom and Slavery" pavilion on the exact footprint within Independence National Historical Park, adjacent to the Liberty Bell Center. While federal public land, this is a dense, high-security urban monument — an implausible hide site; Philadelphia is best read as a provenance/biographical association.

English rose pattern (cut crystal glassware)

The jelly glass is a small, trumpet-shaped late-18th-century cut lead-crystal dessert glass (c. 1790-1800), used to build the era's "pyramid" dessert centerpieces. "English Rose pattern" is a dealer/auction-house descriptor (M.S. Rau / 1stDibs) for its scalloped rim and floral/bellflower cut border. The authoritative Mount Vernon catalog does NOT confirm an English-rose origin: it lists the maker as "Unknown" and origin as "Possibly England" OR "Possibly Ireland" (the Irish/Waterford cut-glass tradition is a real alternative). English lead crystal traces to George Ravenscroft's 1676 formula, with cutting centers at London, Stourbridge, Newcastle upon Tyne, and Bristol. Because "English rose" is a modern marketing label, no specific English town should be read as a literal place clue.
