Sing Your Own Special Song

Lesson: Finding Treasures Along the Way

Chapter 23 · book pages 195-198

Summary

Chapter 23 is the final main chapter of There's Treasure Inside and serves as the book's philosophical conclusion. It opens with two epigraphs about journey over completion — one from motivational author Greg Anderson ("Joy is found not in finishing an activity but in doing it") and one from the Japanese haiku master Bashō Matsuo ("Every day is a journey, and the journey itself is home").

The chapter's treasure item is an ancient Egyptian faience bead necklace featuring a leopard's head bead. The author recounts how, while sourcing antiquities, he crossed paths with Allan Anawati, an antiquities dealer of over forty years who continued a family business his father started. Anawati mentioned he had a collection of loose Egyptian faience beads over three thousand years old and suggested the author could string them into a necklace himself, the way it might have looked over three millennia ago. When the author admitted he had no idea how, Anawati offered to send a picture of a very similar necklace from the Carnegie Museum in Pittsburgh to demonstrate the design.

The author then describes "creative nights" — a weekly ritual he shares with his wife Kimberly after their kids are asleep, in which they write and record songs, paint, scribble poems, act out movie scenes, play at-home escape-room games, and create puzzles for friends. They added a new activity: recreating the 3,000-year-old necklace. The author and Kimberly arranged the faience necklace with its leopard's head bead to look as it might have appeared before 1000 BC. He stresses it is "not the most expensive item in our treasure" but "the most personal item I've included," because the act of working with the beads together is a cherished memory and one of the "priceless treasures" he acquired along the way.

The author explains the faience-making process (invented ~5,000 years ago in Egypt): sand made malleable with salt and water, shaped, glazed with a dull color, then fired in intense heat, where the materials fuse, the glaze saturates, and the bead emerges bright and lustrous, holding its beauty for thousands of years. He uses this as a metaphor: "These faience beads are not unlike you and me… The intensity of life's experiences forges us," making us resilient, radiant, and beautiful — which is why treasures found along a journey can exceed those at the end of a rainbow.

The chapter then introduces "The Five Gifts of Hathor," an ancient Egyptian daily gratitude practice. Although Egyptian society had unequal castes, all castes were said to share two things: their homeland and a daily practice of gratitude. The five gifts were represented by the five fingers of the left hand; each day Egyptians counted at least five blessings. They believed gratitude guided a person toward a balanced, happy life, while rejecting it led to corruption and negative outcomes.

The closing pages deliver the book's core message. Gratitude for each day is "perhaps the most powerful ability a treasure hunter can possess," more impressive than intelligence or deductive reasoning — "a skill that allows you to have already won, even before you begin." Valuing each step of the journey over the final destination is "what truly matters"; "Our eureka is wherever we choose to find it"; and "Treasure is all around, just waiting for you and me to notice." The author frames all eight billion humans as lifelong treasure hunters whose hunts "intersect… intertwine… connect us all." He ties the practice back to the book's opening meditation on the word "treasure," and ends with the idea that he and Kimberly strive to "make our own special music" — a melody about being present, embracing each moment, exploring, discovering, and growing together. A sidebar shows an image of the "creative night" necklace.

The Treasure(s)

The featured item is an ancient Egyptian faience bead necklace — turquoise/blue and coral/red beads, a central pendant with tassels, and a distinctive leopard's head bead. The loose beads are over 3,000 years old and were sourced from antiquities dealer Allan Anawati; the author and his wife Kimberly strung and arranged them during a "creative night" to look as the necklace might have appeared before 1000 BC, using a comparable piece from the Carnegie Museum in Pittsburgh as the design template.

The author is explicit that this is "not the most expensive item" in the treasure but "the most personal item I've included." Its value to the book is symbolic: the shared act of making it together is itself one of the "priceless treasures" he gathered along the journey — the perfect capstone object for a chapter whose lesson is "Finding Treasures Along the Way."

Themes & the Lesson

"Finding Treasures Along the Way" reframes the entire hunt: the journey itself — the experiences, relationships, and growth gathered en route — is the true treasure, not the final box. Two devices carry the message: the faience "forged by fire" metaphor (the intensity of life's experiences fuses and beautifies us, just as kiln heat transforms dull beads into something lustrous) and the "Five Gifts of Hathor" daily-gratitude practice (count five blessings each day on the five fingers of the left hand). The explicit instruction is to value each step of the journey more than the destination and to practice daily gratitude, "since treasure is all around."

For the hunt, the chapter's recurring number is notable: FIVE (five gifts, five fingers) literally echoes the five treasure boxes Jon Collins-Black hid, one per state, with the Lion's Share being the largest. Per the author's hard rule that clues are literal and there is no grand cipher, "five" reads as thematic resonance, not an instruction to play a letter or syllable game. The closing "make our own special music" line ties the chapter title to the broader music/song motif (see the Cass Elliot signal below).

Research Findings

Carnegie Museum, Pittsburgh

The "Carnegie Museum" cited in Chapter 23 is the Carnegie Museum of Natural History (CMNH) in the Oakland neighborhood of Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, founded 1895/1896 by steel magnate and philanthropist Andrew Carnegie. Its ancient-Egypt collection (~2,500 objects) is housed in the Walton Hall of Ancient Egypt; the first accessioned object was an Egyptian coffin/mummy of a Chantress of Amun, and much of the collection was acquired via Egypt Exploration Fund excavations Carnegie funded. The collection includes beaded necklaces of shell, faience (a fired quartz ceramic the Egyptians called "tjehenet," glazed bright blue/green), and gessoed wood, plus broad-collar (usekh) faience-bead necklaces — consistent with the chapter's claim that Anawati used "a very similar necklace from the Carnegie Museum" as the template, though no single specific catalog object could be verified as the exact model. The museum's signature Egyptian piece is the "Carnegie Boat," a ~9-meter Twelfth-Dynasty funerary boat of Senwosret III donated by Carnegie in 1901. Walton Hall closed in 2023 for conservation. This subject ties directly back to Chapter 18 (Andrew Carnegie's Emerald): Carnegie is the recurring thread — a Scottish immigrant who settled in Allegheny, PA and built his steel fortune in Pittsburgh.

The Five Gifts of Hathor

The "Five Gifts of Hathor" is an ancient Egyptian gratitude practice associated with the goddess Hathor (joy, love, music, fertility, sky; cult center at Dendera). The canonical online treatment is by Joshua J. Mark on World History Encyclopedia, which notes the practice is "poorly attested" — surviving largely through oral tradition rather than firm textual/archaeological evidence. In the described ritual a priest or priestess grasps a person's left forearm and asks them to name "the five things you would miss the most if you were to die right now," then raises the left hand and says "These are the five gifts of Hathor," mapping the five fingers to five daily blessings. It was tied especially to poor peasant farmers (the left hand held grain while the right cut it, keeping the "five gifts" always in view). The core motif is purely numerical/symbolic: FIVE and the goddess Hathor. There is no embedded geography in the concept; its only intrinsic physical places are in Egypt (Dendera, Thebes). Hathor-related artifacts in US museums (Met in NYC; Rosicrucian Egyptian Museum in San Jose, CA; Glencairn Museum in Bryn Athyn, PA) are indoor institutions, not hide sites.

Egyptian faience (leopard-head bead necklace)

Egyptian faience is a non-clay, quartz-based ceramic with a vitreous alkaline glaze — the world's first synthetic glaze material. Its characteristic blue-green/turquoise color comes from copper compounds; Egyptians called it tjehnet ("brilliant/dazzling"), associating it with the sun, the Nile, and rebirth. It was used from the Predynastic Period onward for beads, amulets, "mummy beads," and figurines, and was mass-produced in molds, making it available across social classes. The specific comparable object — an ancient Egyptian faience bead necklace with a leopard-head spacer — is in the United States at the Worcester Art Museum (WAM) in Worcester, Massachusetts. It dates to Egypt's New Kingdom; collector records (Laura Norcross Marrs) reported it as coming from the Valley of the Kings, and it was featured in WAM's 2022–2023 exhibition "Jewels of the Nile." Provenance carries notable association: the Marrs collection (donated 1926) was assembled by Kingsmill Marrs and Laura Norcross Marrs (daughter of Boston mayor Otis Norcross), who befriended archaeologist Howard Carter on a 1908 Egypt trip. Comparable faience holdings also exist at the Met and Brooklyn Museum (NYC), the MFA Boston, and the Glencairn Museum (Bryn Athyn, PA).

Hathor (Egyptian goddess)

Hathor is a major Egyptian deity — goddess of the sky, love, beauty, joy, motherhood, music and dance — usually depicted as a cow or a woman with cow's horns framing a sun disk. Her name means "House of Horus." She is strongly associated with music and celebration: her signature instruments were the sistrum (a sacred rattle) and the menat/menit necklace (a beaded necklace that also rattled), both used to accompany song and dance — the direct link to Chapter 23's theme. The "Five Gifts of Hathor" gratitude practice is attributed to her cult but is poorly attested historically. Her principal temple was at Dendera; she was also "Mistress of Turquoise" and patron goddess of miners (Serabit el-Khadim in Sinai, Timna in modern Israel). None of her temples, mines, or cult centers are in the United States. A GNIS-style search returns no US town, mountain, or feature named "Hathor." The Grand Canyon's famous cluster of Egyptian-named buttes (Isis, Osiris, Horus, Ra, Set, Cheops) notably does not include a "Hathor" feature, so Hathor specifically does not point there.

Matsuo Bashō (Bashō Matsuo)

Matsuo Bashō (1644–1694) was a Japanese haiku master, born in Iga-ueno near Kyoto, best known for Oku no Hosomichi ("The Narrow Road to the Deep North"), a haibun (prose-and-haiku) travel diary. He undertook the ~1,500-mile journey on foot in 1689, traveling roughly 156 days through Japan's northern interior (Oku). The epigraph — "Every day is a journey, and the journey itself is home" — is a condensed English rendering of the work's opening passage ("The months and days are travelers of eternity… their homes are wherever their travels take them"). It is genuinely from Bashō's most famous travel work and is squarely about the journey being its own destination. All physical memorials honoring Bashō are in Japan (Tokyo's Koto ward, Iga-ueno birthplace, Hiraizumi); targeted searches found no Bashō monument, memorial, or place-name in the United States.

Andrew Carnegie

Andrew Carnegie (1835–1919) was a Scottish-American steel industrialist and philanthropist, born in Dunfermline, Scotland; his family emigrated in 1848 to Allegheny City (now part of Pittsburgh), PA. He endowed ~2,510 libraries, Carnegie Hall (NYC), Carnegie Mellon University, and the Carnegie Museums of Pittsburgh. He founded the Carnegie Museum of Natural History in 1896; its very first accession was his donation of an Egyptian mummy and coffin of a chantress (temple singer) of Amun (~1069 BC) — a detail that dovetails with Chapter 23's title "Sing Your Own Special Song." He personally funded Egypt Exploration Fund acquisitions and in 1901 purchased the 12th-Dynasty funerary boat of Senwosret III. His most famous US-geography link is the dinosaur Diplodocus carnegii ("Dippy," the "star-spangled dinosaur," first toe bone found July 4, 1899) discovered at Sheep Creek Quarry in Albany County, Wyoming. Carnegie died at "Shadowbrook," his summer estate in Lenox, Massachusetts (the Berkshires), and is buried at Sleepy Hollow Cemetery, NY.
