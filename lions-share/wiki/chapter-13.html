Lesson: Be Like Mike

Chapter 13 · book pages 115-122

Summary

Chapter 13 centers on the treasure item: a 1986 Fleer Michael Jordan rookie card, #57, graded PSA 9 MINT, serial number 26670045. The chapter opens with two epigraphs — Norman Cousins (“He who keeps his cool best wins.”) and Michael Jordan (“Just play. Have fun. Enjoy the game.”) — and explains the chapter title “Be Like Mike,” a Gatorade ad slogan from the early 1990s, placing it culturally alongside “Got Milk?” and “Once You Pop You Can't Stop.” Jordan is framed as “the greatest basketball player to ever play the game,” a “basketball 007—never stirred, never shaken,” at his best under the brightest lights, who won every championship he ever played in.

The author then catalogs Jordan's achievements (6 NBA championships, 5 MVPs, 22 NBA trophies — more than any player ever, 9 All-Defensive Team selections, led the league in scoring 10 of 13 seasons, most points per game in history) and quotes Barack Obama's Presidential Medal of Freedom remarks: “Because Michael Jordan is the Michael Jordan of greatness.”

The chapter is heavily autobiographical. The author states plainly, “I grew up in North Carolina,” and describes a childhood steeped in University of North Carolina Tar Heels basketball — TVs wheeled into classrooms to watch tournament games, people talking hoops “at work, when they met around town, and even at church.” He watched his first games with his mom and dad “around the age of six,” and was nine when Michael Jordan was a freshman at UNC, putting the author's birth around 1972-73. He recounts jumping around in ecstasy at Jordan's game-winning shot in the 1982 NCAA championship, and notes that “two years later” Jordan came to “our local high school” for a pick-up game and autograph signing, where the author was the first person in the gym, dropped off hours early. He reminisces about buying 12-packs of Fleer NBA cards (with a sticker and gum) for a dollar at the convenience store but never keeping them long.

The card was acquired for the treasure from PWCC, described as the largest sports card broker in the world, whose owner Brent Huigens praised it as “arguably the finest PSA 9 Fleer Michael Jordan rookie card we've brokered,” noting it “shows better than most PSA 10s” and earned PWCC's “Superior” certification (pack fresh, 50-50 alignment, dagger-sharp corners, GEM MINT stock). A PSA 9 sells for $20,000-$75,000; a PSA 10 is worth hundreds of thousands. The author notes the card was pictured “on the previous spread (page 112).”

The author describes his father erecting a metal rim with netting in the driveway when he was eight, where he played for hours pretending to be Larry Bird (corner fadeaway), Magic Johnson (hook shot in the lane), and Michael Jordan (reverse layup buzzer-beater). He also mentions that when not playing ball he was “digging for rocks or bugs or searching for snakes or spiders.”

The narrative climaxes with the author's “most embarrassing moment.” At thirteen he tried out for the junior-high team (50 boys, 12 spots), showed up the first day in blue jeans having forgotten his gym shorts, and made the team as the very last pick (#12). He rode the bench for two-thirds of the season. In a home game against the conference's worst team (down two injured stars), preceded by a pep rally and the biggest home crowd of the year, his team built a huge lead (10, 15, then 21 points by quarter). With 2:34 left and up 28, the coach put him in. He grabbed a rebound off a missed free throw, dribbled confidently past defenders, and laid it in — for the other team, having run the wrong direction. The referee told him, “Son, you just scored a basket for the other team.” This becomes the chapter's central lesson.

The chapter closes by noting Jordan himself failed to make varsity at fifteen in Wilmington, NC, was sent to JV, worked harder than anyone, and became a top national recruit. The author reframes his own aspiration not as being the best at something but as becoming “the very best version of myself” — a more attentive father, loving husband, generous friend. He previews Chapter 14's theme of embracing failure (“failure is a wise companion”).

Themes & the Lesson

“Be Like Mike” functions as meta-advice for the treasure hunt itself. The author uses his own catastrophic mistake — getting overexcited, rushing off, and running the wrong direction to score for the opposing team — as a cautionary tale. The lesson: stay calm, cool, and collected when searching; do not let excitement override judgment or sense of direction. He instructs the reader directly: “If you ever find yourself getting too excited about something, such as the prospects of finding the items I've hidden, make sure you remember this story... Take a breath. Reframe and relax... Remaining calm will better suit your efforts.”

The literal, practical implication is that searchers should pause and verify direction and orientation rather than charging ahead impulsively — possibly a hint that the solve requires careful, deliberate orientation (not rushing). Paired with the Norman Cousins epigraph (“He who keeps his cool best wins”), the chapter is consistent with the author's stated stance that clues are literal and there is no grand cipher.

1986 Fleer Michael Jordan Rookie Card #57

The 1986-87 Fleer #57 Michael Jordan rookie card is the most iconic modern sports card in the hobby. It was released in fall 1986 by the Fleer Corporation, which had secured the exclusive NBA license in 1985. The set (132 player cards + 11 stickers) was the first wax-pack basketball set since 1981-82 Topps, with the recognizable red/white/blue American-themed borders. Fleer reportedly printed ~250,000 of each card (~6,400 cases). The card depicts Jordan as a Chicago Bull; high-grade examples sell for six- and seven-figure sums.

Production/company origin: Fleer Corporation was founded in Philadelphia in 1885 by Frank H. Fleer (the family confectionery business traces to 1849). Fleer was headquartered in Philadelphia's Fairmount neighborhood and is famous as the inventor of the first commercial bubble gum: “Blibber-Blubber” (1906) and the successful “Dubble Bubble” (1928, invented by 23-year-old cost accountant Walter Diemer), first sold at a Philadelphia candy store in December 1928. The card's production lineage is firmly Philadelphia, PA.

Subject (Michael Jordan) origin: Born in Brooklyn, NY (Feb 17, 1963), but raised in Wilmington, North Carolina (New Hanover County). He attended Laney High School in Wilmington, then the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill under coach Dean Smith, before joining the Chicago Bulls. The Michael Jordan Discovery Gallery opened in 1995 inside the Cape Fear Museum in Wilmington, NC.

Michael Jordan

Michael Jeffrey Jordan (b. Feb 17, 1963) was born at Cumberland Hospital in the Fort Greene neighborhood of Brooklyn, New York City. In 1968 his family relocated to Wilmington, North Carolina, his lifelong “hometown,” where he grew up at 4647 Gordon Road (a split-level home near U.S. Highway 117) and attended Emsley A. Laney High School. He famously was cut from the varsity team as a sophomore, became a McDonald's All-American as a senior, then played for the University of North Carolina Tar Heels in Chapel Hill (1981-1984), hitting the game-winning shot in the 1982 NCAA national championship and winning national Player of the Year in 1984.

He left UNC after his junior year, was drafted 3rd overall in the 1984 NBA Draft by the Chicago Bulls, and played the bulk of his 15-season career in Chicago, winning six NBA titles (1991-93, 1996-98). Later he became principal owner of the Charlotte Hornets (in North Carolina) and is involved in NASCAR (23XI Racing). His connection to NC is strong and recurring: born Brooklyn, raised Wilmington, college Chapel Hill, later NBA ownership in Charlotte.

Physical memorials/landmarks tied to him: “The Spirit,” a 12-foot bronze statue located at the United Center, 1901 W. Madison Street, Chicago, IL (indoors in the east-addition atrium since 2017). In Wilmington, the Cape Fear Museum holds a dedicated Michael Jordan display case (items donated by his mother Deloris Jordan); a larger dedicated Jordan museum was explored by New Hanover County in 2023 but the family declined to proceed.

Norman Cousins

Norman Cousins (June 24, 1915 - November 30, 1990) was an American essayist, editor, political journalist, peace advocate, and pioneer of mind-body medicine. He is the attributed source of the Chapter 13 epigraph “He who keeps his cool best wins.” Cousins was born to Jewish immigrant parents in West Hoboken, New Jersey (later part of Union City, NJ). He attended Theodore Roosevelt High School in the Bronx, NY, and earned a bachelor's degree from Teachers College, Columbia University in New York City.

His defining career was as editor of the Saturday Review from 1942 to 1972, working out of New York City. He was a prominent advocate for world peace and world federalism, and late in life became famous for self-treating a serious illness with humor and high-dose vitamin C, recounted in his bestseller Anatomy of an Illness (1979). In 1978 he joined the UCLA faculty as an adjunct professor in Los Angeles, where he spent his final years. He died of heart failure on November 30, 1990, in Los Angeles, and was buried at Mt. Lebanon Cemetery in New Jersey. His name is memorialized institutionally rather than geographically — e.g., the Norman Cousins Center for Psychoneuroimmunology at UCLA's Semel Institute.
