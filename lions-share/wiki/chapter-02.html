The 100 Rings of Tuyet Nguyet

Lesson: Don't Wing It

Chapter 2 · book pages 26-37

Summary

Chapter 2 is built around the theme of preparation versus improvisation. It opens with two epigraphs about planning ahead — Mark Twain ("It usually takes me more than three weeks to prepare a good impromptu speech") and Jackie Joyner-Kersee ("It's better to look ahead and prepare, than to look back and regret").

The author then tells a self-deprecating personal story: after college he found a sales job (at an environmentally friendly health-products company) listed in The Daily Tar Heel, the student newspaper of UNC Chapel Hill, located via the Dewey Decimal System. Untrained and "winging it," he botched a water-filter demonstration in front of an elderly man with a kind face, which spawned recurring nightmares about being unprepared. This sets up the lesson: success comes from focus, preparation, and attention to detail.

The chapter's central figure is Tuyet Nguyet, a Vietnamese investigative journalist who became one of the most influential figures in Asian art. In 1970, at age 36, she and her husband Stephen Markbreiter founded Arts of Asia magazine, which she edited and published for its entire run. Over forty years she amassed a collection of 100 ancient Southeast Asian gold rings considered the finest of their kind; in July 2017 Arts of Asia ran a special edition dedicated to them. Twelve of these gold rings plus an additional gold box acquired by Nguyet are now in the author's treasure. The featured rings span the pre-Angkor period (1st–8th centuries AD, from what is today Vietnam), the Khmer Empire (9th–15th centuries AD), and Champa (14th century).

A long passage explores the rings' cultural and religious meaning. In Hindu myth the creator Brahma was born from a golden egg and made the universe from a golden embryo, so wearing more gold reflected the "light of creation" and prepared the soul for a superior reincarnation. In Buddhism the lotus symbolizes resilience and evolution, and "a blue lotus indicates that knowledge and wisdom have conjoined to prepare a path to enlightenment where secrets are revealed." Chinese emissaries' accounts describe pre-Angkor and Khmer courts shimmering with gold: a tall golden tower at the center of Angkor, golden lions guarding a golden bridge that stretched across the river to its entrance, kings draped in pearls, gold bracelets, and a golden sword, and elephants with tusks sheathed in gold.

The chapter then narrates the rise and fall of Angkor — the largest city in the world (population near one million), with a land mass "the size of Los Angeles," over 2,000 temples, and Angkor Wat, the largest religious center in the world, "over four times the size of the Vatican City in Rome." It notes Angkor was where Tomb Raider and Indiana Jones and the Temple of Doom were filmed, and was swallowed by jungle for three centuries after the Khmer were defeated by the Siams in 1431. Crucially for treasure-hunt resonance, it states that because Khmer jewelry was destroyed on cremation pyres, "rings like ours that survive today were discovered mostly in hidden caches, secreted away within temple walls." It also recounts the Champa, a resilient maritime culture on the eastern shore of present-day Vietnam, who repelled the Khmer, briefly fell under Khmer rule in 1145, then in an unexpected twist sailed out, defeated Angkor, and held it for four years.

The chapter closes by tying the cultures together — "None of these cultures were winging it" — and delivers explicit, meta-level advice to treasure hunters: don't wing it, don't rush through these pages, get to know the stories, consider their content, and don't dismiss any details. The author confesses that details are his greatest weakness ("I have always preferred a 36,000-foot view"). He then transitions to the next chapter by introducing Seth Gould, the craftsman who, "with metal and fire, forged each of our five treasure boxes" over the course of eighteen months — "a true master of details" (he is the subject of A Puzzle Box, a Magnifying Glass, & the Mysterious Egg).

The Treasure(s)

The featured items are drawn from the celebrated gold-ring collection of Tuyet Nguyet and Stephen Markbreiter:

  • Twelve ancient Southeast Asian gold rings from the Tuyet Nguyet collection — spanning the pre-Angkor period (1st–8th c. AD), the Khmer Empire (9th–15th c. AD), and Champa (14th c.).
  • One gold box from the same collection, acquired by Tuyet Nguyet.

Notable individual pieces called out in the chapter include a pre-Angkor gold ring (~1 oz); the largest ring — a Khmer lotus-bell ring (1.25+ oz) with a bluish-pink pink-sapphire centerpiece; and a Champa 14th-century ring set with a glowing blue sapphire. The objects' documented provenance runs through Sotheby's Hong Kong (the 2021 "Golden Splendour" sale), with image credits to "© Sotheby's Hong Kong Limited."

Themes & the Lesson

"Don't Wing It" is a literal instruction about preparation and attention to detail. The author uses the historical cultures (Hindu afterlife preparation, Champa's study of attack and defense, the Khmer's meticulous building of Angkor) and Tuyet Nguyet's decades of disciplined research as positive models, contrasted with his own embarrassing "winging it" sales story. The lesson is then turned directly on the reader as a solving strategy: Tuyet Nguyet "might suggest not to wing this... not to rush through these pages... to get to know these stories, to consider their content, and to not dismiss any details."

This is effectively an authorial directive that clues are embedded in the item and people histories, and that careful, thorough reading (not shortcuts or improvisation) is required.

Wordplay risk to flag: "Don't Wing It" could tempt a hunter to read "wing" as a bird or directional hint, but the author's framing is plainly about preparation. This is consistent with the project's hard constraint that clues are literal — there is no grand cipher.

Research Findings

Seth Gould (metalsmith who forged the five treasure boxes)

Seth Gould is the American metalsmith/blacksmith Jon Collins-Black commissioned to hand-forge all five "There's Treasure Inside" boxes. Per Gould's own site, the five mechanical puzzle boxes (forged steel/brass/silver, each requiring movements to open) were made during 2022–2023 to hold a treasure worth over $2 million. Collins-Black says he was inspired to hire Gould after seeing Gould's masterwork "Coffer" — a large all-metal puzzle box/strongbox Gould began during his first year at Penland (every screw and spring handmade, much forged from salvaged wrought iron), now held by the Smithsonian American Art Museum's Renwick Gallery.

Geography/biography: Gould grew up on Cape Cod, Massachusetts; earned a BFA in Metalsmithing/Jewelry Design from Maine College of Art (Portland, ME, 2009); studied colonial ironwork under Peter Ross; and was deeply influenced by the Musée le Secq des Tournelles ironwork museum in Rouen, France. He completed a Core Fellowship and a multi-year Resident Artist appointment at the Penland School of Craft in the North Carolina Blue Ridge Mountains, and once ran a shop in Cleveland, Ohio. He now lives and works from a home studio in Harwich, Massachusetts. His work is also collected by the National Ornamental Metal Museum (Memphis) and the Mint Museum (Charlotte, NC).

Timeline note: Because the boxes were commissioned and forged in 2022–2023 while Gould was still a Penland resident artist (his move back to Massachusetts appears to be 2023–2024), the boxes were almost certainly physically forged at or near Penland School of Craft in Mitchell County, NC.

Tuyet Nguyet

Tuyet Nguyet (1934–2020; the name means "Snow Moon") was a Vietnamese-born journalist, publisher, and art collector, born in Tan An province near the Mekong River. Before founding Arts of Asia (preview edition 1970; regular publication from 1971), she was a war correspondent/journalist for the Saigon Daily News and later wrote for the South China Morning Post, Far Eastern Economic Review, Agence France Presse, and Modern Asia. In 1959 she married English architect Stephen Markbreiter and relocated to Hong Kong, where she lived the rest of her life. She is credited with persuading Sotheby's (1973) and Christie's (1984/86) to open Hong Kong offices. Her ~100-ring collection, assembled over ~40 years (1st–19th century, from Java, Khmer/Angkor, Burma/Pyu, Champa, Thailand, Vietnam, and Tibet), was sold primarily through Sotheby's Hong Kong ("Golden Splendour," 2021).

Her only concrete US geographic association: a 1955 scholarship to study journalism at Mundelein College in Chicago (B.A. 1958), a Catholic women's college on the far north side (Rogers Park / Edgewater, along Sheridan Road). Its landmark 14-story building (NRHP 1980; Chicago Landmark 2007) still stands as Loyola University Chicago's Mundelein Center.

Mark Twain

Mark Twain (Samuel Langhorne Clemens, 1835–1910) appears as the chapter's opening epigraph author. His life is anchored to several documented US locations, several of them formal public-land or public-historic-site designations. He was born in Florida, Missouri (now Mark Twain Birthplace State Historic Site); grew up in Hannibal, Missouri (Mark Twain Boyhood Home & Museum, a National Historic Landmark); lived in Hartford, Connecticut (the Mark Twain House & Museum, which contains Tiffany glass); and summered at Quarry Farm in Elmira, New York (relocated study on the Elmira College campus; buried in Woodlawn Cemetery). His breakthrough "Jumping Frog of Calaveras County" (1865) ties him to California Gold Country (Jackass Hill near Sonora, Tuolumne County; the Angels Hotel in Angels Camp, Calaveras County).

Most significant public-land tie: the Mark Twain National Forest in southern Missouri (~1.5M acres, established 1939, HQ in Rolla) — nine disconnected parcels across 29 counties, six ranger districts, and seven wilderness areas (Bell Mountain, Devils Backbone, Hercules-Glades, Irish, Paddy Creek, Piney Creek, Rockpile Mountain). It includes the Eleven Point National Scenic River, Greer Spring, and the Ozark Trail. Adjacent is Taum Sauk Mountain (Missouri's highest point, 1,772 ft, with Mina Sauk Falls).

Jackie Joyner-Kersee

Jackie Joyner-Kersee (b. March 3, 1962) is an American Olympic track-and-field legend, regarded as the greatest female heptathlete of all time (heptathlon world record, 7,291 points, 1988 Seoul). She won six Olympic medals across four Games: 1984 Los Angeles (silver), 1988 Seoul (gold heptathlon and long jump), 1992 Barcelona (gold heptathlon, bronze long jump), and 1996 Atlanta (bronze long jump). She was born and raised in East St. Louis, Illinois, and competed at UCLA (1980–1985).

A cross-chapter detail: her grandmother named her "Jacqueline" after Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis (subject of Chapter 10), hoping she would be the "first lady" of something. Her Olympic golds also link to the 1996 Atlanta Games (Chapter 16). Her physical legacy is the Jackie Joyner-Kersee Center (opened 2000–2001) at 101 Jackie Joyner Kersee Circle in East St. Louis — a developed urban campus, not public wildland.

Angkor Wat / Khmer Empire

Angkor Wat is the largest religious monument in the world, centerpiece of the Khmer Empire's capital at Angkor (802–1431 AD), near Siem Reap, Cambodia. The "golden lions guarded a golden bridge" imagery traces to Zhou Daguan, the 13th-century Chinese emissary who described the palace reached by a golden bridge flanked by two golden lions, with a central golden tower (the Bayon). Guardian lions and naga-balustraded causeways are the architectural source of the "hidden caches within temple walls" motif. The Khmer/Angkor-period (9th-c.) royal-court rings in Nguyet's collection are real and documented (Sotheby's 2021).

For US connections: there is no Angkor Wat in the US, but Khmer-American Buddhist communities have built Angkor-modeled temples. The most significant is Watt Munisotaram in Hampton, Dakota County, Minnesota (largest US Cambodian temple, 40-acre site, modeled on Angkor Wat with carved lions, a five-headed Naga, and a bridge crossing a pond to a Buddha). A hand-carved Angkor Wat replica with a goldfish moat sits at Wat Dhammagosnaram in Cranston, Rhode Island. US Khmer-art holdings include the Cleveland Museum of Art, the National Gallery of Art (1997 "Sculpture of Angkor"), the University of Michigan Museum of Art, and the Metropolitan Museum of Art.
