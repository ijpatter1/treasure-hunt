The Golden Chalice

Lesson: Confirmation Bias

Chapter 9 · book pages 86–94

Summary

Chapter 9 features a gold Chavín drinking vessel (a chalice) made by the Chavín culture of Peru between 400 and 1000 BC. A sidebar confirms it directly: “Here is a gold vessel from the Chavin culture made between 400 and 1000 BC. This chalice is in our treasure.” But the chapter is one of the most explicitly meta passages in the book — it is less a location clue than a lesson in how to think about the entire hunt.

The chapter opens with two epigraphs that frame its theme of confirmation bias: Paul Broca (“The least questioned assumptions are often the most questionable.”) and William of Ockham (“The explanation requiring the fewest assumptions is most likely to be correct.”). The author quotes the Oxford definition of confirmation bias — “the tendency to interpret new evidence as confirmation of one's existing beliefs or theories” — and warns that “like an alluring spider, confirmation bias has been entangling treasure seekers in its web since the dawn of time” and “should be avoided at any cost.” He urges hunters to remain “rooted onto the firm ground of reality.”

The chapter's extended metaphor is the temple of Chavín de Huántar, sitting at over 10,000 feet in the Andes of Peru and built around 900 BC. When 16th-century Spanish explorers first arrived, they could not find an entrance through its forty-five-foot walls (which feature massive carved heads protruding outward), and they made wild, wrong assumptions: one wrote home that it was built by “a race of giants,” another insisted it was “an ancient fortress.” The author uses this as his prime example of confirmation bias: “their ideas were all wrong.” The truth, he says, is more fascinating — Chavín de Huántar was the main temple of a religious cult whose spiritual dominion, not soldiers or weapons, kept the Chavín culture united for over a thousand years. The Chavín were the first highland civilization in Peru and excelled at monument design, metallurgy, sculpture, architecture, and hydraulics.

The temple is described as “a gigantic puzzle box [with] no discernible entrance.” Priests performed ceremonies on a roof reached by hidden stairs. Visitors bearing valuable offerings might be granted an entry time, led through a nondescript side entrance, given psychedelics to drink, then sent into a “sprawling labyrinth” of windowless narrow tunnels — some branching, some circling back, some dead ends — lit by torchlight, with nails embedded in the walls to create sparkling reflections and mirrors hung as speculums to heighten the hallucinogenic effect. Within the single-file passageways stand immense sculptures, the three most famous being the Lanzón, the Tello Obelisk, and the Raimondi Stele, inlaid with eagles, snakes, and cats and displaying fangs and human body parts; their densely packed inscriptions “forced our visitor to lie on the ground to read them.” A distinctive engineering feature: the temple “is positioned upon the meeting of two rivers,” and a sophisticated aqueduct system channeled water from both rivers into the converging tunnels so that a roaring sound grew louder toward the apex — audible even to people outside. The author suggests the gold chalice may have been used in ceremonies outside the temple, brought as an offering, or used to hold the psychedelics visitors drank.

The chapter then turns to a personal anecdote about confirmation bias affecting even the author's own mother. Every year their “small two-hundred-person congregation” held an evening Christmas Eve candlelight vigil — her favorite event. When the author “Jon” was four, his pastor father thought him too energetic to attend, but his mother insisted “Jon will be fine.” During the candlelit prayer about “the birth of Christ,” young Jon loudly began singing the Easter song “Here Comes Peter Cottontail” (“coming down the bunny trail… Hippity, hoppity Easter's on its way!”). Misreading the congregation's smiles as enjoyment — his own confirmation bias — he sang louder. Unable to cover his mouth for fear of the candle catching him on fire, his mother ran across the room and out the front church door carrying him.

The chapter closes with direct, meta-level treasure-hunting advice: “If you choose to look for our treasure, try to consciously avoid confirmation bias. There is no need to force the clues to fit a location you want them to lead you to. Instead, let them guide you in the direction they want you to take. Be willing to consider different options and explore new possibilities. Look both ways before crossing.” It outlines a defense against confirmation bias (stop justifying your current belief, look for reasons it could be wrong, spend time questioning your beliefs) and previews Chapter 10's Jackie Onassis treasure item.

Themes & the Lesson

The lesson is Confirmation Bias, and this chapter teaches HOW to approach the hunt rather than encoding a location. The author defines the bias, illustrates it twice — the Spanish explorers wrongly imagining Chavín de Huántar as a giants' construction or a fortress, and his mother wrongly assuming a four-year-old would behave at a candlelight vigil — then applies it directly to seekers: do not force the clues to fit a location you have already chosen; let the clues lead you where THEY point; stay willing to consider alternatives.

The capstone line “Look both ways before crossing” works on two levels: a metaphor for open-mindedness (consider both directions / both possibilities) and, given the author's literal style, possibly a real hint about crossing a road, bridge, railroad, or path. It pairs with the chapter's repeated “meeting of two rivers” confluence motif. This lesson is acutely relevant to our hunt: the prior FAILED Rocky Face Mountain search is exactly the kind of premature lock-in the chapter warns against.

Chavín de Huántar

A UNESCO World Heritage pre-Inca ceremonial center in the Ancash Region of northern Peru (Mosna Valley, eastern Cordillera Blanca, ~3,180 m, ~250 km north of Lima), flourishing c. 1200–500 BCE. Its defining features are exactly the ones the chapter borrows: it sits at the confluence of the Mosna and Wacheqsa rivers (a tinkuy); it has a flat-topped temple platform riddled with a multi-level underground labyrinth of narrow galleries, dead ends, hidden chambers, and a concealed entrance; at the labyrinth's heart stands the Lanzón; and an engineered water-channel system produced a roaring, jaguar-like acoustic effect (documented by Stanford's John Rick team). The most-quoted modern namesake is Peru's 1997 “Operation Chavín de Huántar” hostage rescue in Lima — a Peruvian event, not US. The only concrete US tie is the Cleveland Museum of Art's Chavín-attributed gold nose ornaments.

Chavín culture (Peru)

An early Andean civilization that flourished in the northern highlands of Peru roughly 900–250 BCE (the book dates the chalice 400–1000 BC). It was a religious/cultural network rather than a military empire, spread through a shared cult. Chavín artisans were among the earliest goldsmiths of the Americas, hammering and annealing sheet gold over wooden molds and pioneering soldering, alloying, and temperature control. The chapter uses the culture's temple mainly as a metaphor for confirmation bias; the Peru geography is descriptive history, not a US clue. US museums holding Chavín gold include the Met, Art Institute of Chicago, Cleveland, Dallas, Walters, and the Getty (which co-organized the 2017–2018 “Golden Kingdoms” exhibition). Honest read: chasing the museum list risks the very confirmation bias the chapter warns against — treat Chavín as provenance/flavor.

The Lanzón, Tello Obelisk, and Raimondi Stele

The three most famous carved-granite monuments of Chavín de Huántar (c. 900–200 BCE), all in Peru. The Lanzón (~4.35 m) remains in its original cruciform chamber deep in the temple's galleries, depicting a feline-fanged deity with snakes for hair. The Tello Obelisk bears one of the most complex carvings in the ancient Americas — a caiman whose body morphs into feline, fish, and bird. The Raimondi Stele shows the “Staff God” and is famous for “contour rivalry”: the image reads differently right-side-up vs. upside-down — the basis of the book's “lie on the ground to read them” detail. Shared iconography: feline fangs, raptorial birds, and snakes. The only concrete US institution genuinely linked is Dumbarton Oaks (Washington, DC), a private museum/garden that fails the public-land constraint. Honest read: the “read it both ways / view from a different angle” property is a perspective/open-mindedness hint, not a geographic pointer; the eagle/snake/feline triad is generic and should NOT be treated as a cipher.
