Tiffany's Furnace & Thoreau's Fire

Lesson: Fail Forward

Chapter 14 · book pages 123-132

Summary

Chapter 14 uses two historical stories — Louis Comfort Tiffany's decades of failed glass experiments and Henry David Thoreau's catastrophic forest fire — to argue that failure is a necessary, even welcome, part of achievement. It opens with two epigraphs (C.S. Lewis: "Failures, repeated failures, are finger posts on the road to achievement. One fails forward toward success." and Albert Einstein on being right the hundredth time) and a meditation on the United States, "the American Dream" as a "celebrated treasure hunt," capitalism, competition, and the American tendency to love winners and reject failure. The author reframes failure as a teacher — "what is meant by failing forward."

The first half profiles Louis Comfort Tiffany, son of Charles Tiffany (founder of Tiffany & Co.). Louis was a masterful landscape painter (27 exhibitions before age 21) who turned to interior/exterior design and then to glassmaking. In 1882 President Chester Arthur commissioned him to decorate the White House. Admiring medieval stained glass, he spent eight years with a team of chemists experimenting and failing until, in 1893, he unveiled "Favrile" glass — a technique for creating rich color within the glass itself. Tiffany's enameled pieces (like the treasure's iris vase) were first exhibited in 1900 in Paris; only 750 enamel pieces were ever made and Louis kept 90 in his private collection. He was the first to create iridescent enamel — translucent enamel layered directly onto metal (here, hot glass dripped over a copper base), an inexact technique that left the iris shapes "impressionistic." Production of Tiffany Glass ceased in 1933. Of the 90 enamel pieces Louis kept, 65 were from the experimental "EL series," and the author speculates the iris vase may have been "a happy accident, a treasure created through a process of failing forward."

Immediately after describing the vase, the author drops the chapter's most clue-relevant line: "As I look at the blue and pink irises on our vase, I can't help but think of the resting places of the boxes I've hidden and the colors of nature that surround each of them. Perhaps you'll pass by similar flowers along your way." He then shares childhood biography: at age three his parents were gifted twenty acres of woodland on which his father built a log home largely by himself over almost ten years, and he spent many afternoons in those woods. This grounded his high-school love of transcendentalists Ralph Waldo Emerson and Henry David Thoreau and the appeal of "a life of solitude in nature," embodied by Thoreau's Walden.

The second half tells Thoreau's story. He wrote Walden about two years in a cabin on Emerson's property next to Walden Pond in Concord, Massachusetts. On April 30, 1844, fishing with a friend at Fairhaven Bay during an unseasonably dry spring, Thoreau built a campfire in a meadow of dry grass; wind carried a spark and ignited the largest forest fire ever recorded in Concord — over 300 acres of woodland burned, requiring much of Concord's citizenry to extinguish it, and Thoreau became "an accidental villain and local public enemy number one." His journal showed difficulty taking accountability, yet he later advocated for a volunteer fire department in Concord, and scholars speculate the fire was a major reason he began his Walden retreat a year later. His journal — kept for 24 years, several thousand pages, over two million words — became the source of all his public writings.

The treasure contains two original pages of Thoreau's journal, written in 1851, marking the first time he penned the phrase "the art of life" ("...How to extract its honey from the flower of the world... I am like the bee searching the livelong day for the sweets of nature."); a second passage on discipline became part of his posthumous essay "Life Without Principle." The chapter sketches Thoreau's character (quirky, taciturn, knew six languages, played flute) and his progressive activism (children's rights, Native American rights, women's equality, anti-slavery and the underground railroad), culminating in the October 16, 1859 John Brown raid on the Harper's Ferry armory in Virginia (aiming to establish a safe place in Virginia and Maryland for freed slaves). Thoreau delivered "A Plea for Captain John Brown," the lone nationally-recognized voice defending Brown, who was hanged the next day — an event the author says "ushered in the American Civil War."

The chapter closes with the author's confessional reflection on his own fear of failure: conflating school grades with parental love, equating romantic rejection with failure, and anxiety about money in a "family of five" with a father who "earned a small salary" and only "bare essentials." Reading "Failing Forward" by John Maxwell changed his perspective. He notes "Kimberly can confirm" (his wife) that fatherhood and marriage give him plenty of failures. He admits worries about the treasure hunt itself — hurting his family's finances, his wife or children being harassed, the boxes being found immediately, or the book being no good — and resolves to embrace failure. He gives direct hunt advice ("don't demand too much of yourself," "You don't have to find them quickly or on your first try," "I would not be surprised if failure winds up being part of the process that eventually leads you to our treasure. Welcome it as much as you are capable.") and previews Chapter 15 on Wilma Rudolph, his favorite story, "a woman who absolutely and resolutely never, ever gave up."

The Treasure(s)

Two artifacts are featured in this chapter:

  • Tiffany Glass "Iris" vase — made by Louis Comfort Tiffany around 1900, iridescent enamel created by hot glass dripped over a copper base, decorated with blue and pink irises. The "LC/T" initials are carved into the base. Exceedingly rare in its size, with only one other known (owned by the Metropolitan Museum of Art). Likely from Tiffany's experimental "EL series" — possibly, the author muses, "a happy accident" produced by failing forward.
  • Two original pages of Henry David Thoreau's journal — written in 1851, containing the first recorded use of the phrase "the art of life"; a second passage was later folded into Thoreau's posthumous essay "Life Without Principle."

People

Louis Comfort Tiffany (1848–1933) — American decorative artist, son of Tiffany & Co. founder Charles Lewis Tiffany, and maker of the treasure's iris vase. Born and died in New York City. He built his glasshouse/furnaces in Corona, Queens, NY (c. 1892–93), where the iridescent Favrile and enamel work was produced. Themes & the Lesson

The subtitle "Fail Forward" frames failure not as the opposite of success but as a step toward it — mistakes should be embraced, owned, and used as instruction rather than feared. The author illustrates it three ways: Tiffany's eight years of failed glass experiments yielding the Favrile breakthrough (and the iris vase possibly being a "happy accident" from the experimental EL series); Thoreau's catastrophic 300-acre fire eventually channeling into his Walden retreat and lifelong nature philosophy; and the author's own life (grades-as-love, fear of rejection, money anxiety) reformed by John Maxwell's "Failing Forward."

Crucially, the lesson is converted into explicit treasure-hunt meta-advice: searchers should not expect to succeed quickly or on the first try, should treat a failed search as a chance to "evaluate, understand more, and try again," and should "Welcome [failure] as much as you are capable" because failure "winds up being part of the process that eventually leads you to our treasure." This is notable given a prior boots-on-the-ground search failed — the author effectively frames repeated attempts as expected and normal. The recurring "colors of nature" / flower imagery also extends the book's nature-solitude motif.

Research Findings

The blue and pink irises / "colors of nature" flower clue

The clue is real and verified on page 127: "As I look at the blue and pink irises on our vase, I can't help but think of the resting places of the boxes I've hidden and the colors of nature that surround each of them." A literal reading matters: the irises are described as being on the vase (cut flowers indoors), and the statement is only that hide sites are surrounded by "the colors of nature" generally — not that blue/pink irises grow at a hide. Treating it as "look for wild irises at the spot" is an inference, not a stated fact.

Native iris botany (US Forest Service; Lady Bird Johnson Wildflower Center): the US has ~28 native iris species, found in "every state in the lower 48 and Alaska," concentrated on the Pacific Coast (e.g., Iris douglasiana) and the Southeast (the Louisiana iris group). Blue natives are widespread — Iris versicolor (Northern Blue Flag, Northeast), Iris virginica (Southern Blue Flag), Iris missouriensis (Rocky Mountain Iris), Iris lacustris (Dwarf Lake Iris, a federally threatened upper-Great-Lakes endemic), Iris setosa (Alaska) — so "blue iris" alone does not narrow geography. Truly PINK wild irises barely exist; the closest warm-toned native is Iris fulva (Copper Iris, copper/brick-red), essentially Lower-Mississippi / Gulf South. Mysterious Writings notes the blue+pink pairing could also evoke road-map color conventions (interstates blue, highways red) rather than botany. Bottom line: a useful ambiance/confirmation detail, not a primary location pointer; iris-heavy wetlands also conflict with the no-water-crossing constraint.

Henry David Thoreau

Thoreau (1817–1862) was the transcendentalist writer/naturalist born in Concord, MA, who lived in a cabin on Emerson's Walden land 1845–1847. His 1851 journal entries (the source of the treasure's two pages) cover "Purity," "Experiments in Living," "The Study of Nature," and "The Development of the Mind"; the "art of life" theme maps to his line "To affect the quality of the day, that is the highest of arts." The chapter title's "Thoreau's Fire" is the documented April 30, 1844 accident: Thoreau and Edward Sherman Hoar cooked fish at Fair Haven Bay and accidentally burned ~300 acres, earning him the "woods burner" epithet. Journal provenance ran from Thoreau to his sister Sophia, to H.G.O. Blake, to E.H. Russell, to collector Stephen Wakeman, then to Pierpont Morgan (1909); the Morgan Library & Museum (NYC) now holds 39 of his 47 journals, with other manuscripts at the Concord Museum, Houghton Library (Harvard), Middlebury College, the NYPL Berg Collection, and the Concord Free Public Library.

Walden Pond, Concord, Massachusetts

A glacial kettle pond in Concord (extending into Lincoln), MA, now Walden Pond State Reservation (~335 acres, MA DCR); main parking at 915 Walden Street (Route 126). The Emerson family deeded the core land to the Commonwealth in 1922 to preserve "the Walden of Emerson and Thoreau"; it became a National Historic Landmark in 1962. The original cabin was dismantled in 1868; the site (with original hearth stone) was rediscovered by Roland Wells Robbins in 1945 and is reached via the ~1.8-mile Pond Path, marked by a visitor stone cairn; a full-size replica stands by the main lot. The 1844 Fair Haven Bay fire (a year before Thoreau moved to Walden) is the literal "Thoreau's Fire," and some accounts argue his guilt over it helped push him toward the retreat. Caution: the cabin site is a protected, monitored NHL archaeological zone and the pond is one of New England's most crowded, ranger-patrolled spots — a poor concealment site.

Fairhaven Bay, Concord, Massachusetts

A wide, lake-like section of the Sudbury River in Concord (~42.4255 N, 71.3535 W), a glacial kettle in the river channel; the Sudbury flows northeast through it to meet the Assabet at Egg Rock, forming the Concord River. It is the named site of Thoreau's April 30, 1844 fire (~300 acres burned). The bay is surrounded almost entirely by public/conservation land with no access fee; its prominent feature is Fairhaven Cliffs (Fairhaven Hill), a wooded overlook noted as a "favorite resort for the nature lover" since the 1700s–1800s. Wright Woods (Concord Land Conservation Trust) links the trails to Lincoln conservation land and Walden Pond; Scout Island is a conserved island in the bay. Foot access: Fairhaven Road (Concord), Mount Misery on Route 117 (Lincoln), Old Concord Road, and Canoe Landing. Non-dangerous, multiple roadside trailheads well within 3 miles of a road — a clean fit on the hard constraints, but one of several Concord-area candidates under the one-box-per-state rule. No source ties the treasure here.

Louis Comfort Tiffany

Tiffany (1848–1933), son of Tiffany & Co. founder Charles Lewis Tiffany, made Favrile glass (name coined from Old English "fabrile" = hand-wrought). Born and died in NYC. He built his glasshouse/furnaces in Corona, Queens (c. 1892–93) with English glassworker Arthur Nash; his enamel department ran roughly 1898–1907 (public debut London 1899), making a small number of unique copper-based pieces, each different because of unpredictable firing chemistry. His 84-room estate, Laurelton Hall (Laurel Hollow/Oyster Bay, Long Island, completed 1905), burned in 1957; salvaged elements were rescued by Hugh and Jeannette McKean and form the core of the Morse Museum in Winter Park, FL. He is buried at Green-Wood Cemetery, Brooklyn. His geography is almost entirely the NY metro area — not typical wilderness hide environments — with the Morse Museum (FL) as the surviving legacy site.

Tiffany Glass "Iris" enamel vase / EL series

Tiffany began enameling in 1898, first in a small lab in his NYC mansion (72nd & Madison), then relocating the enamel department to the Corona, Queens glass works. The work was iridescent translucent enamel over a copper (often gilt) base, fired so the copper reacted unpredictably — closely linked to Favrile chemistry. Pieces were marked with "SG" or "EL" plus a sequential number ("SG" likely = Stourbridge Glass; a known EL-series poppy vase is dated May 11, 1900). The enamels were developed by the "Tiffany Girls" (Julia Munson, Alice Carmen Gouvy, Patricia Gay, Lillian Palmie) with chemist Dr. Parker McIlhenny. Tiffany kept a private "A-Coll." collection of his best work, displayed at Laurelton Hall; much survives at the Morse Museum (Winter Park, FL). The Met received Tiffany enamels via a 1925 loan that became a 1951 gift from the LCT Foundation (accession group 51.121); the Corning Museum of Glass holds related enamel design watercolors. The "EL"/"SG" marks are genuine catalog/provenance marks, NOT a cipher. The specific iris EL vase's current home could not be independently verified.

Metropolitan Museum of Art

The Met (1000 Fifth Avenue at 82nd St, Upper East Side, Manhattan) holds one of the world's most comprehensive Tiffany collections. Tiffany work first entered in 1896 via the Havemeyers (56 Favrile vases — the first American art glass in the collection); Tiffany loaned more in 1925, gifted by the LCT Foundation in 1951. Roughly 80 objects are on view in a dedicated American Wing gallery (opened 2002), including windows like "Magnolias and Irises" (ca. 1908). The enamel-on-copper department's small output (c. 1898–1907) is why a comparable enamel iris vase is genuinely rare — consistent with the book naming the Met as owner of the "only other comparable" example. The salvaged Laurelton Hall floral loggia has stood in the Met's American Wing since 1980. LOW direct hide relevance: the Met, the Morse Museum, and the loggia are all inside museums or developed land, not accessible public wildland; the institution is best read as a provenance/comparison signpost confirming the vase's rarity.

Harper's Ferry, (West) Virginia

Harpers Ferry sits at the confluence of the Potomac and Shenandoah Rivers, where WV, VA, and MD meet, in a water gap through the Blue Ridge. George Washington selected it for a federal armory (established 1796) because of its waterpower. It is most famous for John Brown's Oct 16–18, 1859 raid; Brown was captured in the armory's fire-engine house ("John Brown's Fort") by U.S. Marines under Robert E. Lee, then tried and hanged, an event widely cited as precipitating the Civil War. Now Harpers Ferry National Historical Park (NPS, designated 1944, expanded 1963). It hosts the Appalachian Trail Conservancy HQ (799 Washington St) and is the AT's "spiritual midpoint" (the true numerical midpoint is north in PA's Michaux State Forest near Pine Grove Furnace State Park). Strong on the constraints (accessible NPS public land near roads), but NPS rules generally prohibit placing/removing objects, and the high-traffic historic core is an awkward fit.

John Brown (raid & Thoreau's "A Plea for Captain John Brown")

John Brown (1800–1859) seized the Harpers Ferry armory on Oct 16, 1859; federal forces under Robert E. Lee suppressed the raid within 36 hours. He was tried at Charles Town, VA (now WV) and hanged Dec 2, 1859 — the first person executed for treason in US history. Thoreau's "A Plea for Captain John Brown" began as a speech delivered Oct 30, 1859 in the First Parish meetinghouse vestry in Concord, MA (repeated at Boston's Tremont Temple and Worcester's Washburn Hall), casting Brown as a martyr — tying the chapter theme back to Concord. Brown was buried Dec 8, 1859 at his farm in North Elba, NY, beneath his grandfather's headstone, near a large rock; followers were reinterred beside him in 1899. His 1856 Pottawatomie killings and "Bleeding Kansas" struggle occurred in Kansas Territory (Adair Cabin near Osawatomie). The strongest public-land association is the John Brown Farm State Historic Site (North Elba/Lake Placid, NY) — NY State Parks land with free year-round trails, about 3 miles SE of Lake Placid.
