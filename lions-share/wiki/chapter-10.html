Jackie Onassis' Diamond Sapphire Brooch

Lesson: Welcome the Good and the Bad

Chapter 10 · book pages 95-100

Summary

Chapter 10 features a bow-shaped brooch that belonged to Jacqueline "Jackie" Kennedy Onassis, made with sapphires and diamonds inlaid around a carved rock crystal center, offered to the author for $150,000. A full-page photo of the brooch opens the chapter (page 95). The chapter is framed around the lesson of resilience and accepting both fortune and hardship.

The author opens with two epigraphs on resilience — Bernard Williams ("Man never made any material as resilient as the human spirit.") and Sheri L. Dew (on life being "a test"). He recounts a deeply personal memory: as a competitive, hard-on-himself 11-year-old, he stood crying in a parking lot after losing a football playoff game, convinced he had cost his team the win. His father knelt, put a hand on his shoulder, and counseled him that the pain was temporary and that "it's not about when bad things happen... It's about how you respond." The author says this moment about resilience remains vivid and inspired his only tattoo: a Sanskrit phrase down his left forearm that "roughly translate[s] as, 'I welcome the good with the bad, as they both come from the one true source.'"

The author then reveals this brooch is his father's FAVORITE item in the entire treasure — the only object that, upon seeing it, prompted his dad to ask whether the author might rather keep it for himself. He uses Jackie Onassis as the embodiment of his father's lesson: a woman who "knew more about resilience than I hope I ever need to learn," who stood unwavering and with class through immense tragedy and "did not stray the course away from the things that brought her joy."

A biographical arc follows. Jackie relentlessly studied culture, French, and art history; at age 21, from a contest essay, she was selected from 1,279 candidates for a Vogue internship — then quit on her first day because the office lacked enough prominent men and didn't fit her vision. She took a job at The Washington-Times writing the "Inquiring Camera Girl" column, walking the streets photographing and interviewing people, which gave her access to media and political influencers. Less than a year and a half later she met up-and-coming congressman Jack Kennedy; though already engaged, "She returned the first man's ring and accepted Mr. Kennedy." The chapter recounts her tragedies: she rode beside JFK when he was assassinated on November 22, 1963, in Dallas; her friend and brother-in-law Robert Kennedy was also assassinated; and the couple suffered a miscarriage, a stillbirth, and the death of infant Patrick, who died only three months before JFK. A black-and-white photo shows Jackie with daughter Caroline (page 98).

The chapter follows her later life: seeking privacy, she married the older Aristotle Onassis (who later died of cancer); then she "pivoted back to New York," becoming the first former first lady to re-enter a traditional workplace as an editor at Viking Press, then at Doubleday for 16 years until her death, editing best-sellers. "Jackie began her career as a writer and ended it as an editor. She had come full circle." Her history-preservation work is highlighted: she created the White House Historical Association (still a free public resource) and helped New York City win the legal fight to save Grand Central Station (called "Grand Central Terminal" historically) from demolition. The author calls Jackie "a treasure hunter any of us would be fortunate to have standing by our side."

The chapter closes with explicit treasure-hunt reassurance: the hunt "is not designed to require any great hardship," "the stakes are not particularly high," and searching without finding will leave "happy memories of spending time outdoors with a loved one or with the delightful sights, smells, and sounds of nature around you." "This hunt is designed to bring you joy, not suffering." Page 100 transitions to Chapter 11, previewing the tale of a ship's captain named Fonseca whose greed cost him his treasure and ship — "Greed is a siren. It's a trickster... Know thy foe, so that you may defeat him."

The Treasure(s)

The featured item is Jackie Onassis' diamond and sapphire brooch — a bow/ribbon-shaped brooch with sapphires and diamonds inlaid around a carved rock crystal center. It was offered to the author for $150,000 and is described as the author's father's favorite item in the entire treasure: "It's the only item in our treasure that when my dad saw it, he turned to me and asked if I might not rather keep it for himself."

Research identifies it as an Art Deco rock-crystal, sapphire, and diamond bow brooch dating to circa 1925, set in platinum with a pair of carved/frosted rock-crystal panels framed by calibre-cut sapphires and old European-cut diamonds (~2.00 carats total), measuring about 2.25 in long. The original maker is not identified in available listings — it is anonymous Art Deco work — but its strongest provenance hook is its original fitted red leather presentation box from A la Vieille Russie, a celebrated New York dealer/jeweler (the box maker/retailer, not necessarily the brooch's maker). The brooch descended through the Kennedy family and was sold via Sotheby's in 2005 in the "Property From The Kennedy Homes" sale (lot 662); the broader Estate of Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis auction ran at Sotheby's New York April 23-26, 1996.

Places & Geographies

The chapter explicitly names Dallas, Texas (JFK's 1963 assassination), New York (where Jackie "pivoted back" to and worked), Washington, DC (the White House Historical Association), and Grand Central Station / Grand Central Terminal (the demolition fight she helped win). Research expands these into a wide set of concrete US locations through clues-by-association:

  • Squibnocket Pond Reservation (former Red Gate Farm), Aquinnah, Martha's Vineyard, Massachusetts — Jackie owned ~340 acres here for 16 years. After her death the Martha's Vineyard Land Bank and Sheriff's Meadow Foundation acquired it; it is now ~323 acres of public conservation land with 5+ miles of trails, ~2,500 ft of boardwalk, viewing platforms, beach access, dunes, Squibnocket Pond, Black Brook and Lily Pond, opening to the public in phases beginning late May 2025 with two trailheads (~12 parking spaces each). The single strongest constraint-compatible lead in this chapter.
  • Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis Reservoir, Central Park, Manhattan, NY — a public 106-acre water body (86th-96th St) with a 1.58-mile running track, renamed for her in 1994 and managed by NYC Parks.
  • Grand Central Terminal, 42nd St & Park/Vanderbilt Ave, Manhattan, NY — the landmark she fought to save; her dedicated entry vestibule is at 89 East 42nd St / Pershing Square.
  • Dispersed Grand Central eagle sculptures — twelve ~4,000-lb cast-iron eagles from the 1898 station scattered across New York and beyond when it was demolished. Notable landings: the Shandaken Eagle, Phoenicia / Town of Shandaken, Ulster County, NY (roadside on Route 28, surrounded by Catskills public land — Phoenicia-Mt. Tobias Wild Forest and Shandaken Wild Forest); the Suffolk County Vanderbilt Museum, Centerport, Long Island, NY (two eagles); Philipse Manor / Sleepy Hollow and Bronxville and Mount Vernon (Westchester Co., NY); Garrison, Putnam County, NY (Hudson Highlands); and Space Farms Zoo and Museum, Sussex, New Jersey.
  • Washington, DC — the White House / President's Park, Lafayette Square (her preservation work), and the White House Historical Association office at 740 Jackson Place NW with the Decatur House.
  • Whiteface Mountain & Wilmington Wild Forest, Essex County, NY (Adirondacks) — reached via clue-by-association: Jackie edited at Viking Press, whose Viking-ship logo was drawn by artist Rockwell Kent, whose home "Asgaard Farm" near Ausable Forks sat beneath Whiteface (a peak he painted repeatedly). Whiteface and the surrounding Forest Preserve are public land with road access (NY-431) and marked trails; Asgaard Farm itself is private.
  • Flat Creek Ranch, Jackson Hole, Wyoming — another association chain: Jackie's early employer was the Washington Times-Herald, owned by Cissy Patterson, whose ranch sits inside Bridger-Teton National Forest near Grand Teton National Park (genuine accessible public land; two steps removed).
  • Newport, RI (Hammersmith Farm), Southampton and East Hampton, NY (childhood/birthplace); Wexford estate near Marshall/Middleburg, VA (private), and a horse farm at Peapack-Gladstone, NJ (private).
  • NYC provenance / institutions: A la Vieille Russie (781 Fifth Ave 1961-2017; now 745 Fifth Ave; Faberge dealer), Sotheby's New York; museums holding related collections — Virginia Museum of Fine Arts, Richmond, VA; Cleveland Museum of Art, OH; John F. Kennedy Presidential Library, Boston, MA.
  • Garden City, NY (Long Island) — Doubleday's "Country Life Press" building and still-named LIRR station; a dense suburb, not a hide site.

Themes & the Lesson

The subtitle "Welcome the Good and the Bad" is drawn directly from the author's Sanskrit forearm tattoo ("I welcome the good with the bad, as they both come from the one true source") and from his father's parking-lot lesson that hardship is temporary and "it's about how you respond." The author uses resilience and pliability as the moral throughline, with Jackie Onassis as the exemplar who endured staggering loss yet repeatedly reinvented herself and kept pursuing joy.

He then explicitly applies the lesson to the hunt itself: it is low-stakes, not dangerous, requires no great hardship, and should bring joy — even an unsuccessful search yields happy memories outdoors. Read against the author's confirmed rules, this is best understood as a meta-clue reinforcing the constraints (accessible, safe, outdoor public-land location), not a geographic pointer. A secondary motif worth noting is preservation and free public access (the White House Historical Association as a "free public resource," the public fight to save Grand Central), which thematically echoes the public-land nature of the hunt.

Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis

Jacqueline Lee Bouvier (1929-1994) was born in Southampton, NY, raised partly in Newport, RI and East Hampton, served as First Lady (1961-63), then was a book editor at Viking Press and Doubleday in NYC after Aristotle Onassis's death. Three threads tie her to specific, named, accessible public places: (1) Historic preservation — the "First Lady of Historic Preservation," she restored the White House, helped preserve Lafayette Square (DC) and parts of Newport, RI, and in 1975-1978 led the public campaign to save Grand Central Terminal, riding the "Landmark Express" to DC for the Supreme Court case (Penn Central v. NYC, 1978). (2) A named-after-her public landmark — the Central Park Reservoir was renamed the Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis Reservoir in 1994. (3) Her own estate became public conservation land — ~340 acres at Red Gate Farm, Aquinnah, Martha's Vineyard, now the publicly managed Squibnocket Pond Reservation (~323 acres) with trails and boardwalks, opening to the public from late May 2025. Other residences (Wexford, VA; 1040 Fifth Ave; Peapack-Gladstone, NJ; Skorpios) are private or overseas.

Grand Central Terminal / Grand Central Station

Grand Central Terminal (Manhattan; opened 1913, on the site of the 1871 Depot and 1898 Station) is the central real-world landmark of Chapter 10. Jackie Onassis was a leading public face of the campaign to save it after Penn Central proposed a Marcel Breuer tower atop the landmark in 1968. The Municipal Art Society's "Committee to Save Grand Central Terminal" was announced at the Oyster Bar on Jan 30, 1975; Onassis ran a "Landmark Express" Amtrak trip to DC for oral arguments. On June 26, 1978, the Supreme Court (opinion by Justice Brennan) upheld NYC's Landmarks Law in Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City — the foundational US historic-preservation precedent. The most treasure-hunt-relevant feature is the dispersal of the building's twelve cast-iron eagles across New York State and beyond when the 1898 station was demolished — notably the Shandaken Eagle on Route 28 in the Catskills, the Suffolk County Vanderbilt Museum (Centerport), Sleepy Hollow, Bronxville, Garrison, Mount Vernon, and Space Farms Zoo in Sussex, NJ. The Main Concourse celestial ceiling is famously painted backwards (east-west reversed).

White House Historical Association

The WHHA is a private non-profit founded by First Lady Jacqueline Kennedy on November 3, 1961, as a workaround to fund White House restoration with proceeds from the first official guidebook (1962, 500,000 copies in ten months) rather than public money. It remains an active, free public educational resource. It is centered entirely in Washington, DC: its office is at 740 Jackson Place NW (Lafayette Square / President's Park), and it operates the David M. Rubenstein National Center for White House History at the historic Decatur House (built 1818-1819 by Benjamin Henry Latrobe for Commodore Stephen Decatur). Its annual Christmas ornaments are made by ChemArt in Lincoln, Rhode Island. As an organization in high-security urban DC, it is thematic reinforcement (preservation / public access), not a hide location.

Doubleday (publisher)

Doubleday (founded 1897) consolidated on Long Island in Garden City, NY, in 1910, building the famed "Country Life Press" plant (cornerstone laid by Theodore Roosevelt). Jackie Onassis edited there from 1978 until her death in 1994 (~16 years, ~100 books, never crediting herself). Her edited titles span clue-rich topics: "Remember the Ladies" (Revolutionary-era American history), Joseph Campbell & Bill Moyers' "The Power of Myth" (1988), Michael Jackson's "Moonwalk" (1988, foreword), Martha Graham's "Blood Memory," and the English Naguib Mahfouz "Cairo Trilogy." The single concrete US anchor is Garden City, NY (the Country Life Press building and still-named LIRR station) — a dense suburb, not a hide site; the richer angle is associative through edited titles (Campbell taught at Sarah Lawrence in Bronxville, NY).

Viking Press

Viking Press (founded NYC, 1925) was acquired by Penguin in 1975; Jackie Onassis joined in September 1975 as a consulting editor — her first traditional job after returning to private life — resigning in 1977 over Jeffrey Archer's "Shall We Tell the President?" before moving to Doubleday. The most clue-relevant detail is the publisher's Viking-longship colophon, drawn by artist Rockwell Kent in 1925. Kent (1882-1971) was born in Tarrytown, NY, made his name on Monhegan Island, Maine, and in 1927 bought a ~200-acre Adirondack farm near Ausable Forks, NY, that he named "Asgaard," living there until his death; he is buried in view of Whiteface Mountain ~10 miles away. Asgaard Farm (NRHP 2020) is private, but Whiteface and the surrounding Wilmington Wild Forest are New York State Forest Preserve public land with road access (NY-431) and marked trails — a constraint-compatible region, though reached two hops removed from the chapter text.

Aristotle Onassis

Aristotle Socrates Onassis (1906-1975) built the world's largest privately owned merchant fleet; he married Jackie in 1968 on Skorpios, Greece. His concrete US footprint is private/commercial: in NYC he developed the Olympic Tower at 641 Fifth Avenue (built 1971-1976, glass facade reflecting St. Patrick's Cathedral); after WWII he bought ~23 surplus US ships and in 1947 placed the first postwar US tanker order at Bethlehem Steel's Sparrows Point shipyard, Maryland. No US public-land sites are associated with him; treat as flavor/association.

The Washington Times-Herald ("Inquiring Camera Girl")

The Washington Times-Herald (1939-1954) was a DC daily created when heiress-publisher Eleanor "Cissy" Patterson merged two papers bought from Hearst; its building stood at 1307 H Street NW. Jacqueline Bouvier worked there from fall 1951 as the "Inquiring Camera Girl" ($25/week), roaming DC streets photographing people and posing witty questions; she resigned after accepting JFK's proposal. The owning Medill-McCormick-Patterson family ties the paper to Chicago, Long Island, and especially Cissy Patterson's estates: the Dower House / Mount Airy mansion near Rosaryville, Maryland (now part of Rosaryville State Park) and her Flat Creek Ranch in Jackson Hole, Wyoming — inside Bridger-Teton National Forest near Grand Teton National Park, ~14 miles from Jackson. The Wyoming link is a constraint-compatible but two-steps-removed association.

Vogue Prix de Paris contest (Jacqueline Bouvier's 1951 win)

The Prix de Paris was Vogue's annual writing/talent competition for senior college women; the prize was a one-year junior-editor apprenticeship split between Paris and New York. Jacqueline Bouvier entered as a senior at George Washington University (DC) and won in 1951 from ~1,280 applicants (the book's "1,279 candidates / age 21" matches within one). Her essay "People I Wish I Had Known" named Baudelaire, Wilde, and Diaghilev; she declined the prize. Its only fixed anchors are the Vogue / Conde Nast offices (NYC), GW University (DC), the Paris office, and the JFK Library, Boston (which holds her entry papers) — none are public-land sites. Family ties commonly associated with this period (East Hampton, NY; Newport, RI; McLean/Merrywood, VA, near Great Falls Park) are speculative associations, not contest facts.
