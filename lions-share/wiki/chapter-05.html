Masterworks by Art Smith

Lesson: An Exercise in Faith

Chapter 5 · book pages 56-63

Summary

Chapter 5 centers on four treasure pieces by Art Smith (Arthur George Smith), described in the book as America's first prominent African-American jewelry designer. The chapter opens with three epigraphs on faith — from Martin Luther King, Jr., Henri Bergson, and Thomas Aquinas — framing its theme. The author, Jon Collins-Black, recounts a conversation with a friend about the meaning of "faith," distinguishing his own sense of faith as an "undeniable knowing" rather than mere hope.

The biographical narrative traces Art Smith's family origins. His mother, Mary Elizabeth Williams, was born April 19, 1879, in Morgan's Pass, Jamaica, into harsh poverty; she learned to read from a local priest and read Bible scriptures to her parents. She took a job as a hotel maid in Kingston, Jamaica, where she met James Smith; the two bonded over a love of literature, married, and moved to Cuba for business opportunities. There Mary gave birth to her son Arthur George Smith. On July 21, 1920, the family boarded a ship from Cuba to New York City.

In New York, Art was bullied for his Jamaican accent but stayed committed to his vision. After an honorable mention in a local ASPCA poster contest, the eighth-grader declared he would become a professional artist — drawing skepticism, since (as the book frames it) "no commercially successful black artists existed in the US in 1934." The Great Depression paradoxically created his opening: with no jobs available, he faced no resistance applying to art school and won a full scholarship to Cooper Union, becoming one of only six black students in its art program. The book says he won first prize in a New York World's Fair Corporation contest to design a multi-purpose building (a center for police, firefighters, and medical responders), but politely declined his advisors' push toward civil engineering. He left painting for sculpture and three-dimensional design — the foundation for his jewelry career.

After graduating, Art took a part-time job teaching children at an art center in Harlem, where he met mentor Winifred Mason, an African-American jewelry artist who had just opened a store in Greenwich Village. Inspired, Art opened his own shop — overcoming racist landlords (with a white business partner's help to secure a lease), a brick thrown through his window, and shouted insults. His breakthrough came through friendship with Talley Beatty, a dance choreographer who worked on shows with Duke Ellington; Talley commissioned bold jewelry for his dancers that was "striking, from both close and far away" and contoured the body. Many of Art Smith's most iconic pieces were originally worn in these performances. His signature style used asymmetrical forms of copper, silver, and brass, drawing on ancient Egypt, tribal Africa, and surrealism. His work has appeared in over a dozen museums; his pieces are now very rare and "do not often change hands."

The chapter closes with the author's most pointed meta-advice to hunters: he predicts that online forums, chat rooms, and YouTube videos full of speculation about the treasure's location will emerge, and counsels readers to "value your own [opinion]. Trust in what you know. Have faith." He calls faith "an ethereal treasure map guiding each step of our way" and names inspiration as faith's "first cousin," saying "sometimes a dash of inspiration is all a treasure hunter really needs." He transitions to Chapter 6 (Rubies to Wear) by introducing two modern-day jewelry artists he discovered while researching Art Smith, whose bespoke pieces center on rubies.

Themes & the Lesson

The subtitle "An Exercise in Faith" reframes faith as an "undeniable knowing" rather than hope. The author uses Art Smith's life — persisting against poverty, racism, and skeptics to become America's first prominent African-American jewelry designer — as a parable for the treasure hunter.

The explicit application is directive and self-referential: the author warns that "online forums, chat rooms, or YouTube videos" of speculation will arise about where the treasure is hidden, and advises hunters to ignore the crowd and trust their own instincts — "value your own. Trust in what you know. Have faith. This will make a difference." Faith is described as "an ethereal treasure map," and its "first cousin" inspiration as sometimes "all a treasure hunter really needs." The takeaway: solve it yourself, follow your own reading of the clues, and don't be swayed by others' theories.

Research Findings

Art Smith (Arthur George Smith), modernist jewelry designer

Arthur George "Art" Smith (1917–1982) was a leading mid-century American modernist jeweler and one of the first prominent African-American jewelry designers. Born in Cuba to Jamaican immigrant parents, he moved to Brooklyn at age three. He studied at Cooper Union (architecture, then sculpture) and took night jewelry courses at NYU, where he was mentored by Winifred Mason at her Greenwich Village studio. Smith opened his own first studio on Cornelia Street around 1946–1949 but faced racist and homophobic harassment that briefly required police surveillance, prompting a move to 140 West 4th Street, half a block from Washington Square Park, which remained his shop and home base until 1979. He died in 1982. His style drew on surrealism, biomorphism, African forms, and the mobiles of Alexander Calder; pieces were designed to be large but light, partly for dancers.

Smith was deeply embedded in the Greenwich Village jazz, modern-dance, and Black-arts scene. He designed jewelry for the dance companies of Talley Beatty, Pearl Primus, and Claude Marchant, and was connected to figures including James Baldwin, Lena Horne, Harry Belafonte, and Duke Ellington (for whom he made cuff links). A notable documented commission: the Peekskill, New York chapter of the NAACP commissioned him to design a brooch for Eleanor Roosevelt. He had a one-man show at New York's Museum of Contemporary Crafts (now the Museum of Arts and Design) in the 1960s.

His work is concentrated in museum collections. The Brooklyn Museum holds roughly 21 of his pieces and mounted the major touring retrospective "From the Village to Vogue: The Modernist Jewelry of Art Smith" (2008–2011), which later traveled to the High Museum of Art in Atlanta. His work is also in the Metropolitan Museum of Art, Cooper Hewitt, the Museum of Arts and Design (all NYC), the Museum of Fine Arts Boston, and the National Museum of African American History and Culture in Washington, D.C.

Winifred Mason (Winifred Mason Chenet)

Winifred Mason (1912–1993) was a Caribbean-American jeweler, widely cited as the first commercial Black jeweler in the United States. Born in Brooklyn, she earned a BS in English Literature (1934) and an MA in Education (1936) from NYU. In the early 1940s she taught metalworking and crafts (Junior Achievement, the WPA, and the Harlem Boys Club) and ran a jewelry studio in Greenwich Village, expanding into a shop at 133 West 3rd Street. Correction to the chapter framing: Mason was the MENTOR — Art Smith became her full-time assistant before founding his own shop. Her work sold at Lord & Taylor and to a custom clientele including Billie Holiday. She married Haitian-born Jean E. Chenet in 1948 and, as Winifred Chenet, produced Vodou/veve-inspired jewelry in Port-au-Prince, Haiti, fleeing back to the US after her husband's murder in 1963. Five of her 1940s pieces are held in the Smithsonian's NMAAHC.

Talley Beatty (choreographer/dancer)

Talley Beatty (1918–1995) was one of the first prominent Black American modern-dance choreographers. Born in Cedar Grove, Louisiana (a section of Shreveport), he grew up in Chicago, trained under Katherine Dunham and later Martha Graham, and moved to New York City in 1946. He had a strong creative affinity for the music of Duke Ellington, choreographing Ellington works including "A Drum Is a Woman" (1957) and "My People" (1963); his signature work "The Road of the Phoebe Snow" (1959) used Ellington/Billy Strayhorn music. Beatty was a close friend and client of Art Smith, wearing his jewelry and introducing him to other dancers — commissions credited with pushing Smith toward his famous bold, oversized scale. He premiered "Southern Landscape" at Jacob's Pillow (Becket, MA) in 1948 and returned there in 1952, 1960, and 1992.

Cooper Union

Cooper Union (The Cooper Union for the Advancement of Science and Art) is a private college founded in 1859 by industrialist/inventor Peter Cooper. Its landmark Foundation Building and Great Hall sit at 7 East 7th Street in Manhattan's East Village. Art Smith won a scholarship there (one of only about six Black students at the time) and graduated in 1945, where exposure to three-dimensional sculpture shaped his jewelry. Note: the "World's Fair design prize" claim could not be independently confirmed and should be treated as unverified. A notable cross-connection: Peter Cooper is the "Cooper" in the Cooper Hewitt, Smithsonian Design Museum, founded by his granddaughters and, since 1970, housed in the Andrew Carnegie Mansion — linking Chapter 5 to Andrew Carnegie (subject of Chapter 18).

New York World's Fair Corporation

The New York World's Fair Corporation was a non-profit formed in 1935 to organize the 1939–1940 New York World's Fair, built on a former ash dump in Flushing Meadows, Queens. Grover Whalen became its full-time president. After the fair closed, the grounds became Flushing Meadows–Corona Park. Important caveat: the chapter's claim that this corporation hosted a contest Art Smith won for a "first-responders building" could NOT be verified — no source connects the jeweler Art Smith to a World's Fair building-design contest. This detail appears unsupported or conflated and should be treated with low confidence; do not build a location theory on it.

Duke Ellington

Edward Kennedy "Duke" Ellington (1899–1974) was a jazz composer/bandleader born in Washington, D.C. His tie to Chapter 5 runs through Talley Beatty: Beatty's signature 1959 work "The Road of the Phoebe Snow" was set to Ellington/Strayhorn music. The most geographically loaded thread is the "Phoebe Snow" reference: a Delaware, Lackawanna and Western Railroad advertising mascot whose namesake passenger train ran from Hoboken, NJ to Buffalo, NY, crossing the Delaware Water Gap, the Pocono Mountains, and the Susquehanna and Genesee valleys. Ellington himself is memorialized mainly in Washington, D.C. (birthplace site, Duke Ellington Park, the Duke Ellington Bridge over Rock Creek, the Smithsonian's Duke Ellington Collection) and NYC (Duke Ellington Circle at Central Park, Duke Ellington Boulevard).

Martin Luther King, Jr.

MLK (1929–1968) authored the chapter's opening epigraph: "Faith is taking the first step even when you don't see the whole staircase." This exact quote is NOT documented in King's own writings; its earliest published source is 1986, and the image derives from 2 Corinthians 5:7. King's most concrete US-place associations come from his memorialized sites and from the named places in his "I Have a Dream" speech, whose "Let Freedom Ring" passage explicitly enumerates mountains: New Hampshire hilltops; New York mountains; Pennsylvania's Alleghenies; Colorado's Rockies; California's slopes; Stone Mountain of Georgia; Lookout Mountain of Tennessee; and Mississippi's hills. Several are real, accessible public-land features. The MLK Memorial in Washington, D.C. is itself a literal mountain metaphor (the "Stone of Hope" emerging from the "Mountain of Despair").
