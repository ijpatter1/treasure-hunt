## Chapter Summary (Factual, 2-3 paragraphs)

Chapter 18 features Andrew Carnegie's Colombian emerald (from the Muzo Mine, Boyaca Department), which was part of Carnegie's rare mineral collection. The author opens by addressing his editor's question about why he would give away such extravagant items -- whether there is a "punchline" to the treasure hunt. He explains his motivation through his childhood and Carnegie's philanthropic philosophy.

The author reveals that his parents met in seminary and started a church in Huntersville, NC, "built upon the ideal of service." The church assisted the local homeless shelter, soup kitchen, battered women's shelter, and Habitat for Humanity chapter. His father sat on the board of the local Food Bank and Red Cross. The author was "required to volunteer from a young age." Carnegie's biography follows: born in Dunfermline, Scotland, he immigrated to Pittsburgh at age 13 in 1848, starting as a bobbin boy (16 hours/day, 6 days/week). He rose through Western Union and Pennsylvania Railroad (mentor Thomas A. Scott), wrote the "Gospel of Wealth" (1889), sold his steel companies to J.P. Morgan for $480 million in 1901 (equivalent to $310 billion today), and devoted the rest of his life to philanthropy.

Carnegie pioneered the charitable endowment (first of its kind), funded almost 1,700 libraries (800+ still operating), and gave away $350 million. His endowments funded the discovery of insulin, Sesame Street production, and Pell Grants. The author notes that Carnegie also funded the Mount Wilson Observatory, which is "not far from my home, just to the northeast" -- confirming the author lives in the Los Angeles area. The chapter closes with the author's wish that finders "share their joy with others" and a transition to space exploration themes.
