## Chapter Summary (Factual, 2-3 paragraphs)

Chapter 17 opens with the importance of sharing personal stories and documenting one's life, then debunks the cherry tree myth about George Washington. Mason Locke Weems invented the story in the fifth edition of his Washington biography, written one year after Washington's death, to sell more books. 183 years later, the myth was presented as fact in the author's fourth-grade textbook. The author contrasts this with Washington's own extensive documentation -- "tens of thousands of pages of journals and letters, fastidiously ordered and filed away."

The chapter covers Washington's remarkable military career: never formally trained as a soldier, he commanded a "ragtag" army of farmers' sons. The famous Delaware River crossing on Christmas night succeeded only because Washington's boat was the sole group of three to make it across (the water froze for the other two). Germans dismissed spy warnings as "sheer madness," and Washington achieved an improbable victory. For eight years of war, Washington was never hit by a bullet despite standing conspicuously in the line of fire. He rallied retreating troops at the Battle of Monmouth (June 1778) while riding his white steed. He became the unanimous choice for first President.

The treasure item is Washington's crystal jelly glass with an English rose pattern and the Washington family green wax seal on its bottom, used during his presidency for dinners and entertaining in Philadelphia. After his two terms, he brought it to Mount Vernon, Virginia. It is one of only two known to exist (sister glass at Mount Vernon Museum), displayed in more than ten museums. The author encourages treasure hunters to "share your stories with one another" and "document your adventures."

**CRITICAL FINDING: 317 slaves.** Washington's ownership of 317 slaves (p. 154) produces the number 317, which is a marker on the Hollow Rock Trail. This is genuinely notable. However, the number is a well-documented historical fact -- Washington's slave holdings are extensively recorded. The author could not have altered this number. The question is whether the author *chose to include this specific fact* because the number 317 resonated with the trail marker system, or whether he included it because it is one of the most significant facts about Washington's moral complexity. The latter seems far more likely given the chapter's theme of honest storytelling.

**Surveying language test:** Washington was professionally a land surveyor before becoming a military commander. The chapter does NOT mention his surveying career. This is a notable *absence* -- if the author wanted to embed geometric/measurement language through Washington, the surveying career was an obvious vehicle. Its omission suggests the author was not using Chapter 17 as a geometric clue carrier.

## Confirmation Bias Audit

1. The author has cited Washington's slave count in interviews without any connection to trail markers
2. The number 317 appears in all standard Washington biographies (it does -- this is well-documented)

More broadly, the chapter's HRT connections would be disproven if the treasure is found at any non-HRT location, since the connections here are primarily thematic (documentation, perseverance) with one numerical coincidence.
