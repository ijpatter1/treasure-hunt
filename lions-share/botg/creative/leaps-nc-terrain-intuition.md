# Creative Leaps — Lens: NC Terrain / Sensory "Spidey-Sense" Intuition

*Track: North Carolina. Pass: creative / intuitive / speculative (faith + abduction, not falsification). Method: map the FEEL of the poem (water, shady oaks, a sit-rock, a view opening onto lower country, blue+pink bloom, dark sky, "the haiku curls a little further on") to specific western-NC corridor landforms by intuition, not GIS filter. Everything here is a HYPOTHESIS TO TEST. No ciphers used — only association, sensory resonance, and author-psychology.*

*Corridor of interest carried (not caged): lat ~35.8–36.0 N, lon ~81–82.2 W — the Piedmont→Blue-Ridge escarpment seam from Hiddenite/Alexander Co. up toward the Mitchell/Avery escarpment. JCB writes like a songwriter (motif, refrain, emotional through-line) and is romantic about place; the hide should RHYME with the book's heart. He hides meaning in what he loves.*

**Leap 1 — The poem's whole geometry IS the escarpment "lip," and the feel resolves to the Brushy Mountains rim above Hiddenite.**
- **The creative read:** Read as pure sensory choreography, the poem walks you UP through shade and water, then the "view opens onto lower country." That single gesture — turning around at the top of a climb and having the flat land fall away beneath you — is the defining bodily feeling of the Brushy Mountain escarpment, an isolated monadnock range that rises abruptly out of the Piedmont. You don't get "lower country opening" on a plateau or deep in the Blue Ridge interior (you're surrounded by higher peaks there). You get it precisely on a FRONT range standing alone — the Brushies are exactly that, and they sit directly over Hiddenite.
- **Points to:** Brushy Mountains rim / Rocky Face Mountain & the ridgeline E–W of it, Alexander Co. NC — specifically a south- or southeast-facing overlook where the Piedmont spreads below (lat ~35.93 N, lon ~81.2 W).
- **Why it coheres:** The poem's emotional arc (effort → arrival → the world opening up) is the same arc as JCB's redemption story; he'd pick a landform that physically performs that arc. An isolated front range gives the strongest "lower country opening" sensation in the whole corridor. It also keeps the strongest single name-anchor (Hiddenite, in a "There's Treasure Inside" book) right under your feet.
- **Resonates with:** Hiddenite/Alexander anchor (Ch4 "Blaze the Path"); Ch21 "Antiquities of Alexander" ↔ Alexander Co. wink; the convergence engine's NC #1 ranking; Rocky Face not-eliminated note.

**Leap 2 — "The haiku curls a little further on" = follow flowing water just past the obvious overlook; the hide is at a small creek's bend, not the summit.**
- **The creative read:** "Curls a little further on" is a river/creek instruction dressed as a literary one. A haiku is short, three turns; a curling thing that goes "further on" is a meander. Treasure hunters rush the summit/overlook; the poem gently says keep going — the spot is one bend past where you'd stop, where water curls. Sensory-wise the poem keeps water present (you HEAR it before the view), so the sit-rock is streamside, in oak shade, just downstream of the viewpoint.
- **Points to:** A creek bend in oak shade below an overlook — strong candidates in-corridor: the headwater branches of Lower Little River / Rocky Creek draining the Brushies (Alexander Co.), or the laurel-shaded forks above Hiddenite. Look one meander past the first overlook on the trail.
- **Why it coheres:** JCB the songwriter loves the soft redirect ("not the obvious peak — the quiet bend"). It satisfies "near a road / single-day walkable / not dangerous" because creek bends low on a front range are reachable. It honors his hide-in-plain-sight-but-overlooked instinct: everyone stops at the view; he put it one curl further.
- **Resonates with:** Leap 1 (same massif); Ch3 "Joy Is in the Details" (the detail is the extra bend); the water motif throughout the poem; "not buried, not dangerous" constraints.

**Leap 5 — "Shady oaks" specifically = an OAK-dominant dry ridge with long views, i.e., a chestnut-oak/scarlet-oak ridgecrest, not a cove — placing the sit-rock on an open rocky ledge with the lower country in view.**
- **The creative read:** The poem says OAKS, not "forest." Oak-dominant stands in this corridor sit on dry, rocky ridgecrests and south slopes (chestnut oak, scarlet oak love thin rocky soil). So "shady oaks + sit-rock + view opening" all co-occur naturally on an oak-clad rocky ridgecrest. The water is the creek you crossed on the way up; the view is from the dry crest.
- **Points to:** An oak-and-outcrop ridgecrest with a south/southeast overlook — Rocky Face Mountain ridgeline (Alexander) is a textbook example (bare rock faces + oak woods + Piedmont views); also the oak-pine outcrops along the Brushy crest toward Pores Knob.
- **Why it coheres:** It reconciles "shade" and "open view" without contradiction (the apparent tension is resolved by oak-ridge ecology). Rock outcrops give a natural, non-buried, carry-to nook for a 25 lb box at the base of/under a ledge — fitting "not buried, near road, not dangerous." Rocky Face literally NAMES the rock face.
- **Resonates with:** Rocky Face not-eliminated note; Leap 1 (same massif, different micro-read — convergence on the Brushy crest); Ch4 "Blaze the Path" (a ridgecrest IS a blazed path).
