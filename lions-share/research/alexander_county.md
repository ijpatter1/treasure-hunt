# Public land in Alexander County that fits a treasure hunt

**Rocky Face Mountain Recreation Area is overwhelmingly the only viable candidate in Alexander County for a hidden treasure location.** The county contains no state parks, no national forests, no game lands, and no federal land — just a handful of county-managed parks, most of which are ball fields and playgrounds with no meaningful natural terrain. Only three properties have hiking trails at all, and only Rocky Face offers the combination of exposed rock formations, remote-feeling terrain, and geological richness that a treasure hider would seek. The area's deep personal connection to Jon Collins-Black — who grew up 30 minutes away in Statesville and spent childhood hours at Emerald Hollow Mine in Hiddenite — strengthens the case considerably.

---

## Rocky Face Mountain dominates Alexander County's public land

Alexander County is a rural foothills county of roughly 36,400 people with remarkably little public land. The county operates seven parks, but six of them are essentially athletic facilities — ball fields, basketball courts, playgrounds, and walking tracks on flat ground. The Town of Taylorsville maintains one small municipal park (Matheson Park, 7 acres). The NC Wildlife Resources Commission manages two boat ramps and a fishing pier on Lake Hickory. That's it. There are **no state parks, state forests, NCWRC game lands, national forest parcels, or Nature Conservancy preserves** anywhere in the county.

**Rocky Face Mountain Recreation Area** stands alone as the county's significant natural public land: **318 acres** of granitic dome terrain in the Brushy Mountains foothills, with **5–6 miles of hiking trails**, sheer quarry cliffs, exposed granite expanses, rare plant communities, and panoramic views. Opened in May 2012, it draws roughly 200,000 visitors annually and was placed on the NC Registry of Natural Heritage Areas in 2003.

Three conservation properties exist in the county — a 120-acre Foothills Conservancy easement, a ~100-acre joint Foothills/Blue Ridge Conservancy parcel in the eastern Brushy Mountains, and the newly acquired 101-acre Boone Gap Forest — but **none have confirmed public access**, making them ineligible under the treasure hunt's "public land" requirement.

## The Hollow Rock Trail and its namesake formation

The Hollow Rock Trail is Rocky Face Mountain's signature hike: a **2.5-mile round-trip lollipop loop** blazed in red, rated moderate to strenuous, with roughly **500–688 feet of elevation gain**. It climbs from the quarry base via the Buzzard Loop connector, traverses large expanses of exposed granite along the ridge, and makes a summit loop around Rocky Face's **1,800-foot peak** with panoramic views reaching Grandfather Mountain, Table Rock, and the Black Mountains.

The trail's name almost certainly derives from **a specific hollow rock formation** rather than a general terrain description. The park designates two primitive camping sites: the "Grindstone Camping Site" (fires allowed) and the **"Hollow Rock Camping Site" (no fires allowed)**. The naming convention parallels the Grindstone Trail — named for a geological feature — suggesting a distinct hollow or cave-like formation near the campsite. Rocky Face's granite gneiss undergoes **exfoliation**, a process where massive rock breaks off in sheets parallel to the surface, creating detached slabs, overhangs, and hollow spaces. At least one **cave-like formation** has been documented in the park's natural play area. Multiple hikers describe side paths leading to additional rock faces and overlooks along the trail. The specific "Hollow Rock" is likely a rock shelter, overhang, or hollow created by this exfoliation process, located at or near the designated camping site along the ridge.

The park also features five other named trails: the **Buzzard Loop** (0.8 mi, purple blaze, easy-moderate), **Grindstone Trail** (1.0 mi, green blaze, moderate, includes creek crossing), **Prison Camp Trail** (1.0 mi, orange blaze, passes foundations of former inmate housing from the 1920s quarry era), **Vertical Mile Challenge Trail** (2.2-mi loop, blue blaze, strenuous climb up a 30° exposed granite slab), and a **0.3-mile paved ADA walking track** with wetland boardwalks at the base.

## Geological and ecological features that match the poem clues

Rocky Face Mountain's natural features align remarkably well with several of the suspected clue elements. The mountain is a **Low Elevation Granitic Dome** — an extremely rare community type in North Carolina — with granite gneiss bedrock shaped by exfoliation into smooth domes, flat rock expanses, and detached slab features where one could certainly "sit upon a rock."

**Yucca plants are explicitly documented as "abundant in the wooded areas"** of Rocky Face, per the NC Natural Heritage Program's documentation. These aren't incidental — they support the **Cofaqui giant skipper** (*Megathymus cofaqui*), a rare butterfly species listed as NC Significantly Rare. A second rare butterfly, the **giant swallowtail** (*Papilio cresphontes*), feeds on hop-tree at the site. The presence of these butterflies — potential "dancers" — alongside abundant yucca on public land is a distinctive combination.

The "pike" reference finds a plausible match in the **chain pickerel** (*Esox niger*), a member of the pike family native to both the Catawba and Yadkin River basins that border and originate in Alexander County. Chain pickerel are documented in the Yadkin system adjacent to Alexander County. Alternatively, "pike" could reference the spiky yucca leaves or prickly pear cactus (also native to Rocky Face).

The forest communities include **oaks, pines (Virginia pine colonizes granite outcrops), and red cedar** in a documented Montane Red Cedar-Hardwood Woodland community. Water features include **Beaverdam Creek** along the northern boundary, **Wallace Creek** on the western edge, **Rocky Face Branch** with tributaries near the quarry, and a creek crossing on the Grindstone Trail. The quarry depression holds standing water and supports restored wetlands with boardwalks.

## Jon Collins-Black's personal connection to this exact area

The biographical link between the treasure hunt's creator and Hiddenite is direct and documented. **Collins-Black grew up in Statesville, NC** (Iredell County, immediately adjacent to Alexander County, roughly 30 minutes from Rocky Face Mountain). In interviews with The Guardian and CNBC, he described childhood hours spent at **Emerald Hollow Mine in Hiddenite** — the only mine in the world where the public can dig for emeralds in their natural setting. He told The Guardian: "It was the hours spent at Emerald Hollow Mine, a short drive away, that left the deepest impression. There, he sifted through creeks and dirt in search of hidden gems." He also stated: "I am from North Carolina. So it would make sense if there's a treasure in the South."

One critical caveat: the book's rules state **no more than one box per state**. The Pokémon box is connected to a family road trip originating in Statesville, NC, though most hunters place that box in Arkansas. If the Pokémon box is indeed in Arkansas, North Carolina remains open for another box. The Lion's Share box clues are distributed throughout the entire book, and as of March 2026, **no public theory specifically naming Rocky Face Mountain or Hiddenite has been found** on treasure hunting forums — though substantive discussion may exist in private Discord channels.

## Conclusion: Rocky Face stands alone in Alexander County

The search for alternative treasure-hiding locations in Alexander County effectively ends where it begins. **Rocky Face Mountain Recreation Area is the only public land in the county with hiking trails through natural terrain, exposed rock formations, and the geological richness** that both the treasure hunt rules and poem clues seem to require. No other property comes close. The two lakefront parks with short nature trails (Dusty Ridge and Wittenburg Access) lack rock features entirely. Every other park is a maintained athletic facility.

If the treasure is in Alexander County, the operative question is not *which* public land — it's *where on Rocky Face Mountain*. The Hollow Rock Trail, with its namesake formation, exposed granite expanses, abundant yucca, rare butterflies, and multiple overlook points where a box could be concealed among exfoliation features, remains the primary search zone. The Grindstone Trail's creek crossing and south-facing rock views, and the Prison Camp Trail's historical ruins, also warrant attention. The entire park sits directly off Rocky Face Church Road — well within the three-mile road proximity rule — and every trail qualifies as a "leisurely afternoon outing" for someone of average fitness.
