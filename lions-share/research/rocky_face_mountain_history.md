# Rocky Face Mountain: 12,000 years of history on a rare granite dome

**Rocky Face Mountain is one of North Carolina's rarest geological formations — a Low Elevation Granitic Dome rising 600 feet above Alexander County's foothills — whose story spans from ancient magma chambers to a Paleo-Indian presence 12,000 years ago, through a brutal convict-labor quarry era, decades of neglect, and a dramatic modern resurrection as a 318-acre park drawing 200,000 visitors annually.** The mountain sits at the southern edge of the Brushy Mountains near Hiddenite, NC, a community world-famous for its emerald deposits and the discovery of the mineral hiddenite in 1879. What was once an illegal dump site earned Alexander County a National Association of Counties Achievement Award in 2015, just three years after opening. The site harbors globally imperiled plant species found nowhere else on Earth and preserves the crumbling foundations of 1920s prison camp barracks along trails that now carry hikers across sweeping granite vistas.

## A dome of ancient magma breaks through the earth

Rocky Face Mountain formed as a pool of magma deep beneath the Earth's surface within the Inner Piedmont geological province of the southern Appalachians. Over hundreds of millions of years, softer overlying terrain eroded away to expose the metamorphosed **granite gneiss** dome — a type of geological feature called a **bornhardt** or exfoliation dome. The rock dates to the broader Siluro-Devonian period of Inner Piedmont formation; nearby Walker Top Granite in the Brushy Mountains has been dated to **407 ± 1 million years ago** (Merschat et al., 2023, *Geosphere*). The exfoliation process — where massive granite, originally formed under intense underground pressure, fractures and peels in concentric layers once overlying rock erodes and pressure is released — produced the mountain's characteristic smooth, rounded surfaces and dramatic exposed slabs.

The dome reaches a **maximum elevation of approximately 1,800 feet above sea level**, rising roughly 600 feet above the surrounding countryside. Its rock is classified as granite gneiss, with documented minerals including members of the **Feldspar Group** per the NC Geological Survey's Mineral Commodity Files. The soil classification around the mountain falls under the **Cleveland-Ashe-Rock Outcrop complex** — a combination found in Alexander County only at Rocky Face and nearby Round Top.

A 2002 survey by the NC Natural Heritage Program identified **three rare natural community types** on the mountain. The most significant is the **Low Elevation Granitic Dome** — one of the rarest community classifications in North Carolina's natural community system. These domes, occurring below approximately 3,000 feet, are distinguished by a near-absence of crevices and deep soil pockets, forcing vegetation into thin mats zoned by soil depth: bare rock supporting only lichens and mosses, thin soil mats dominated by spikemoss (*Selaginella* spp.), and slightly deeper soils supporting grasses, herbs, and eventually shrubs. The mountain also contains **Montane Red Cedar-Hardwood Woodland** along outcrop margins and **Piedmont Monadnock Forest**, an uncommon type associated with isolated mountain outliers. Other notable Low Elevation Granitic Domes in North Carolina include Stone Mountain State Park and sites at Crowders Mountain and Hanging Rock.

Multiple geological studies have examined the Brushy Mountains context. A 2003 University of Tennessee master's thesis (Kalbas) documented six deformation events in the southwestern Brushy Mountains, and a 2012 USGS field trip guide described a geo-traverse through the migmatitic Inner Piedmont from the Brushy Mountains to Lincolnton. The Brushy Mountains lie within the region affected by the **Brindle Creek fault**, a major crustal-scale boundary between the western and eastern Inner Piedmont belts.

## Catawba homeland and 12,000 years of human presence

Rocky Face Mountain sits squarely within the historically documented territory of the **Catawba people** (Iswa, "people of the river"), a Siouan-speaking confederation that occupied the Catawba River Valley for at least 6,000 years. The Catawba River forms Alexander County's southern border. At European contact in the mid-1500s, Catawba population was estimated at **15,000–25,000**. They were sedentary agriculturists noted for the oldest surviving pottery tradition east of the Mississippi. The Cherokee occupied territory to the west in the Appalachian mountains, making Alexander County a transitional zone between the two nations.

Direct archaeological evidence of ancient human presence in the Brushy Mountains comes from a **very rare clear quartz crystal fluted point** found by Blake Deal in the Brushy Mountain community of Vashti, in northern Alexander County. Published in the *Central States Archaeological Journal*, this Paleo-Indian artifact dates to approximately **10,000 BC or earlier**, demonstrating human presence in Alexander County's Brushy Mountains stretching back at least 12,000 years. Farmers throughout Alexander County have regularly discovered arrowheads and green stones while plowing fields, and local collectors like Ron Harris of the Piedmont Archaeology Society have assembled extensive artifact collections from the area.

No specific indigenous name for Rocky Face Mountain has been documented, and no petroglyphs have been recorded on the site. However, given the Paleo-Indian artifacts from the immediate Brushy Mountain area, the mountain's location within Catawba territory, and the pervasive archaeological record throughout the county, indigenous peoples almost certainly knew and used the mountain for millennia before European arrival. The nearby Berry Site in Burke County has been identified as the town of **Joara**, visited by Hernando de Soto in 1540 and Juan Pardo in 1567, placing the broader region at the frontier of the earliest European-indigenous contact in North Carolina.

## Hiddenite, emeralds, and the making of a gem-hunting capital

Alexander County was established in **1847** from portions of Iredell, Wilkes, and Caldwell Counties, named for William Julius Alexander, Speaker of the North Carolina House of Commons. The county seat, Taylorsville, held its first lot sale on August 10–11, 1847. Early settlers were primarily of **Scots-Irish and German descent**, farming the rolling Piedmont foothills.

The community near Rocky Face Mountain was originally called **White Plains**, noted as a health resort for its sulfur springs. Its transformation began around **1874**, when farmers plowing fields near the area stumbled upon green stones. Around 1879, a boy named **George Warren**, plowing his father's field, found greenish-colored rocks. J.A.D. Stephenson, an Alexander County native and mineral collector, posted rewards for interesting specimens and eventually brought the finds to the attention of **William Earl Hidden** (1853–1918), a Rhode Island-born mineralogist whom **Thomas Edison** had dispatched to North Carolina to find platinum for light bulb filaments.

Hidden sent samples to chemist J. Lawrence Smith in Louisville, who identified them as a **new variety of spodumene** — a chromium-colored green gem — and named the mineral **"hiddenite"** in Hidden's honor in 1881. Today hiddenite is considered one of the **four rarest gemstones in the world**. The community renamed itself Hiddenite (formally incorporated under that name in 1913, though the charter was repealed in 1919). Its post office was established February 10, 1888.

The **Emerald and Hiddenite Mining Company** was organized in 1881, employing roughly **100 workers** between 1881 and 1885. The district's emerald production has been extraordinary: a **1,276-carat emerald** discovered in 1882 was the largest in North America at the time (later stolen from the American Museum of Natural History in 1950 and never recovered); a **1,438-carat "Stephenson Emerald"** surfaced in 1969; and a massive **1,869-carat emerald** in 2003 set the current North American record. North Carolina designated the emerald as its **official state precious stone in 1973**. An estimated **60,000 carats of emeralds and 20,000 carats of hiddenite** have been produced from the district between 1875 and 2007.

Adding color to this gem-rich history is the story of **James Paul "Diamond Jim" Lucas**, an international diamond merchant who purchased a home in Hiddenite around 1908 and expanded it into a 22-room mansion filled with exotic collections — large diamonds, antique guns, Buffalo Bill's clothes, a pipe from Czar Nicholas of Russia, and a WWI helmet from General Pershing. Listed on the **National Register of Historic Places in 1982**, the mansion now houses the **Hiddenite Arts & Heritage Center**.

## Convict labor, sledgehammers, and the quarry that scarred a mountain

The quarry at Rocky Face Mountain **began operations in 1922** and continued until the **early 1940s** — roughly two decades of extraction that fundamentally altered the mountain's south face. The operation was not a private commercial venture but a **state and county convict labor operation**, consistent with North Carolina's extensive system of using prison labor for road construction. North Carolina became known as the "Good Roads State" through this system, which could build roads for as little as $800 per mile while guarding and feeding prisoners for just **21 cents per day**.

**Prisoners from a jail on Highway 16 south of Taylorsville** were transported to the quarry at Rocky Face, according to David Odom, Taylorsville's Town Manager. There, inmates broke granite gneiss with **sledgehammers** by hand, loaded the crushed gravel and rocks into railroad cars on a **spur track running approximately eight miles to Hiddenite**, where the stone was crushed into gravel for road construction. A historical photograph survives showing the train engine used to haul granite on this spur, and remnants of the railroad line remain visible in places along the route to Rocky Face.

A **prison camp was established on the east side of the mountain**, where inmate housing was constructed. The **foundations of these structures are still visible today** along what is now called the Prison Camp Trail. No formal archaeological survey of the prison camp site has been conducted, as noted in the 2009 Master Plan. At a 2009 public meeting about the park's future, one attendee mentioned a "grandfather guard at prison," indicating that local families retain personal memories of the operation. A secondary, smaller quarry on the southwestern side was abandoned in the 1920s, suggesting there may have been an earlier or separate operation.

The quarrying created a **sheer cliff face approximately 100 feet in height** on the south face and a large flat depression at the quarry base — the only relatively level area on the mountain. No specific operator name, inmate counts, or production tonnage figures have been located in available records. The NC prison system was not separated from the State Highway and Public Works Commission until 1957, and detailed records for individual quarry camps from this era are sparse.

After quarrying ceased in the early 1940s, the mountain was **abandoned and became an illegal dump site and magnet for graffiti**. Off-road vehicles tore across the terrain, and the property deteriorated for decades. The first documented recreational use of the quarry face came in **1975**, when U.S. Army Reservists from the 3rd Brigade, 518th Division (Hickory) used it for rappelling training. In **1982**, the **Felburn Foundation** purchased the mountain, but the organization lacked the resources to develop or protect it, and the property remained largely neglected for another two decades.

## From illegal dump to award-winning park in a decade

The transformation of Rocky Face Mountain from abandoned quarry to premier county park is a remarkable story of conservation, community organizing, and strategic grant-writing. The catalyst came in **2002**, when the **Carolina Land and Lakes Resource Conservation and Development District (CLLRC&D)** made Rocky Face a focus, and the **NC Natural Heritage Program** conducted its landmark biological survey identifying three rare natural community types.

In **2003**, the Felburn Foundation transferred the approximately **328-acre tract** to the CLLRC&D, which instituted cleanup efforts and partnered with the Natural Heritage Program to place the mountain on the **NC Registry of Natural Heritage Areas**. That same year, **Kimberly-Horn and Associates** was selected to develop an initial master plan — an ambitious $1.79 million proposal centered on environmental education with a 6,000-square-foot Visitor Center. This plan was ultimately deemed too costly.

In **March 2009**, Alexander County contracted with the **Western Piedmont Council of Governments (WPCOG)** to prepare a revised, more realistic master plan. Environmental Administrator **Samuel W. Erwin** led the effort. A **14-member Rocky Face Park Advisory Committee** — including future Park Director **Alisha Hayes** (later Stamey), Rev. Mark Dula, and community members — began meeting in April 2009. A public meeting at Rocky Face Baptist Church on **July 9, 2009** drew over 30 citizens who expressed a desire for the park to remain a low-impact, educational recreation area faithful to the Felburn Foundation's original principles.

The September 2009 Master Plan proposed three phases of development over ten years, with parking, shelters, and buildings in lower-lying areas and mountaintop activities limited to hiking trails and educational signage. The plan acknowledged the site's constraints from both the Felburn Foundation deed restrictions and the Natural Heritage Program agreement.

In **2010**, Alexander County was awarded a **$477,000 Phase 1 grant** from the NC Parks and Recreation Trust Fund (PARTF). The CLLRC&D donated the land to the county in **2011**. **McGill Associates**, a regional engineering firm, performed design, engineering, and construction administration for the park using LEED-applicable sustainable design principles. Their director of land planning, **Mike Norris**, led the project.

**Rocky Face Mountain Recreational Area officially opened on May 25, 2012.** Phase 1 included a paved ADA walking track, picnic shelter, restrooms, park office, and hiking trails. Phase 2 added a community building, natural playground with slide and zipline, outdoor amphitheater, outdoor classroom, two wildlife observation decks, wetland boardwalks, and educational signage along trails. Total PARTF funding across both phases reached **$702,882**.

The park received the **2015 NACo Achievement Award** in the Parks and Recreation category — one of only five such awards in North Carolina that year. Commission Chairman **Larry Yoder** noted: "A lot of hard work went into the planning and development of Rocky Face Park as the County stepped up and transformed this property into one of the best parks in North Carolina." By that time, the park had logged over **41,000 visits** in three years. Today it draws approximately **200,000 visitors per year** and features six named trails totaling roughly six miles, **58–60 established rock climbing routes** on the quarry face, primitive backcountry camping, and annual events including **RockyFest** (since 2013, drawing ~2,000 attendees) and the **Vertical Mile Challenge** — a trail race requiring eight laps for a cumulative vertical mile of ascent, with over 4,000 participants in its club as of 2018.

## Globally imperiled species and a century of botanical discovery

Rocky Face Mountain's ecological significance extends far beyond its geological rarity. The site has attracted botanists for nearly a century, and its documented species include organisms found **nowhere else on Earth**.

The most globally significant species is **Keever's Onion (*Allium keeverae*)**, ranked **G2 (globally imperiled)** — endemic exclusively to the Brushy Mountains of Alexander and Wilkes Counties, growing in calcitic seepages on granite domes. Formerly considered a variety of *Allium cuthbertii*, it was recognized as a separate species by Poindexter, Weakley, and Williams in 2017. The UNC-Chapel Hill Botanical Garden calls it "the Piedmont's most beautiful species of *Allium*," blooming with light pink-white flowers in May.

Equally remarkable is **Keever's bristle moss (*Orthotrichum keeverae*)**, a state-endangered species whose **holotype was collected at Rocky Face Mountain in 1948** by Catherine Keever and Lewis Anderson. Most of the world's population of this diminutive moss occurs in the Brushy Mountains.

The mountain's botanical documentation stretches to **1933**, when Rev. Charles E. Raynal collected Elf Orpine (*Diamorpha smallii*) specimens. In **May 1938**, UNC-Chapel Hill botanist **Dr. Henry Roland Totten** joined Raynal and S. Wallace Hoffmann for a botanical expedition that collected 20 specimens. In 2020, UNC Herbarium Curator **Carol Ann McCormick** published a detailed account documenting current populations of *Allium keeverae*, Appalachian Sandwort, Michaux's Saxifrage, and Elf Orpine growing on the granite slopes. Other notable plants include prickly pear cactus, Table Mountain pine, shinyleaf meadow-sweet, and — historically — yellow and purple pitcher plants collected nearby in the late 1930s. Yucca populations are described as "abundant in the wooded areas."

Two rare butterfly species inhabit the mountain: the **Cofaqui giant skipper (*Megathymus cofaqui*)**, which feeds on the abundant yucca, and the **eastern giant swallowtail (*Papilio cresphontes*)**, which feeds on hop-tree. Both appear on the NC Natural Heritage Program's list of significant animal species.

Regarding **Schweinitz's sunflower (*Helianthus schweinitzii*)**, which is sometimes associated with the site: the subagent research found **no specific documentation** of this federally endangered species at Rocky Face Mountain. Schweinitz's sunflower is endemic to the Piedmont of North Carolina and South Carolina, primarily within 100 km of Charlotte, and prefers clayey soils — habitat different from Rocky Face's granite dome. It was not listed among the species found in the 2002 Natural Heritage survey.

## What's in a name: Rocky Face and Hollow Rock

The name **"Rocky Face Mountain"** is descriptively literal. As the NC Historic Hundred guide notes: "There's little reason to question how Rocky Face got its name. One look at the craggy outcropping of gray stone 100 feet in height will convince visitors that the mountain does indeed have a rocky face." The prominent exposed granite dome, even before quarrying expanded the cliff face, would have presented a striking "rocky face" visible from the surrounding lowlands. **Rocky Face Baptist Church**, located near the current park entrance on Rocky Face Church Road, predates the park by many decades, confirming the name's deep roots in local usage — likely dating to the early settlement of Alexander County in the mid-1800s. The mountain appears on the **Hiddenite Quadrangle 7.5' USGS topographic map**.

**Hollow Rock Trail** — the 2.5-mile signature summit loop marked in red — is named for a rock formation at the top of the mountain. Specific documentation describing the exact formation is sparse in available sources, but the trail crosses large expanses of exposed granite where weathering and exfoliation create natural hollows, overhangs, and cavities in the rock. **Primitive backcountry camping** is available along the trail system near the summit for $13 per night, requiring reservations — this appears to be the "Hollow Rock Camping Site" referenced in some descriptions.

The **Prison Camp Trail** (orange, 1 mile) takes its name directly from the 1920s–1940s prison camp whose foundations still stand along its route. **Grindstone Trail** (green, 1 mile) likely references the grinding of stone during the quarry era. The **Vertical Mile Challenge Trail** (blue, 2.2-mile loop) and **Buzzard Loop** (purple, 0.8 mile) are modern names given during park development.

## Gems, legends, and a treasure hunter's childhood

No specific ghost stories, supernatural legends, or ancient folklore traditions have been documented for Rocky Face Mountain itself. The mountain's cultural narrative centers on its quarry-and-prison history and its modern rebirth. However, the broader **Brushy Mountains** hold several folklore traditions: the **"Mysterious Varmint"** of the mid-1940s — a terrifying beast reported in the Brocktown and Pores Knob communities that killed hounds and chased hunters, written up in *Outdoor Life* magazine; phantom wagons "weighted down by something unseen" on Lithia Springs Road; and mysterious lights in "Coone Hollow." The celebrated **Brown Mountain Lights** flicker in nearby Burke and Caldwell Counties.

A beloved local legend surrounds the **"June Bug Railroad"**: State Senator Romulus Z. Linney reportedly told the NC Legislature in 1887 that "a well-wintered June Bug can carry away $1,000 worth of this valuable gem" (hiddenite) tied to its hind leg, arguing for a railroad charter. The Alexander Railroad's locomotives have been painted green and gold — and nicknamed "The June Bug" — ever since.

The gem-hunting culture of Hiddenite runs deep. The **Emerald Hollow Mine**, opened to the public in 1986 by geologist Mike Watkins, is the **only emerald mine in the world open to public prospecting**, offering over 60 types of naturally occurring gems and minerals. It has been featured on the Travel Channel, National Geographic, and Discovery Channel. The stolen 1882 emerald from the American Museum of Natural History — 1,276 carats, taken in 1950, never recovered — represents a genuine lost-treasure legend from this district.

This gem-hunting tradition directly shaped **Jon Collins-Black**, author of *There's Treasure Inside* (released November 12, 2024). Collins-Black grew up in **Statesville, NC** (neighboring Iredell County), and when he was 12, his family moved into a log cabin his father built on 20 acres in **northern Iredell County** — very close to the Alexander County line and Hiddenite. As he told CNBC: "As a kid growing up in 'the boondocks' of North Carolina, [I] was fond of digging in the dirt to look for rocks and arrowheads. With an active emerald mine not too far from where he lived, Collins-Black was drawn to the idea of finding something of value hidden away, just out of sight." Multiple sources confirm he spent formative childhood hours at **Emerald Hollow Mine**, sifting through creeks and dirt for hidden gems.

Collins-Black, a 1991 Statesville High School graduate who attended UNC-Chapel Hill and later became an early Bitcoin investor (purchasing at $125 per coin in 2013), spent nearly five years and over **$2 million** acquiring treasures and hiding **five puzzle boxes** across the United States. The largest, called **"The Lion's Share,"** is valued at over $1 million. Contents include gold bars, rare Pokémon cards, a Michael Jordan rookie card, Jackie Onassis's diamond sapphire brooch, a 1996 Olympic gold medal, a Picasso gold pendant, items from the recovered Forrest Fenn treasure, a moon rock, and shipwreck doubloons. **No direct, confirmed connection between Collins-Black and Rocky Face Mountain specifically** has been documented, but his childhood in northern Iredell County places him within easy reach of both Emerald Hollow Mine and Rocky Face Mountain. Some treasure searchers have speculated that North Carolina may be the location of one of the five hidden boxes.

## Conclusion: A mountain that embodies reinvention

Rocky Face Mountain's story is one of deep time and human resilience. Its granite gneiss crystallized hundreds of millions of years ago, was shaped by exfoliation over geological epochs, witnessed 12,000 years of indigenous presence, endured two decades of convict-labor quarrying that scarred its face and housed prisoners in barracks whose foundations still stand, survived decades as an illegal dump, and emerged as an ecological treasure harboring species found nowhere else on the planet. The **holotype of Keever's bristle moss** and the globally imperiled **Keever's Onion** make Rocky Face not just a recreational asset but a site of irreplaceable scientific value. The transformation from dump site to 200,000-visitor-per-year park in barely a decade — driven by the CLLRC&D, the Felburn Foundation, Alexander County government, and less than $703,000 in state grants — stands as a model for what rural counties can achieve. The mountain now anchors a broader regional identity that stretches from the emerald mines that gave Hiddenite its name to a treasure hunter's childhood memories at Emerald Hollow Mine. Rocky Face earned its name from exposed stone, but its deeper identity comes from the layers of human history written across that stone — Catawba, settler, prisoner, botanist, and hiker alike.
